\subsubsection*{Research Questions}
This thesis is guided by the following primary research question:

\begin{quote}
    How does the availability of multiple concurrent \ac{BNPL} facilities affect the trajectory of consumer financial distress among low-to-middle income South African households, and what platform-level interventions can reduce systemic default cascades?
\end{quote}

This is decomposed into three subsidiary questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Under what conditions does debt stacking become self-reinforcing?
    \item At what threshold of \ac{BNPL} access does the population-level default rate shift non-linearly?
    \item Do ``cool-off period'' interventions meaningfully alter outcomes, or do agents simply defer rather than desist?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Thesis Outline}

\section*{Literature Review}

Four bodies of work inform this thesis. The first asks whether an \ac{ABM} is the right instrument
for the question, and concludes that it is, because household debt distress becomes systemic through
a structure that aggregate models cannot represent. The second collects what is empirically known
about \ac{BNPL}, above all about the concurrent borrowing that motivates the research question. The
third supplies behavioural micro-foundations for the agent decision rules, since the borrowing
patterns at issue sit poorly with rational intertemporal optimisation. The fourth turns to South
Africa, where \ac{BNPL} occupies a regulatory position quite unlike the one it holds in the
jurisdictions that most of the empirical literature describes.

\subsection*{Agent-Based Models of Household Credit}

Systemic credit distress is not the sum of individual distress, and this is the case for modelling
it from the bottom up. Cardaci \cite{cardaci2018inequality} builds a hybrid agent-based
stock-flow-consistent model in which heterogeneous households and banks interact through a credit
network, and finds that the link between inequality, household debt and financial instability runs
through the structure of that network rather than through aggregate leverage. Two of its features
matter for the present design. Households do not consume in isolation: their consumption follows a
behavioural rule based on \emph{expenditure cascades}, so that rising inequality exerts upward
pressure on the consumption norms of lower and middle-income households. That social pressure drives
the debt-financed consumption boom, and the accumulation of non-performing loans that follows
produces a banking crisis endogenously rather than by assumption. Peer influence is therefore not an
optional embellishment in this literature but part of the mechanism that generates the crisis. D'Orazio and Giulioni
\cite{dorazio2017micro} reach a compatible conclusion from a different direction. In their model
households form beliefs about future employment, set desired consumption accordingly, and approach
the bank when consumption outruns income; the bank rations credit proportionally once lending
exceeds a multiple of deposits. Unsustainable debt and financial fragility emerge from those local
rules without being written into them.

Both papers support the methodological premise of this thesis. A representative-agent treatment
cannot express the mechanism under investigation, because that mechanism, a single household holding
obligations to several lenders none of whom can observe the others, exists only on the individual
balance sheet.

Two further models supply procedural precedent. Madeira \cite{madeira2018chile} is the closest
analogue to the present work in both method and setting. Writing at the Central Bank of Chile, he
models households that absorb labour-income shocks, choose among available loan contracts, and
default when they can no longer finance minimum consumption. Two features recommend it here. Its
default condition is a cash-flow insolvency test rather than a leverage threshold, which is the
definition this thesis adopts. More importantly, Madeira validates the model against observed
Chilean default rates, demonstrating that a household-level credit \ac{ABM} in a middle-income
economy can be held to external behavioural targets rather than to internal consistency alone.

Hamill et al. \cite{hamill2023creditcard} model the United Kingdom credit-card market and are
relevant for the lender side. They show that promotional strategies chosen by profit-maximising
lenders can raise borrower indebtedness, and they reproduce the non-monotonic relationship between
income and debt-to-income ratio, with middle-income households carrying the highest
balance-to-income values. Lender conduct is therefore a driver of household distress in its own
right, not merely a constraint on it. Yun and Moon \cite{yun2020housing} contribute the remaining
piece by implementing loan-to-value and \ac{DSTI} constraints as explicit rule-based credit gates
and treating them as policy levers, which establishes that regulatory ratios can be modelled as
first-class parameters whose variation produces non-linear population responses.

This thesis follows all four in treating the balance sheet as the accounting core, and describes its
model using the \ac{ODD} protocol \cite{grimm2006odd, grimm2020odd}. The second update's insistence
that a model state the patterns against which its realism should be judged is adopted directly in
the validation strategy.

\subsection*{Empirical Evidence on Buy Now, Pay Later}

The strongest causal evidence comes from deHaan et al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl}, who track banking
records for 10.6 million United States consumers between 2015 and 2021. Adopting \ac{BNPL} is
followed by a 4.0 percent rise in bank overdraft charges, a 1.1 percent rise in credit-card interest
and a 2.3 percent rise in credit-card late fees. An instrumental-variables specification exploiting
pre-adoption shopping habits at \ac{BNPL} partner retailers produces larger estimates still: 8.9
percent, 2.5 percent and 8.4 percent respectively. The effects arrive quickly and fall hardest on
consumers without credit cards or facing liquidity constraints. Their conclusion is that \ac{BNPL}
facilitates overborrowing, and it is the empirical claim this model is built to interrogate.

Where deHaan et al. establish that \ac{BNPL} damages financial health, the \ac{CFPB}
\cite{cfpb2025bnpl} explains the shape of the exposure. Its analysis of roughly 145 million
applications originated between 2017 and 2022 shows first that \ac{BNPL} is not a marginal product,
with 21 percent of consumers holding a consumer record financing at least one purchase in 2022,
against 17.6 percent in 2021. Second, and more consequentially, concurrent borrowing is normal
rather than exceptional. Across 2021 and 2022, 63 percent of \ac{BNPL} borrowers held simultaneous
loans at some point and 32 percent held them across different firms, while roughly 20 percent
originated more than one loan per month on average.

Those figures are the empirical foundation of the debt-stacking mechanism. Holding several
obligations at once, particularly across providers who cannot see one another, converts what looks
at the level of any single transaction like a small interest-free deferral into an aggregate
servicing burden that no individual lender has assessed.

Two further findings sharpen the picture. Hayashi and Routh \cite{toh2025bnplconstraints} report
that \ac{BNPL} users are more financially constrained than non-users, and that 96 percent of users
who paid late were at least mildly financially constrained, against 86 percent of those who did not.
Distress therefore concentrates in precisely the \ac{LMI} population this thesis models. Ackert et
al. \cite{ackert2025bnpl} add an explanation for the demand side: in an incentivised experiment,
consumers chose \ac{BNPL} over a credit-card loan for the same purchase and expected their social
networks to approve, leading the authors to conclude that a payment scheme carrying social approval
raises indebtedness. Wang \cite{wang2026buynow} surveys developments since.

\subsection*{Behavioural Foundations for the Decision Rules}

Behaviour of this kind resists a rational-optimisation account, so the model draws its decision
rules from behavioural economics instead. Meier and Sprenger \cite{meier2010present} connect
experimentally elicited present bias to credit-card borrowing, which justifies a borrowing trigger
that answers to immediate shortfall rather than to a lifetime budget constraint. Kuchler and Pagel
\cite{Kuchler2021} carry the argument to repayment, showing that present-biased borrowers
systematically fail to execute the paydown they planned. Intention and execution come apart, and the
model should let them.

Repayment needs a further ingredient. Keys and Wang \cite{Keys2019} find that 29 percent of
credit-card accounts regularly pay at or near the contractual minimum, and that at least 22 percent
of near-minimum payers shift their payments when the minimum-payment formula changes, which is the
signature of anchoring. Scheduled amortisation would therefore be the wrong rule. A substantial
minority of borrowers pay the salient amount rather than the amount that retires the debt, and that
is exactly the behaviour by which obligations persist and compound.

Together these results describe a consumer agent that borrows on shortfall, repays by salience
rather than optimisation, and varies across the population in how strongly it does both.

\subsection*{The South African Regulatory Setting}

South Africa differs from those jurisdictions in a way that sharpens the question rather than
weakening it. The \ac{NCA} \cite{sa_nca_2005} obliges registered credit providers to conduct a
mandatory affordability assessment, prescribed in Regulation 23A of the Affordability Assessment
Regulations \cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015}. That regulation does not prescribe a \ac{DSTI}
ceiling. It prescribes a residual-income test built on a table of minimum necessary monthly living
expenses by gross income band, from which discretionary income follows as gross income less
statutory deductions, less those necessary expenses, less every other committed obligation the
consumer discloses or the credit bureau records. Affordability is judged against what survives that
subtraction. The \ac{NCA} also caps the cost of credit by sub-sector, with maximum prescribed rates
in force from 6 May 2016 running from the repurchase rate plus 12 percent for mortgages to the
repurchase rate plus 21 percent for unsecured credit transactions \cite{sa_ncr_ratecaps_2016}.

\ac{BNPL} providers sit outside this regime. Levying neither interest nor fees on the deferred
amount, they argue that they do not transact credit within the meaning of the \ac{NCA}, and so are
not required to register with the \ac{NCR}, not required to perform the Regulation 23A assessment,
and, decisively for this thesis, not required to report obligations to credit bureaux
\cite{nortonrose_bnpl_sa}. TransUnion \cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025} confirms the last point from
inside the bureau system, listing \ac{BNPL} history among the categories of information absent from
the standard South African credit report. The same survey finds \ac{BNPL} named by 20 percent of
consumers intending to apply for credit, behind only new credit cards at 30 percent and new
personal loans at 28 percent.

This is the mechanism the model formalises. A registered lender conducting a lawful and diligent
affordability assessment computes discretionary income from a bureau record that is incomplete by
regulatory construction. The invisibility of \ac{BNPL} obligations to traditional lenders is not a
simplification adopted for tractability. It is a documented feature of the South African regulatory
perimeter, and the model treats bureau visibility accordingly, as a switchable intervention lever.

\subsection*{Synthesis and Gap}

Each of these literatures answers a different part of the question, and none answers it whole.
deHaan et al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl} establish that \ac{BNPL} harms financial health but observe a
United States market in which the product is at least partly visible to bureaux. The \ac{CFPB}
\cite{cfpb2025bnpl} measures concurrent borrowing at scale but describes rather than explains it.
The behavioural work \cite{meier2010present, Keys2019, Kuchler2021, ackert2025bnpl} explains why
individuals borrow and fail to repay, but stops at the individual. The agent-based models
\cite{cardaci2018inequality, dorazio2017micro, madeira2018chile, hamill2023creditcard} show how
individual credit behaviour aggregates into instability, yet none of them includes a lender class
that is exempt from affordability regulation and invisible to its competitors.

That last gap is where this thesis sits. The South African regulatory perimeter
\cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015, nortonrose_bnpl_sa, transunion_cps_sa_2025} produces a credit
market containing two lender types, one regulated and informed, the other neither, lending to the
same households. Modelling that market at the balance-sheet level, over a household population drawn
from survey data rather than assumed, is what makes the three subsidiary questions tractable: when
stacking becomes self-reinforcing, at what level of \ac{BNPL} access population default shifts
non-linearly, and whether cool-off interventions change outcomes or merely postpone them.

\section*{ABM Design}

This chapter specifies the model using the \ac{ODD} protocol \cite{grimm2006odd, grimm2020odd}, the
standard for communicating agent-based models. The second update's \emph{purpose and patterns}
element is used as intended: the patterns that judge the model's realism are stated in advance,
before any result is presented, so that the validation criteria cannot be chosen after the fact.

Every behavioural rule in the model is specified here, and each carries a citation, parameters with
either a source or an explicit sweep, and a validation hook. Two exceptions are marked in place
rather than quietly filled. The borrowing-amount rule of Submodel~4 is adopted by assumption, since
the literature sweep found no work that fixes one, and it therefore carries mandatory rather than
optional sensitivity analysis. The cap on concurrent facilities in Submodel~15 is hypothetical, as
no jurisdiction currently imposes such a cap, and it is labelled so rather than presented alongside
the interventions that do exist.

\subsection*{1. Overview}

\subsubsection*{1.1 Purpose and patterns}

The model exists to answer one question: how does the availability of multiple concurrent \ac{BNPL}
facilities affect the trajectory of consumer financial distress among \ac{LMI} South African
households, and what platform-level interventions reduce systemic default?

The design that answers it is a \textbf{counterfactual experiment, not a historical fit}. A
household population is calibrated to observed South African conditions in 2017, a period in which
\ac{BNPL} was effectively absent from the South African market. That population's credit behaviour
is validated against the 2017 first-quarter \ac{NCR} \ac{CCMR} \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017}. A \ac{BNPL}
lender class is then \emph{injected} into the calibrated economy and the change in dynamics is
observed. The model therefore answers what \ac{BNPL} does to a household population of this kind,
and not what happened in South Africa in any particular later year.

The 2017 anchor is a control rather than a limitation. \ac{BNPL} reached the South African market at
scale from roughly 2021, so any attempt to fit the model to its actual arrival would have to
separate it from pandemic income shocks, the 2020 credit contraction and post-pandemic inflation,
none of which this thesis is equipped to identify. The absence of \ac{BNPL} in 2017 is what makes
the injection interpretable. Accordingly, no model output is claimed to describe an actual year
after 2017, and results are read as directional and mechanistic rather than as forecasts.

Four patterns are registered in advance as the criteria for realism.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Complementarity, not substitution.} deHaan et al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl} find that
    \ac{BNPL} adoption is followed by rising credit-card interest and late fees. Enabling \ac{BNPL}
    in the model must therefore \emph{increase} arrears and interest burden on traditional debt. If
    the model instead shows traditional stress falling, the lender-choice submodel is wrong. Note
    that deHaan et al. measure fees and interest, not balances, so the model analogue is servicing
    stress rather than debt stock. This is the model's primary falsification test.
    \item \textbf{Baseline arrears.} With \ac{BNPL} disabled, the population's arrears profile
    should approximate the 2017 first-quarter \ac{CCMR} account-level figures for unsecured credit
    and credit facilities combined: 71.63 percent current, 16.54 percent at 60 or more days, and
    14.21 percent at 90 or more days \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017}.
    \item \textbf{Non-monotonic debt burden.} Hamill et al. \cite{hamill2023creditcard} report a
    non-monotonic relationship between income and debt-to-income ratio, with middle-income
    households carrying the highest balance-to-income values. A model producing debt-to-income
    rising monotonically in income would disagree with an independent model of the same phenomenon.
    \item \textbf{Savings exhaustion.} The share of agents reaching zero liquid savings should be
    consistent with the TransUnion Consumer Pulse finding that 36 percent of South African consumers
    anticipated missing a bill payment \cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025}.
\end{enumerate}

One qualification belongs here rather than in a footnote. The income-shock probability is
\emph{fitted} so that baseline arrears reproduce pattern~2. The baseline is therefore calibrated
rather than validated, and only the results obtained after \ac{BNPL} is injected are genuine
predictions. Patterns 1, 3 and 4 remain independent tests, because no parameter is tuned to them.

\subsubsection*{1.2 Entities, state variables and scales}

The model contains four entity types.

\textbf{Household agents} are the only adaptive entities. Each is a real \ac{NIDS} Wave 5 household,
carried into the model rather than synthesised. Static attributes comprise monthly income and its
dominant source (wage, grant or other), committed and discretionary expenditure, a liquid savings
proxy, consolidated traditional debt, income quintile, household size and composition, head
demographics, and four financial-inclusion flags matched in from FinScope: banked status, formal
credit access, savings product holding, and informal finance. Dynamic state comprises the current
cash balance, outstanding traditional debt, outstanding \ac{BNPL} obligations, accrued arrears, a
consecutive-distress counter, and a default flag. One behavioural type is assigned: minimum-payer or
scheduled-payer.

The \textbf{traditional lender} is a single aggregate entity holding all traditional debt. It does
not compete and does not adapt. Its only behaviour is a deterministic affordability gate.

The \textbf{credit bureau} is not an agent but a shared information structure. It records
traditional debt only. Its contents define what the traditional lender can observe, and its scope is
the model's central mechanism.

The \textbf{\ac{BNPL} platforms} are the injected entities. Four are active in the baseline, matching
the number of providers operating in South Africa. Each holds its own receivables, applies its own
light eligibility screen and per-order cap, and cannot observe any other platform's exposure. Their
state comprises outstanding receivables per household and the instalment schedule attached to each.

\textbf{Reference groups} are the model's social structure. Each household belongs to exactly one,
defined as the intersection of its income quintile and its province. This is deliberately the same
cell already used for the FinScope donor match, so no new construct is introduced. Over the
5{,}000-agent population it yields 45 groups, with a minimum of 23 agents, a median of 84 and a
maximum of 353. Groups are fixed at initialisation and static thereafter, consistent with static
household composition. Three groups in the Northern Cape fall between 23 and 31 agents and are
therefore noise-prone, which is noted wherever group-level results are reported.

The environment is homogeneous and exogenous, held at 2017 conditions. The time step is 14 days,
chosen to align with the pay-in-four \ac{BNPL} cadence and to divide cleanly into monthly survey
income. The horizon is 24 months, or 52 ticks, of which the first 12 ticks are burn-in and are
discarded. The population is 5{,}000 households, weighted-resampled from 3{,}250 unique \ac{NIDS}
source households. All monetary values are in 2017 Rands, with no inflation forwarding anywhere in
the model.

\subsubsection*{1.3 Process overview and scheduling}

Scheduling is synchronous at the level of the tick. Within a tick, households act in random
asynchronous order reseeded each tick, then the lender processes applications, then state updates
and observation occur. The reasoning behind random rather than uniform activation, and the reason
the peer channel is insensitive to it, are given in Submodel~17.

The order of operations inside a household's tick is itself a modelling decision, and it is
priority-ordered as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Income arrives, subject to the shock process.
    \item \textbf{Committed expenditure is paid first.} This is the minimum consumption floor and it
    outranks debt service.
    \item Scheduled debt service is attempted.
    \item Discretionary consumption is set.
    \item If cash is insufficient at step 2 or step 3, the household seeks credit.
    \item The household observes the \ac{BNPL} adoption share of its reference group \emph{as it
    stood at the end of the previous tick}, and its want-driven \ac{BNPL} propensity is set
    accordingly.
    \item Balances, arrears, the distress counter and default flags update, and group adoption
    shares are recomputed for the next tick.
\end{enumerate}

Two features of this ordering carry the model's mechanism. Committed expenditure precedes debt
service because Madeira's default condition is the failure to finance minimum consumption
\cite{madeira2018chile}, which is only coherent if consumption has priority; placing servicing first
would make default impossible by construction. Borrowing occurs last because in D'Orazio and
Giulioni \cite{dorazio2017micro} credit answers a realised shortfall rather than an anticipated one.
Placing it at step 5 is what permits a household to borrow in order to service existing debt, which
is the first of the two self-reinforcing spirals the thesis exists to observe. The second is social
and enters at step 6. The one-tick lag on the group adoption share is not cosmetic: reading a share
that is still being written within the same tick would make the synchronous update ill-defined and
would introduce an artificial dependence on activation order.

\subsection*{2. Design concepts}

\textbf{Basic principles.} The balance sheet is the accounting core, following
\cite{cardaci2018inequality} and \cite{dorazio2017micro}. Household behaviour is drawn from
behavioural economics rather than intertemporal optimisation, because the borrowing and repayment
patterns the model must reproduce are not consistent with the latter
\cite{meier2010present, Keys2019, Kuchler2021}. Regulatory constraints are modelled as explicit
rule-based gates and treated as policy levers, following \cite{yun2020housing}.

\textbf{Emergence.} Population default rates, the aggregate debt trajectory, and any threshold
behaviour in the relationship between \ac{BNPL} access and systemic default are emergent. Nothing in
the rules specifies a default rate, a stacking depth, or a tipping point. The quantities the research
questions ask about are precisely the quantities not imposed.

Two distinct positive feedback loops can generate that behaviour, and the model is constructed so
they can be separated. The \emph{financial} loop runs through the balance sheet: a household short
of cash borrows to meet its obligations, which raises future servicing, which deepens the shortfall.
The \emph{social} loop runs through the reference group: a household that adopts \ac{BNPL} raises
its group's adoption share, which raises its neighbours' propensity to adopt. Setting the peer
coefficient to zero disables the second loop while leaving the first intact, so the contribution of
each can be measured rather than asserted.

\textbf{Adaptation.} Households adapt through consumption compression, borrowing and repayment
choice. They do not optimise; they follow heuristics keyed to immediate cash position. The lender
does not adapt at all.

\textbf{Objectives.} Households have no explicit objective function. They meet obligations in
priority order and compress discretionary spending under pressure. This is a deliberate rejection of
utility maximisation, defensible because present bias predicts borrowing behaviour that utility
maximisation does not \cite{meier2010present}.

\textbf{Learning.} None. Households do not update rules from experience within the horizon. This is
a stated simplification.

\textbf{Prediction.} None beyond the implicit assumption that current income persists. Households
form no forecast of future income or prices.

\textbf{Sensing.} Households sense their own balance sheet exactly, and sense one aggregate
property of their reference group: the share of its members currently holding a \ac{BNPL} balance,
lagged one tick. They do not observe any individual neighbour, nor any neighbour's income, debt or
distress. The lender senses the household's declared gross income, its own outstanding loans, and
all other traditional debt through the bureau. It does not sense \ac{BNPL} obligations or informal
debt. That second asymmetry is the model's central mechanism and is discussed as a submodel below.

The contrast is worth stating plainly. Households can see \ac{BNPL} spreading among their peers;
the regulated lender cannot see it at all.

\textbf{Interaction.} There are two channels. The first is bipartite, between households and
lenders, mediated by credit applications and repayments. The second is household-to-household, and
runs through the reference group: a household's want-driven \ac{BNPL} propensity rises with its
group's adoption share. Influence is indirect and aggregate rather than dyadic, since a household
responds to a summary statistic of its group and not to identified individuals.

Interaction is confined to the \emph{want-driven} path. A household facing a genuine shortfall
borrows regardless of what its peers do, which follows from Ackert et al. \cite{ackert2025bnpl},
where social approval shapes the \emph{choice} of \ac{BNPL} for a discretionary purchase rather than
the underlying need for credit. Peer influence on consumption itself, the expenditure-cascade
mechanism of \cite{cardaci2018inequality}, is not implemented, and is recorded as the natural
extension.

\textbf{Stochasticity.} Three sources. The income shock is Bernoulli. The behavioural type
assignment is drawn once at initialisation. The want-driven \ac{BNPL} purchase trigger is
probabilistic, and its probability is now state-dependent rather than fixed, since it responds to
the group adoption share. All runs are seeded, and reported results are averaged across replicates
with dispersion shown. Replication matters more than it did before the peer channel was introduced,
because positive feedback amplifies early random differences between runs.

\textbf{Collectives.} The reference group, income quintile crossed with province, is a genuine
collective in the \ac{ODD} sense: agents belong to one, and its aggregate state feeds back into
their behaviour. It is not merely a reporting category. That the same cell serves three purposes,
as the FinScope match cell, the reporting stratum and the social reference group, is economical
rather than accidental, since all three rest on the same claim that income and geography together
identify households in comparable circumstances.

\textbf{Observation.} Collected each tick: population default rate, arrears distribution by \ac{CCMR}
age band, aggregate traditional and \ac{BNPL} debt, mean and quintile debt-service-to-income,
consumption compression, share of households at zero savings, and application and rejection counts.

\subsection*{3. Details}

\subsubsection*{3.1 Initialisation}

The population is loaded from the static data layer described in the previous chapter, in which
5{,}000 households were resampled with probability proportional to survey weight from 10{,}841 valid
\ac{NIDS} Wave 5 households, then enriched with FinScope financial-inclusion flags by a cell-donor
match on per-capita income quintile crossed with province.

Opening balance sheets are the survey values. Monthly servicing on opening traditional debt is
constructed rather than observed, since neither survey records a repayment amount: the balance is
amortised at the \ac{NCA} statutory maximum prescribed rate for the relevant sub-sector, evaluated
at the 2017 repurchase rate of 7.00 percent, and then capped by the affordability rule in
Submodel~9. Opening \ac{BNPL} balances are zero in every run, which is the injection design.

\subsubsection*{3.2 Input data}

The model uses no external time series. The macro environment is static and exogenous at 2017
conditions. All input is the initialised population and the parameter set.

\subsubsection*{3.3 Submodels}

\textbf{Submodel 1: income and shock.} Baseline income is static per household. A Bernoulli shock is
applied to \emph{labour} income only, following Madeira's treatment of labour income shocks
\cite{madeira2018chile}, and therefore only to households whose dominant income source is wage
income. Grant-dependent households face no labour shock, since South African social grants are
statutory transfers that do not vary with employment. The shock hits income rather than savings and
does not persist beyond one tick. Its probability is not available from any source: \ac{NIDS} Wave 5
is a single wave in this design, so within-household income volatility cannot be estimated from it.
The probability is consequently promoted from assumption to calibration target, fitted so that
baseline arrears reproduce pattern~2 and then held fixed across all \ac{BNPL} runs. Shock magnitude
is swept.

\textbf{Submodel 2: consumption.} Consumption is two-tier and taken directly from the data layer.
Committed expenditure, comprising food and rent, is a hard floor and is not compressible.
Discretionary expenditure is the target and compresses to zero when the household is cash
constrained. A household compresses discretionary spending fully before missing a debt instalment. A
propensity-to-consume or buffer-stock rule was rejected because both require a calibrated propensity
for which no South African estimate exists, and both would discard the observed expenditure split
already held in the data.

\textbf{Submodel 3: borrowing trigger.} The trigger differs by lender type. Traditional credit is
sought only on a realised shortfall, when cash cannot meet committed expenditure plus scheduled
service \cite{dorazio2017micro}. \ac{BNPL} has an additional want-driven path: an eligible household
initiates a discretionary \ac{BNPL} purchase with a probability that is not fixed but responds to
peer behaviour, as specified in Submodel~11. The second trigger is necessary because Ackert et al.
\cite{ackert2025bnpl} find \ac{BNPL} uptake driven by social norm rather than need, and deHaan et
al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl} find harm following adoption even among consumers with credit available.
A purely shortfall-driven \ac{BNPL} agent would model \ac{BNPL} as a pure substitute and could
reproduce neither finding. Peer influence applies to this path alone; the shortfall path is
untouched, since a household in genuine need borrows irrespective of what its peers are doing.

\textbf{Submodel 4: borrowing amount.} \textbf{This submodel is an assumption with no literature
anchor.} The sweep of consumer-credit agent-based models and behavioural studies located no work
that fixes a borrowing amount rule; D'Orazio and Giulioni use the shortfall but do not defend the
choice. The household requests exactly the shortfall with no precautionary buffer, and for \ac{BNPL}
the amount is anchored to purchase size. Because the rule is uncited, sensitivity analysis over the
shortfall, the shortfall increased by one quarter, and the shortfall plus one tick of committed
expenditure is mandatory rather than optional, and any material movement of results across those
specifications must be reported as amount-rule sensitivity.

\textbf{Submodel 5: lender choice.} Where \ac{BNPL} exists and the need is eligible, households
approach \ac{BNPL} first, with traditional credit sought for the remainder. In the no-\ac{BNPL}
baseline the traditional lender is the only channel. Cost ranking was rejected: \ac{BNPL} is
nominally interest-free, so a cost rule selects it trivially and attributes the choice to price,
whereas the evidence attributes it to convenience and social approval \cite{ackert2025bnpl}. The
distinction matters because the two mechanisms imply different interventions.

\textbf{Submodel 6: repayment and arrears.} Repayment is mixed rather than uniform. A share of
households are minimum-payers who pay only the contractual minimum; the remainder pay the scheduled
amortised instalment. A household unable to meet its due amount pays what it can and accrues the
residual as arrears. The minimum-payer share is set at 0.29, from Keys and Wang's finding that 29
percent of credit-card accounts pay at or near the contractual minimum \cite{Keys2019}. That figure
is United States credit-card evidence applied to South African unsecured credit, which is a transfer
assumption, and it is swept from 0.20 to 0.40. Arrears are tracked in \ac{CCMR} age bands.

\textbf{Submodel 7: distress and default.} A household is distressed in a tick when income plus
available credit cannot cover committed expenditure plus scheduled service, and defaults after seven
consecutive distressed ticks. Seven is not a round number chosen for convenience: at a 14-day tick it
is 98 days, which falls inside the \ac{CCMR} 91-to-120-day bucket and therefore maps cleanly onto the
90-or-more-days impairment convention, whereas six ticks would be 84 days and would land inside the
61-to-90-day bucket with no clean analogue in the validation target. A cash-flow insolvency test was
chosen over a debt-service-to-income threshold because such a threshold is arbitrary and because,
as Submodel~9 sets out, the \ac{NCA} itself does not use one; Madeira's minimum-consumption test is
cited, consistent with the affordability rule already implemented, and validated against observed
default rates in a middle-income economy \cite{madeira2018chile}. Default consequences are
credit cut-off from the traditional lender, which observes the default through the bureau, and
penalty accrual. There is no scarring or rehabilitation path, which over 24 months is tolerable but
must be stated.

\textbf{Submodel 8: heterogeneity.} Rule parameters vary by income quintile and by the demographic
and financial-inclusion attributes the data layer already carries. Only one latent behavioural type
is introduced, the binary minimum-payer distinction of Submodel~6. A continuous present-bias
parameter was rejected because no South African distribution exists against which to calibrate it,
so it would add a free parameter with no discipline.

\textbf{Submodel 9: the credit-granting gate.} The lender applies a \textbf{residual-income test},
not a debt-service-to-income cap. This is not a modelling preference. Regulation 23A of the
Affordability Assessment Regulations \cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015} prescribes no
debt-service-to-income ratio; it prescribes a table of minimum necessary monthly living expenses by
gross income band, against which affordability is assessed on what remains. Credit is granted only
where gross income, less statutory deductions, less Regulation 23A necessary expenses, less visible
existing obligations, covers the new instalment. The resulting ceiling varies with income, from 10.4
percent of income at R900 per month to 83.2 percent at R7{,}712 per month. A flat cap would be both
unsourced and, at the bottom of the distribution, wildly over-permissive: a 65 percent rule would
permit a household earning R900 per month to service R585 per month. Regulation 23A applies to an
individual consumer and is applied here to household income, which is a stated simplification.

\textbf{Submodel 10: information asymmetry.} The bureau records traditional debt only. The lender
observes declared gross income, its own loans, and other traditional debt. It does not observe
\ac{BNPL} obligations, which fall outside the \ac{NCA} and carry no bureau-reporting obligation
\cite{nortonrose_bnpl_sa}, a gap TransUnion confirms from inside the bureau system by listing
\ac{BNPL} history among the information absent from the standard South African credit report
\cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025}. Nor does it observe informal debt. South African informal finance runs
substantially through stokvels, burial societies and similar rotating savings arrangements
\cite{investing_in_social_capital}, along with mashonisa lending, none of which reports to a credit
bureau; it therefore enters the model only as static balance-sheet state carried from \ac{NIDS}. The consequence is
that the Regulation 23A test is computed over an incomplete liability set. The lender is neither
unlawful nor careless: it complies with the regulation using the record the regulation gives it, and
that is the point. Bureau visibility of \ac{BNPL} is a boolean parameter, disabled by default, and
is also the intervention lever of Submodel~14. Running with it enabled should recover affordability
outcomes close to the no-\ac{BNPL} baseline, so the gap between the two runs measures the cost of
the regulatory reporting gap directly.

\textbf{Submodel 11: peer influence.} Each household belongs to one reference group, defined as its
income quintile crossed with its province. Let $s_g(t-1)$ denote the share of households in group
$g$ holding a non-zero \ac{BNPL} balance at the end of the previous tick. The want-driven \ac{BNPL}
propensity of household $i$ is

\begin{equation*}
    q_i(t) = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(q_{\text{base}} + \beta \, s_{g(i)}(t-1),\; 0,\; 1\right).
\end{equation*}

A strictly positive $q_{\text{base}}$ is structurally required rather than merely convenient. Every
household begins with no \ac{BNPL} balance, so $s_g(0)=0$ everywhere, and under a purely
multiplicative rule adoption could never begin. The two coefficients therefore play the roles that
$p$ and $q$ play in Bass diffusion: $q_{\text{base}}$ governs spontaneous adoption and $\beta$
governs imitation.

Three literatures license this submodel. Ackert et al. \cite{ackert2025bnpl} supply the domain
claim, that \ac{BNPL} uptake specifically is norm-driven, since their participants chose \ac{BNPL}
over a credit-card loan for the same purchase and expected their social networks to approve. Cardaci
\cite{cardaci2018inequality} supplies the methodological precedent, since peer effects and
expenditure cascades are central to the household-credit agent-based model this thesis takes as its
principal anchor. Granovetter \cite{granovetter1978threshold} supplies the mechanism, being the
canonical account of how share-dependent adoption with heterogeneous thresholds yields discontinuous
aggregate outcomes.

Neither $q_{\text{base}}$ nor $\beta$ can be calibrated: no South African data fixes either. This
raises a methodological difficulty that must be met directly rather than buried. The submodel exists
partly because the second research question asks for a non-linear threshold, and with wholly
independent households the population default rate is close to a smooth function of \ac{BNPL}
access, thresholds being products of feedback. Introducing a peer channel and then reporting
non-linearity as a finding would be circular.

The resolution is to treat $\beta = 0$ as a control arm rather than as an uninteresting corner of
the parameter space. At $\beta = 0$ the model reduces exactly to independent agents, and results for
both the first and second research questions are reported as a surface over the swept parameter and
$\beta$ jointly, with the $\beta = 0$ row always shown. The claim available at the end is then not
that a threshold exists, but that population default responds non-linearly to \ac{BNPL} access
\emph{only where social transmission is present}, which is both stronger and falsifiable. The same
device separates the two feedback loops for the first research question, since $\beta = 0$ isolates
the financial loop from the social one.

Whether a \emph{sharp} threshold emerges is left as an empirical question about the model rather
than an assumption built into it. Linear coupling of this kind produces diffusion; whether diffusion
combined with the affordability gate of Submodel~9 and the arrears accrual of Submodel~6 produces a
sharp transition is not presupposed. Should the linear form yield only a smooth response,
Granovetter-style heterogeneous individual thresholds are registered here, in advance, as the
structural robustness check.

Finally, the submodel is inert in the baseline. With \ac{BNPL} disabled, $s_g$ is identically zero,
so the calibration of baseline arrears against the 2017 \ac{CCMR} is untouched by anything in this
submodel.

\textbf{Submodel 12: BNPL eligibility and limit.} A household is eligible only if it is
\emph{banked}. Both major South African providers debit a bank card for the checkout instalment
\cite{payflex_terms, payjustnow_terms}, so an unbanked household cannot transact at all. Eligibility
is therefore capped at 83.1 percent of the population by an observed data field rather than by
assumption, and the access sweep of the second research question runs within the banked
subpopulation. The platform applies a light automated screen and a per-order cap of R15{,}000,
following Payflex. The screen is deliberately \emph{not} the Regulation 23A residual-income test of
Submodel~9: that asymmetry is the mechanism under study, since \ac{BNPL} routes around the
affordability assessment precisely because it sits outside the \ac{NCA}. Making the screen a real
affordability test is an intervention, not the baseline.

\textbf{Submodel 13: stacking.} A household may hold concurrent facilities across several platforms,
with no aggregate limit. Platforms cannot observe one another, and this is a consequence of
Submodel~10 rather than a fresh assumption: with no bureau-reporting obligation there exists no
shared infrastructure through which one platform could see another's exposure. Each platform
therefore screens against a liability set it cannot fully see, exactly as the traditional lender
does. Four platforms are active in the baseline, matching the South African market, and the number
is swept from one to six, with a single platform serving as the control that isolates
single-platform accumulation from genuine cross-platform stacking. Stacking depth is emergent rather
than imposed, and at a realistic access rate the model should approximate the \ac{CFPB} shares:
roughly 63 percent of adopters holding simultaneous loans and 32 percent holding across different
firms \cite{cfpb2025bnpl}. Those are United States figures, so the comparison is an
order-of-magnitude check rather than a point target.

\textbf{Submodel 14: BNPL repayment and penalties.} Repayment follows the Payflex Pay in 4 schedule
\cite{payflex_terms}: 25 percent of the purchase at checkout, then 25 percent at each of the next
three ticks, interest free. This confirms rather than merely assumes the choice of a 14-day step.
The design chapter adopted a biweekly tick on the grounds that it aligns with the pay-in-four
cadence; because Payflex instalments fall every two weeks, they land exactly on model ticks and no
discretisation error enters the \ac{BNPL} schedule anywhere. Interest is zero, which is the product
and also the basis on which providers claim to fall outside the \ac{NCA}. A missed instalment
accrues a late fee of R95 per week capped at three weeks, so R190 per tick to a maximum of R285.
Once that cap is exhausted the balance converts to arrears and the household is cut off from that
platform while remaining eligible at others, since platforms are blind to each other.

\textbf{Submodel 15: intervention levers.} Four levers answer the third research question, and three
correspond to instruments that exist in the world. The first is \textbf{bureau visibility}, a boolean
that closes the South African reporting gap of Submodel~10; the difference between the two settings
measures the cost of the reporting exemption directly. The second is a \textbf{mandatory
affordability check}, which applies the Regulation 23A test to \ac{BNPL} as well as to traditional
credit, expressing the \ac{FCA}'s requirement of proportionate affordability checks
\cite{fca_ps26_1} in South African statutory terms. That requirement descends from the Woolard
Review \cite{woolard2021}, whose recommendations began the process of bringing \ac{BNPL} inside the
United Kingdom regulatory perimeter, and it is precisely the assessment South African providers are
not obliged to perform. The third is a \textbf{cool-off period}, set at
one tick in the baseline because section 66A of the Consumer Credit Act gives a 14-day right of
withdrawal, which again falls exactly on a tick boundary; after a want-driven purchase a household
cannot initiate another for the cool-off duration, while the shortfall-driven path remains open,
since a cool-off on emergency borrowing would be a different instrument answering a different
question. The fourth is a \textbf{cap on concurrent facilities}, and this one is genuinely
hypothetical: no jurisdiction currently imposes such a cap, and it is labelled as such rather than
presented alongside the three real instruments.

The third research question asks whether cool-off interventions change outcomes or whether agents
simply defer rather than desist, and the model answers it directly. The comparison is
\emph{cumulative \ac{BNPL} volume over the full horizon} with and without the cool-off, not the
timing of purchases. If total volume is unchanged and only its distribution over time shifts, agents
defer. If cumulative volume falls, they desist.

\textbf{Submodel 16: macro environment.} Fully static. No macro variable varies within a run, and
the 2017 repurchase rate of 7.00 percent is held constant, so the statutory maximum rates feeding
Submodel~6 are constant as well. This is a consequence of the counterfactual design rather than a
matter for the literature: the experiment exists to isolate the effect of injecting \ac{BNPL}, and
any macro time-variation would confound that effect with a macro effect, making it impossible to
attribute a change in default to the injection. Both Cardaci \cite{cardaci2018inequality} and
Madeira \cite{madeira2018chile} do carry macro dynamics, because both ask macro questions; the
divergence here is deliberate and follows from asking a product-level question over 24 months. The
cost is that the model cannot speak to how \ac{BNPL} stress interacts with a downturn, which,
given that \ac{BNPL} grew in South Africa through a period of rising rates, is named as future work.

\textbf{Submodel 17: activation order.} Households are activated in random asynchronous order,
reseeded every tick, and the lender processes applications only once all households have acted.
Random rather than uniform activation is chosen because credit in this model is scarce, being
rationed by the affordability gate of Submodel~9 and by per-platform limits, and a fixed order would
hand the same households first claim on that scarce resource in every tick, manufacturing a
persistent artefactual advantage. The choice is not incidental: Comer and Loerch
\cite{comer2013activation} find statistically significant differences in emergent population
behaviour across uniform, synchronous and random activation in a replicated civil-violence model,
and Alizadeh and Cioffi-Revilla \cite{alizadeh2015activation} find that different regimes produce
different results with no scheme dominating.

One property of the design deserves emphasis, because it is a payoff of an earlier decision. The
peer channel of Submodel~11 is \emph{activation-order independent by construction}, since it reads
the reference-group adoption share lagged one tick. Every household within a tick therefore sees the
same settled share regardless of when it acts, and the model's principal interaction does not
inherit the sensitivity these authors warn about. Had the share been read live within the tick,
results would have depended on activation order and the two decisions would have become entangled.
Following Comer and Loerch directly, the model is nonetheless re-run under uniform and under fully
synchronous activation, and any material movement is reported as activation-order sensitivity;
asserting robustness without testing it would not be defensible in a model containing a positive
feedback loop.
