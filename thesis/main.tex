\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

% Every number is read from a generated table or a results/summary JSON:

% 0.10 +/- 0.06, difference -0.09 +/- 0.08 (Section 5). No systematic rise with platforms: two of
% the 20 contrasts with one platform pass two SEs (nmax_minus_n1_beta*, Section 4.3). The group
% the lender's test refuses: results/summary/short_group_lending.json (55 of the 120, Section 4.1).
% February 2027: businessday_bnpl_2026 attributes to the NCR an order to report from then.
This thesis asks how \ac{BNPL} changes credit distress in a model of South African households, and what
bureau visibility and affordability screening would change. Bureau reporting of \ac{BNPL} is
reportedly to be mandatory from 2027.
We build an agent-based model of 5{,}000 households from \ac{NIDS} and \acs{FinScope} microdata. A traditional lender applies the Regulation 23A test to the debts the bureau records, while four \ac{BNPL} platforms apply a light screen and cannot see one another's balances. Job losses offset hiring, which follows labour-force data.
The baseline starts from the 2017 arrears profile, and two parameters are fitted to its five bands before \ac{BNPL} is added.
A household defaults when an instalment on any facility is more than 90 days past due.
The constructed population matches household size, income inequality and the order of magnitude of debt, and the model holds the arrears profile as a share for two years while its book of credit-active households halves. The profile is an input and a calibration target, so it cannot validate the model. We therefore interpret differences between runs, conditional on their shared assumptions.
Under the assumed routing, many households owe several platforms at once, and default shows no systematic rise with the number of platforms.
Adding \ac{BNPL} raises default during the two-year run by 1.0 percentage point, from 14.4\% in the baseline.
The effect runs through households that use \ac{BNPL} to bridge cash shortfalls and default on it, most of them households whose surveyed income does not cover food and rent and whom the lender's test refuses; it is not detectable when \ac{BNPL} cannot be used against a shortfall.
Peer influence acts only on want-driven purchases. It raises borrowing 2.4-fold at the top of the range tested, and the effect shows no detectable slope.
The model has no credit score, so bureau visibility acts through the lender's Regulation 23A test. Adding \ac{BNPL} obligations to it raises refusals by about 13\% and default by 0.20 points without peer influence, because in the model refused households have no other credit; the estimate with peer influence does not differ detectably.
Screening \ac{BNPL} requests with the same test lowers default by 0.9 to 1.0 points, almost all in the poorest quintile. Bureau visibility also leaves more households short of food and rent. Screening does so only without peer influence, and the runs do not record how much goes unpaid, so screening's welfare effect is open.

\section*{Data and Code}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Data and Code}

% TODO (front matter): confirm whether the department requires a separate ethics
% statement for secondary analysis of licensed survey microdata, and the required
% wording for the data acknowledgements. Blocked on the departmental template.

This study is a secondary analysis of licensed survey microdata. \ac{NIDS} Wave 5
is produced by the Southern Africa Labour and Development Research Unit at the
University of Cape Town and distributed by DataFirst~\citep{nids_w5_2018}; the 2019
\ac{FinScope} is produced by FinMark Trust~\citep{finscope2019}. Both are used under
their access conditions, and no respondent is identifiable in the data or the
results. The simulation code, the constructed population and the scripts that
generate every table and figure are submitted with this thesis. The simulations were run in
Python 3.14.4 with Mesa 3.5.1; \texttt{simulation/requirements.txt} pins every package
version. The results come from the commit tagged in the code release.
