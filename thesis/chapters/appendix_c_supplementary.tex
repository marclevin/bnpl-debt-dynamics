\subsection{Calibration Procedure}
\label{app:calibration}

The separation rate is set first and is not fitted: 0.00446 per tick, the re-employment rate
of 0.0187 times 1{,}113 strictly unemployed over 4{,}670 employed members. Friction is then
chosen from $\{0,\allowbreak 0.02,\allowbreak 0.04,\allowbreak 0.06,\allowbreak 0.08,\allowbreak 0.09,\allowbreak 0.10,\allowbreak 0.12,\allowbreak 0.16\}$, with \ac{BNPL} disabled and
twenty replicates (seeds 1{,}000--1{,}019) at each value, by the smallest absolute error against
the 1--30 day band. The selected value is 0.09, which gives 8.27\% against 8.24\%. The 90+ share
barely moves with friction, from 8.76\% to 9.29\% across the grid, so friction cannot stand in
for the separation rate.

An earlier version fitted the separation rate to the 90+ band without labour flows. That fit
chose 0.016, about 1.4 times the upper end of the \ac{QLFS} band, and because no member who
started out of work could find a job, the share of starting earners out of work rose from zero
to 39\% by the final tick. The model reached the 2017 stock of long arrears only by producing a
recession that did not occur, so the fit was dropped. That version gave a \ac{BNPL} effect of
$0.60 \pm 0.07$ points at $\beta=1$, against $0.17 \pm 0.04$ now.

\subsection{Submodel 9: The Credit-Granting Gate}
\label{app:gate}

The lender uses a simplified residual-income test based on the Regulation 23A expense norms
\cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015}. It grants credit when gross household income, less the expense
norm and visible existing obligations, covers the new instalment.
Income is the amount observed in the survey, which the model does not reduce during a spell of
unemployment.
The implementation does not deduct tax or other statutory deductions, which also makes the test
more permissive. The resulting ceiling
varies with income, from 10.4\% of income at R900 per month to 83.2\% at R7{,}712 per month, and
two hand-computed checks from the regulation's Table~1 are asserted in the verification suite
(Table~\ref{tab:reg23a-norms}).

Regulation 23A applies to an individual consumer and is applied here to household income, because
\ac{NIDS} is a household survey. For a multi-earner household this makes the test more permissive
than the regulation would be in practice. It also means the ceilings above, which are evaluated at
per-capita quintile bounds, understate what the test permits a large household at the same bound:
across the population the applied ceiling spans zero to 93.2\% of gross income, with a mean of
49.6\% in the bottom quintile.

\subsection{Submodel 17: Activation Order}
\label{app:activation}

Households act in random asynchronous order, reseeded every tick. The peer channel of Submodel~11
is activation-order independent by construction, since it reads the reference-group adoption share
lagged one tick, so every household within a tick sees the same settled share regardless of when it
acts. Comer and Loerch \cite{comer2013activation} and Alizadeh and Cioffi-Revilla
\cite{alizadeh2015activation} find population behaviour sensitive to the activation regime, so the
model is also run under a fixed order, the same every tick: default changes by $-0.07 \pm 0.07$
points (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}).

The lender has no shared funding constraint, and platform limits apply per household.
Activation order therefore does not allocate a common credit pool. The alternative schedule
checks whether implementation details or the assignment of random draws affect the outcomes.

\subsection{Two Further Levers}
\label{app:levers}

Two further levers are run at the same two values of $\beta$ as the
scenarios of Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}, with twenty replicates and their own benchmark arm. The first is a cooling-off window that blocks want-driven
\ac{BNPL} purchases for $k^{\text{cool}}$ ticks after a want-driven purchase. One tick matches the
fourteen-day right of withdrawal under section 66A of the United Kingdom's Consumer Credit Act
1974, which the \ac{FCA} confirms applies to regulated deferred payment credit
\cite{fca_ps26_1}, although that
right cancels an agreement and does not block the next one. It does not
block borrowing for a shortfall. The second is a cap on the number of platforms a household
may owe, which no jurisdiction we found imposes and which is therefore hypothetical. Both are judged on cumulative \ac{BNPL} volume as well as
default (Tables~\ref{tab:cooloff} and~\ref{tab:cap}; Figure~\ref{fig:scenarios-appendix}
places them beside the scenarios).

The cap counts platforms on which the household owes a balance. It does not count agreements.
A household that owes the stated number of platforms or more is refused every new agreement,
including one on a platform it already uses, until a balance clears. A cap of one therefore
allows one agreement at a time, which is stricter than the single-platform arm of
Table~\ref{tab:stacking}, where a household may hold several agreements at once. Under a cap of
two or three a household below the cap may open further agreements on a platform it already
uses, so it can hold more agreements than the cap. A shortfall request that the cap refuses
passes in full to the traditional lender. Under a cap of one, new traditional lending is
R$0.09 \pm 0.08$ million higher than in the benchmark at $\beta = 0$ and R$0.07 \pm 0.09$
million at $\beta = 1$, neither detectable.
% lever_cap1_beta{0,1}_lending_diff (+ _se), results/summary/results_numbers.json.

The levers give fourteen contrasts with the benchmark. At two standard errors chance alone would
make about one of them detectable, and two are. At $\beta = 0$ the two-tick window raises default
by $0.15 \pm 0.06$ points, while the one- and three-tick windows give $+0.10 \pm 0.08$ and
$+0.12 \pm 0.08$ and the four-tick window $+0.03 \pm 0.07$. The window removes 7.3\% of
\ac{BNPL} volume at two ticks and 14.4\% at four, so a rise confined to two ticks has no
mechanism we can point to, and we treat it as chance. At $\beta = 1$ the four-tick window lowers
default by $0.21 \pm 0.07$ points and removes 79.1\% of volume; the two- and three-tick windows
give $-0.13 \pm 0.08$ and $-0.10 \pm 0.08$, the same sign without being detectable. No cap
changes default detectably at either $\beta$. At $\beta = 1$ the caps of one and two give
$-0.14 \pm 0.08$ and $-0.13 \pm 0.07$ points and remove 65.3\% and 31.6\% of volume.

Counting agreements in place of platforms does not change the cap's effect on default. A
diagnostic on the cap arms' seeds, paired with an arm without a cap, compares three definitions. At $\beta = 1$ and a cap of
two, the cap as modelled lowers default by $0.11 \pm 0.05$ points, about two standard errors, and removes 31.8\% of volume,
against $-0.13 \pm 0.07$ and 31.6\% in Table~\ref{tab:cap}, where the cap arms are compared
unpaired with the benchmark on other seeds. A cap that counts agreements in place of platforms
removes 33.1\% of volume and also lowers default by $0.11 \pm 0.05$ points. Paired by seed, the
cap as modelled less the agreements cap is $+0.00 \pm 0.06$ points. At a cap of one the two
definitions coincide, and at a cap of three the difference is $+0.03 \pm 0.05$. A cap on
platforms that still allows further agreements on platforms already in use removes 1.4\% of
volume and changes default by $+0.02 \pm 0.05$ points. The cap therefore works through the
volume it removes.

\subsection{Further Extensions}
\label{app:extensions}

Two extensions lie further from the thesis's results than those in the final paragraph of Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\textbf{Macroeconomic dynamics.} Submodel~16 holds the environment static to protect the
experimental control. A successor design could relax this, since \ac{BNPL} grew in South Africa
through a period of rising rates, and the interaction between \ac{BNPL} stress and a downturn is
what a regulator would want to know. The injection effect and the macro effect would then have to
be separately identified, which requires a more elaborate design than a single counterfactual
injection.

\textbf{Competition among traditional lenders.} The traditional side is a single non-adaptive stub.
Hamill et al.\ \cite{hamill2023creditcard} find, in a model of the United Kingdom card market, that longer interest-free promotions
raise card balances, so a competing, adapting traditional sector could add a driver of
indebtedness that this model holds fixed. In their model the share of accounts with missed
payments did not rise, so the channel is balance growth and not default.
