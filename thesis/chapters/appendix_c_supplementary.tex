\label{app:supplementary}

This appendix holds material kept out of the body for length: the calibration procedure,

\subsection{Calibration Procedure}
\label{app:calibration}

The separation rate is set first and is not fitted: 0.00446 per tick, the re-employment rate
of 0.0187 times 1{,}113 strictly unemployed over 4{,}670 employed members. The write-off rate is
derived next, 0.0707 per tick (Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}). Payment friction and arrears
persistence are then chosen jointly, friction from
$\{0.05,\allowbreak 0.0525,\allowbreak 0.055,\allowbreak 0.0575,\allowbreak 0.06\}$ and persistence
from $\{0.70,\allowbreak 0.71,\allowbreak 0.72,\allowbreak 0.73,\allowbreak 0.74\}$, with \ac{BNPL}
disabled and twenty replicates (seeds 1{,}000--1{,}019) at each pair, by the smallest root sum of
squared errors over the five arrears bands. The selected pair, 0.0575 and 0.72, gives an error of
0.98 points. Fitting each parameter to one band, friction to 1--30 days and persistence to 31--60,
left every band above 60 days short and the 90+ share at 9.9\%, because one persistence parameter
cannot reproduce the rising roll rates of the \ac{CCMR} profile.
% results/summary/calibration.json (fit_error_points) and docs/DECISIONS.md D18.

The write-off rate and the flow check of Section~\ref{sec:data-validation} read the \ac{CCMR}
91--120 day band in two ways. The derivation treats the band as two ticks wide and lets half of
it reach 120 days each tick, an inflow of 0.88\% of accounts a tick. The flow check spreads the
band's 1.76\% over 30 days, 0.82\% a tick; on that reading the write-off rate would be 0.0660.
Write-off also applies from the tick after default, so a small share of accounts leaves the book
before it reaches 120 days.
% 0.88% = 1.76/2; 0.82% = 1.76 x 14/30 (ccmr_inflow_91plus_per_tick_of_accounts,
% results/summary/default_level.json); 0.0660 = 0.82/12.45. Write-off from default: agents.py.

\textbf{The earlier default rule.} Under the streak rule, now replaced, a household
defaulted after seven consecutive ticks in which it could not pay for food and rent or could not meet its debt
service, every household started current, and nothing was written off. Friction alone was
fitted to the 1--30 day band, at 0.09. The bands from 31 to 120 days then held 0.4 to 0.5\% of
credit-active households, and the 90+ share averaged 9.10\% after burn-in and reached 11.03\% at
the final tick. Baseline default was 7.28\%, the \ac{BNPL} effect without peer influence was
$-0.10 \pm 0.06$ points on the seeds of Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}, and bureau visibility
raised default by 0.18 points. Under that rule food and rent shortfalls drove default, and
\ac{BNPL} arrears did not count. The rule is kept in the code and reproduces those results
exactly.
% docs/ARREARS_RULE_2026-11.md; results/summary/pre_arrears_rule/calibration.json;
% simulation/tests/test_streak_rule_reproduces.py.

A still earlier version fitted the separation rate to the 90+ band without labour flows. That
fit chose 0.016, about 1.4 times the upper end of the \ac{QLFS} band, and because no member who
started out of work could find a job, the share of starting earners out of work rose from zero
to 39\% by the final tick. The model reached the 2017 90+ share only by producing a
recession that did not occur, so the fit was dropped.
% 39%: results/summary/defaulter_credit.json.

\subsection{Submodel 9: The Credit-Granting Gate}
\label{app:gate}

The lender uses a simplified residual-income test based on the Regulation 23A expense norms
\citep{sa_ncr_affordability_2015}. It grants credit when household income, less the expense
norm and visible existing obligations, covers the new instalment.
Income is survey take-home income, so statutory deductions are largely already out of it, and
the model does not reduce it during a spell of unemployment.
The norm table is indexed by gross income bands, so take-home income places some households in a
lower band with a lower expense norm, which makes the test more permissive. The resulting ceiling
varies with income, from 10.4\% of income at R900 per month to 83.2\% at R7{,}712 per month, and
two hand-computed checks from the regulation's Table~1 are asserted in the verification suite
(Table~\ref{tab:reg23a-norms}).

Regulation 23A applies to an individual consumer and is applied here to household income, because
\ac{NIDS} is a household survey. For a multi-earner household this makes the test more permissive
than the regulation would be in practice. The ceilings above are evaluated at per-capita quintile
bounds, so they understate what the test permits a large household at the same bound. Across the
population the applied ceiling runs from zero to 93.2\% of income, with a mean of 49.6\% in the
bottom quintile.

\subsection{Submodel 17: Activation Order}
\label{app:activation}

Households act in random asynchronous order, redrawn every tick. The peer channel of Submodel~11
is activation-order independent by construction, since it reads the reference-group adoption share
lagged one tick, so every household within a tick sees the same settled share regardless of when it
acts. \citet{comer2013activation} and \citet{alizadeh2015activation} find population behaviour sensitive to the activation regime, so the
model is also run under a fixed order, the same every tick. Default does not change detectably
($+0.17 \pm 0.11$ points; Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}).

The lender has no shared funding constraint, and platform limits apply per household.
Activation order therefore does not allocate a common credit pool. The alternative schedule
checks whether implementation details or the assignment of random draws affect the outcomes.

\subsection{Two Further Levers}
\label{app:levers}

Two further levers are run at the same two values of $\beta$ as the
scenarios of Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}, with twenty replicates and their own benchmark arm. The first is a cooling-off window that blocks want-driven
\ac{BNPL} purchases for $k^{\text{cool}}$ ticks after a want-driven purchase. One tick matches the
fourteen-day right of withdrawal under section 66A of the United Kingdom's Consumer Credit Act
1974. The \ac{FCA}'s rules treat that right as applying to regulated deferred payment credit
\citep{fca_ps26_1}, as its consultation states~\citep[Table~1, p.~17]{fca_cp25_23}, although it cancels an agreement and does not block the next one. The window
does not block borrowing for a shortfall. The second is a cap on the number of platforms a household
may owe, which no jurisdiction we found imposes and which is therefore hypothetical. Both are judged on cumulative \ac{BNPL} volume as well as
default (Tables~\ref{tab:cooloff} and~\ref{tab:cap}; Figure~\ref{fig:scenarios-appendix}
places them beside the scenarios).

The cap counts platforms on which the household owes a balance. It does not count agreements.
A household that owes the stated number of platforms or more is refused every new agreement,
including one on a platform it already uses, until a balance clears. A cap of one therefore
allows one agreement at a time, which is stricter than the single-platform arm of
Table~\ref{tab:stacking}, where a household may hold several agreements at once. Under a cap of
two or three a household below the cap may open further agreements on a platform it already
uses, so it can hold more agreements than the cap. A shortfall request that the cap refuses
passes in full to the traditional lender. Under a cap of one, new traditional lending is
R$0.13 \pm 0.21$ million higher than in the benchmark at $\beta = 0$ and R$0.24 \pm 0.19$
million at $\beta = 0.3$, neither detectable.
% lever_cap1_beta{0,0.3}_lending_diff (+ _se), results/summary/results_numbers.json.

The levers give fourteen contrasts with the benchmark, and none is detectable. The window
removes 4.2 to 11.7\% of \ac{BNPL} volume at $\beta = 0$ and 13.7 to 40.5\% at $\beta = 0.3$, and
changes default by $-0.18$ to $+0.05$ points. The caps of one to three remove 11.1 to 35.1\% of
volume at $\beta = 0$ and 6.8 to 40.3\% at $\beta = 0.3$, and change default by $-0.19$ to $-0.01$
points. Neither lever acts on the households that carry the \ac{BNPL} effect: the window does not
block borrowing for a shortfall, and a shortfall request that the cap refuses passes to the
traditional lender.

A diagnostic compares three definitions of the cap on the cap arms' own seeds, each paired with
an arm without a cap. Its numbers therefore differ from those of Table~\ref{tab:cap}, which
compares the caps with the benchmark of the cooling-off block. The definitions are the cap as
modelled, a cap on agreements, and a cap on platforms that still allows further agreements on
platforms already in use. At a cap of one the first two coincide. At $\beta = 0.3$ and a cap of
two, the cap as modelled changes default by $-0.02 \pm 0.10$ points and removes 13.9\% of
volume, and the agreements cap removes 14.1\% and changes default by $-0.05 \pm 0.12$. Paired
by seed, the cap as modelled less the agreements cap is $+0.03 \pm 0.11$ points at a cap of two
and $+0.24 \pm 0.12$ at a cap of three, at the two-standard-error threshold. The third
definition, at a cap of two, removes 2.5\% of volume and changes default by $+0.07 \pm 0.15$
points.
% results/summary/cap_definitions.md (notebooks/scripts/check_cap_definitions.py).
Neither lever was run under the alternative borrowing
rules of Table~\ref{tab:effect}, and the model has no measure of what a household loses when a
purchase or a shortfall loan is refused.

\subsection{Further Extensions}
\label{app:extensions}

Two extensions lie further from the thesis's results than those in the final paragraph of Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\textbf{Macroeconomic dynamics.} Submodel~16 holds the environment static to protect the
experimental control. A successor design could relax this and ask how \ac{BNPL} stress interacts with a
downturn. The injection effect and the macro effect would then have to
be separately identified, which requires a more elaborate design than a single counterfactual
injection.

\textbf{Competition among traditional lenders.} The traditional side is one non-adaptive lender.
\citet{hamill2023creditcard} suggest, in a model of the United Kingdom card market, that longer
interest-free promotions could raise indebtedness, so a competing, adapting traditional sector
could add a driver of indebtedness that this model holds fixed. In their model the share of
accounts with missed payments stays about constant, so the channel is balance growth.
