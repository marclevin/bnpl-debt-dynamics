\chapter{Model Design}
\label{ch:design}

This chapter specifies the model using the \ac{ODD} protocol \cite{grimm2006odd, grimm2020odd},
the standard for communicating agent-based models. The second update's \emph{purpose and
patterns} element is used as intended: the patterns that judge the model's realism are stated in
advance, before any result is presented, so that the validation criteria cannot be chosen after
the fact.

Every behavioural rule carries a citation, parameters with either a source or an explicit sweep,
and a validation hook. Table~\ref{tab:submodels} gives the full specification; the five submodels
that carry the argument are then discussed at length. Two rules are exceptions and are marked in
place. The borrowing-amount rule of Submodel~4 is adopted by assumption, since the literature
sweep found no work that fixes one, and its sensitivity analysis is therefore mandatory. The cap on concurrent facilities in Submodel~15 is hypothetical, as no
jurisdiction currently imposes such a cap, and it is labelled as such and kept apart from
the interventions that do exist. Appendix~\ref{app:rationale} records the alternatives that were
considered and rejected for each rule.

\section{Overview}
\label{sec:design-overview}

\subsection{Purpose and patterns}
\label{sec:purpose}

The model exists to answer the question set out in Section~\ref{sec:rqs}: how the availability of
multiple concurrent \ac{BNPL} facilities affects the trajectory of consumer financial distress
among \ac{LMI} South African households, and which platform-level interventions reduce
population-level default.

The design that answers it is a counterfactual experiment, not a historical fit. A household
population calibrated to 2017 conditions, when \ac{BNPL} was effectively absent from the South
African market, has a \ac{BNPL} lender class \emph{injected} into it, and the change in dynamics
is observed. Section~\ref{sec:injection} states the claim and its restrictions in full; the
consequence for this chapter is that no model output describes an actual year after 2017, and
results are read as directional and mechanistic.

Four patterns are registered in advance as the criteria for realism.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Complementarity, not substitution.} deHaan et al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl} find
    that \ac{BNPL} adoption is followed by rising credit-card interest and late fees. Enabling
    \ac{BNPL} in the model should therefore \emph{increase} arrears and interest burden on
    traditional debt. If traditional stress instead falls, the lender-choice submodel is wrong.
    Note that deHaan et al. measure fees and interest, not balances, so the model analogue is
    servicing stress and not debt stock.

    \item \textbf{Baseline arrears.} With \ac{BNPL} disabled, the population's arrears profile
    should approximate the 2017 first-quarter \ac{CCMR} figures for unsecured credit and credit
    facilities combined, reproduced in Table~\ref{tab:ccmr} \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017}.

    \item \textbf{Non-monotonic debt burden.} Hamill et al. \cite{hamill2023creditcard} report a
    non-monotonic relationship between income and debt-to-income ratio, with middle-income
    households carrying the highest balance-to-income values. A model producing debt-to-income
    rising monotonically in income would disagree with an independent model of the same
    phenomenon.

    \item \textbf{Savings exhaustion.} The share of agents reaching zero liquid savings should be
    broadly consistent with the TransUnion Consumer Pulse finding that 36 percent of South African
    consumers anticipated missing a bill payment \cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025}.
\end{enumerate}

Three qualifications belong here and not in a footnote, because each weakens a target that the
model is otherwise judged against.

First, the \ac{CCMR} counts \emph{accounts} and the model counts \emph{households}. A household
may hold several accounts, so an account-level arrears rate is not a household-level default rate.
Pattern~2 is an order-of-magnitude target, not a point target, and Section~\ref{sec:lim-units}
returns to it.

Second, the income-shock probability is \emph{fitted} so that baseline arrears reproduce
pattern~2. The baseline is therefore calibrated and not validated, and only the results obtained
after \ac{BNPL} is injected are genuine predictions.

Third, pattern~1 is weaker than it appears, and the weakness is of the model's own making. The
want-driven borrowing path of Submodel~3 was adopted partly because a purely shortfall-driven
\ac{BNPL} agent would treat the product as a pure substitute and could not reproduce the deHaan
et al. result at all. The direction of the effect is therefore not fully independent of the
design. What remains genuinely open is its magnitude, and whether the Regulation 23A gate of
Submodel~9 offsets it by crowding out traditional credit; a fall in traditional servicing stress
would still falsify the lender-choice rule. Pattern~1 is the sharpest test available, and it is
not the clean falsification test it would be had the trigger been chosen on other grounds. This
is the same circularity identified for the peer-influence coefficient in
Section~\ref{sec:peer-influence}, and it is handled the same way: by saying so, and by reporting
the result with the caveat attached.

Patterns 3 and 4 remain independent, since no parameter is tuned to either.

\subsection{Entities, state variables and scales}
\label{sec:entities}

The model contains four entity types.

\textbf{Household agents} are the only adaptive entities. Each is a real \ac{NIDS} Wave 5
household, carried into the model and not synthesised, as described in Chapter~\ref{ch:data}.
Static attributes comprise monthly income and its dominant source (wage, grant or other),
committed and discretionary expenditure, a liquid savings proxy, consolidated traditional debt,
income quintile, household size and composition, head demographics, and four financial-inclusion
flags matched in from \ac{FinScope}: banked status, formal credit access, savings product
holding, and informal finance. Dynamic state comprises the current cash balance, outstanding
traditional debt, outstanding \ac{BNPL} obligations, accrued arrears, a consecutive-distress
counter, and a default flag. One behavioural type is assigned: minimum-payer or scheduled-payer.

The \textbf{traditional lender} is a single aggregate entity holding all traditional debt. It does
not compete and does not adapt. Its only behaviour is a deterministic affordability gate.

The \textbf{credit bureau} is not an agent but a shared information structure. It records
traditional debt only. Its contents define what the traditional lender can observe, and its scope
is the model's central mechanism.

The \textbf{\ac{BNPL} platforms} are the injected entities. Four are active in the baseline,
matching the number of providers operating in South Africa. Each holds its own receivables,
applies its own light eligibility screen and per-order cap, and cannot observe any other
platform's exposure.

\textbf{Reference groups} are the model's social structure. Each household belongs to exactly one,
defined as the intersection of its income quintile and its province. This is the same cell already
used for the \ac{FinScope} donor match, so no new construct is introduced. Over the 5{,}000-agent
population it yields 45 groups, with a minimum of 23 agents, a median of 84 and a maximum of 353.
Groups are fixed at initialisation and static thereafter, consistent with static household
composition. Three groups in the Northern Cape fall between 23 and 31 agents and are therefore
noise-prone, which is noted wherever group-level results are reported.

The environment is homogeneous and exogenous, held at 2017 conditions. The time step is 14 days,
chosen to align with the pay-in-four \ac{BNPL} cadence and to divide cleanly into monthly survey
income. The horizon is 24 months, or 52 ticks, of which the first 12 ticks are burn-in and are
discarded. The population is 5{,}000 households, weighted-resampled from 3{,}250 unique \ac{NIDS}
source households. All monetary values are in 2017 Rands, with no inflation forwarding anywhere in
the model.

\subsection{Process overview and scheduling}
\label{sec:scheduling}

Scheduling is synchronous at the level of the tick. Within a tick, households act in random
asynchronous order reseeded each tick, then the lender processes applications, then state updates
and observation occur. Submodel~17 gives the reasoning behind random rather than uniform
activation, and the reason the peer channel is insensitive to it.

The order of operations inside a household's tick is itself a modelling decision, and it is
priority-ordered as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Income arrives, subject to the shock process.
    \item \textbf{Committed expenditure is paid first.} This is the minimum consumption floor and
    it outranks debt service.
    \item Scheduled debt service is attempted.
    \item Discretionary consumption is set.
    \item If cash is insufficient at step 2 or step 3, the household seeks credit.
    \item The household observes the \ac{BNPL} adoption share of its reference group \emph{as it
    stood at the end of the previous tick}, and its want-driven \ac{BNPL} propensity is set
    accordingly.
    \item Balances, arrears, the distress counter and default flags update, and group adoption
    shares are recomputed for the next tick.
\end{enumerate}

Two features of this ordering carry the model's mechanism. Committed expenditure precedes debt
service because Madeira's default condition is the failure to finance minimum consumption
\cite{madeira2018chile}, which is only coherent if consumption has priority; placing servicing
first would make default impossible by construction. Borrowing occurs last because in D'Orazio and
Giulioni \cite{dorazio2017micro} credit answers a realised shortfall and not an anticipated one.
Placing it at step 5 is what permits a household to borrow in order to service existing debt,
which is the first of the two self-reinforcing spirals the thesis exists to observe. The second is
social and enters at step 6. The one-tick lag on the group adoption share is not cosmetic: reading
a share that is still being written within the same tick would make the synchronous update
ill-defined and would introduce an artificial dependence on activation order.

\textbf{Emergence.} Nothing in the rules specifies a default rate, a stacking depth, or a tipping
point. The quantities the research questions ask about are precisely the quantities not imposed.
Two distinct positive feedback loops can generate that behaviour, and the model is constructed so
they can be separated. The \emph{financial} loop runs through the balance sheet: a household short
of cash borrows to meet its obligations, which raises future servicing, which deepens the
shortfall. The \emph{social} loop runs through the reference group: a household that adopts
\ac{BNPL} raises its group's adoption share, which raises its neighbours' propensity to adopt.
Setting the peer coefficient to zero disables the second loop while leaving the first intact, so
the contribution of each can be measured.

\textbf{Sensing.} Households sense their own balance sheet exactly, and sense one aggregate
property of their reference group: the share of its members currently holding a \ac{BNPL} balance,
lagged one tick. They do not observe any individual neighbour, nor any neighbour's income, debt or
distress. The lender senses the household's declared gross income, its own outstanding loans, and
all other traditional debt through the bureau. It does not sense \ac{BNPL} obligations or informal
debt. That second asymmetry is the model's central mechanism and is the subject of Submodel~10.
The contrast is worth stating plainly: households can see \ac{BNPL} spreading among their peers,
and the regulated lender cannot see it at all.

\section{Details}
\label{sec:details}

\subsection{Initialisation and input data}
\label{sec:initialisation}

The population is loaded from the static data layer of Chapter~\ref{ch:data}, in which 5{,}000
households were resampled with probability proportional to survey weight from 10{,}841 valid
\ac{NIDS} Wave 5 households, then enriched with \ac{FinScope} financial-inclusion flags by a
cell-donor match on per-capita income quintile crossed with province.

Opening balance sheets are the survey values. Monthly servicing on opening traditional debt is
constructed, since neither survey records a repayment amount: the balance is
amortised at the \ac{NCA} statutory maximum prescribed rate for the relevant sub-sector, evaluated
at the 2017 repurchase rate of 7.00 percent, and then capped by the affordability rule of
Submodel~9. Opening \ac{BNPL} balances are zero in every run, which is the injection design.

The model uses no external time series. The macro environment is static and exogenous at 2017
conditions, so all input is the initialised population and the parameter set.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 9: the credit-granting gate}
\label{sec:gate}

The lender applies a residual-income test and not a debt-service-to-income cap. This is not a
modelling preference. Regulation 23A of the Affordability Assessment Regulations
\cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015} prescribes no debt-service-to-income ratio; it prescribes a table
of minimum necessary monthly living expenses by gross income band, against which affordability is
assessed on what remains. Credit is granted only where gross income, less statutory deductions,
less Regulation 23A necessary expenses, less visible existing obligations, covers the new
instalment.

The resulting ceiling varies with income, from 10.4 percent of income at R900 per month to 83.2
percent at R7{,}712 per month. A flat cap would be both unsourced and, at the bottom of the
distribution, wildly over-permissive: a 65 percent rule would permit a household earning R900 per
month to service R585 per month. Regulation 23A applies to an individual consumer and is applied
here to household income, which is a stated simplification (Section~\ref{sec:lim-reg23a}).

\subsubsection*{Submodel 10: information asymmetry}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

The bureau records traditional debt only. The lender observes declared gross income, its own
loans, and other traditional debt. It does not observe \ac{BNPL} obligations, which fall outside
the \ac{NCA} and carry no bureau-reporting obligation \cite{nortonrose_bnpl_sa}; TransUnion
confirms the gap from inside the bureau system by listing \ac{BNPL} history among the information
absent from the standard South African credit report \cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025}.

Nor does it observe informal debt. South African informal finance runs substantially through
stokvels, burial societies and similar rotating savings arrangements, along with mashonisa
lending, none of which reports to a credit bureau \cite{finscope2019}. Informal obligations
therefore enter the model only as static balance-sheet state carried from \ac{NIDS}.

The consequence is that the Regulation 23A test is computed over an incomplete liability set. The
lender is neither unlawful nor careless: it complies with the regulation using the record the
regulation gives it, and that is the point. Bureau visibility of \ac{BNPL} is a boolean parameter,
disabled by default, and is also the first intervention lever of Submodel~15. Running with it
enabled should recover affordability outcomes close to the no-\ac{BNPL} baseline, so the gap
between the two runs measures the cost of the regulatory reporting gap directly.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 11: peer influence}
\label{sec:peer-influence}

Each household belongs to one reference group, defined as its income quintile crossed with its
province. Let $s_g(t-1)$ denote the share of households in group $g$ holding a non-zero \ac{BNPL}
balance at the end of the previous tick. The want-driven \ac{BNPL} propensity of household $i$ is

\begin{equation}
    q_i(t) = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(q_{\text{base}} + \beta \, s_{g(i)}(t-1),\; 0,\; 1\right).
    \label{eq:peer}
\end{equation}

A strictly positive $q_{\text{base}}$ is structurally required. Every household begins with no
\ac{BNPL} balance, so $s_g(0)=0$ everywhere, and under a purely multiplicative rule adoption could
never begin. The two coefficients therefore play the roles that $p$ and $q$ play in Bass
diffusion: $q_{\text{base}}$ governs spontaneous adoption and $\beta$ governs imitation.

Three literatures license this submodel. Ackert et al. \cite{ackert2025bnpl} supply the domain
claim, that \ac{BNPL} uptake specifically is norm-driven, since their participants chose \ac{BNPL}
over a credit-card loan for the same purchase and expected their social networks to approve.
Cardaci \cite{cardaci2018inequality} supplies the methodological precedent, since peer effects and
expenditure cascades are central to the household-credit agent-based model this thesis takes as
its principal anchor. Granovetter \cite{granovetter1978threshold} supplies the mechanism, being
the canonical account of how share-dependent adoption with heterogeneous thresholds yields
discontinuous aggregate outcomes.

Neither $q_{\text{base}}$ nor $\beta$ can be calibrated, since no South African data fixes either.
This raises a methodological difficulty that must be met directly. The submodel exists partly
because the second research question asks for a non-linear response, and with wholly independent
households the population default rate is close to a smooth function of \ac{BNPL} access,
thresholds being products of feedback. Introducing a peer channel and then reporting non-linearity
as a finding would be circular.

The resolution is to treat $\beta = 0$ as a control arm and not as an uninteresting corner of the
parameter space. At $\beta = 0$ the model reduces exactly to independent agents, and results for
both the first and second research questions are reported as a surface over the swept parameter
and $\beta$ jointly, with the $\beta = 0$ row always shown. The claim available at the end is then
not that a threshold exists, but that population default responds non-linearly to \ac{BNPL} access
\emph{only where social transmission is present}, which is both stronger and falsifiable. The same
device separates the two feedback loops for the first research question, since $\beta = 0$
isolates the financial loop from the social one.

Whether a \emph{sharp} threshold emerges is left as an empirical question about the model rather
than an assumption built into it. Linear coupling of this kind produces diffusion; whether
diffusion combined with the affordability gate of Submodel~9 and the arrears accrual of
Submodel~6 produces a sharp transition is not presupposed. Should the linear form yield only a
smooth response, Granovetter-style heterogeneous individual thresholds are registered here, in
advance, as the structural robustness check.

Finally, the submodel is inert in the baseline. With \ac{BNPL} disabled, $s_g$ is identically
zero, so the calibration of baseline arrears against the 2017 \ac{CCMR} is untouched by anything
in this submodel.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 13: stacking}
\label{sec:stacking}

A household may hold concurrent facilities across several platforms, with no aggregate limit.
Platforms cannot observe one another, and this is a consequence of Submodel~10 and not a fresh
assumption: with no bureau-reporting obligation there exists no shared infrastructure through
which one platform could see another's exposure. Each platform therefore screens against a
liability set it cannot fully see, exactly as the traditional lender does.

Four platforms are active in the baseline, matching the South African market, and the number is
swept from one to six. A single platform serves as the control that isolates single-platform
accumulation from genuine cross-platform stacking. Stacking depth is emergent and not imposed, and
at a realistic access rate the model should approximate the \ac{CFPB} shares: roughly 63 percent
of adopters holding simultaneous loans and 32 percent holding across different firms
\cite{cfpb2025bnpl}. Those are United States figures, so the comparison is an order-of-magnitude
check and not a point target.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 15: intervention levers}
\label{sec:levers}

Four levers answer the third research question, and three correspond to instruments that exist in
the world.

The first is \textbf{bureau visibility}, a boolean that closes the South African reporting gap of
Submodel~10. The difference between the two settings measures the cost of the reporting exemption
directly.

The second is a \textbf{mandatory affordability check}, which applies the Regulation 23A test to
\ac{BNPL} as well as to traditional credit, expressing the \ac{FCA}'s requirement of proportionate
affordability checks \cite{fca_ps26_1} in South African statutory terms. That requirement descends
from the Woolard Review \cite{woolard2021}, whose recommendations began the process of bringing
\ac{BNPL} inside the United Kingdom regulatory perimeter, and it is precisely the assessment South
African providers are not obliged to perform.

The third is a \textbf{cool-off period}, set at one tick in the baseline because section 66A of
the United Kingdom Consumer Credit Act 1974 gives a 14-day right of withdrawal, which again falls
exactly on a tick boundary. After a want-driven purchase a household cannot initiate another for
the cool-off duration, while the shortfall-driven path remains open, since a cool-off on emergency
borrowing would be a different instrument answering a different question.

The fourth is a \textbf{cap on concurrent facilities}, and this one is genuinely hypothetical: no
jurisdiction currently imposes such a cap, and it is labelled as such and not presented alongside
the three real instruments.

The third research question asks whether cool-off interventions change outcomes or whether agents
simply defer, and the model answers it directly. The comparison is \emph{cumulative \ac{BNPL}
volume over the full horizon} with and without the cool-off, not the timing of purchases. If total
volume is unchanged and only its distribution over time shifts, agents defer. If cumulative volume
falls, they desist.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 17: activation order}
\label{sec:activation}

One property of the design deserves emphasis, because it is a payoff of an earlier decision. The
peer channel of Submodel~11 is \emph{activation-order independent by construction}, since it reads
the reference-group adoption share lagged one tick. Every household within a tick therefore sees
the same settled share regardless of when it acts, and the model's principal interaction does not
inherit the sensitivity that Comer and Loerch \cite{comer2013activation} and Alizadeh and
Cioffi-Revilla \cite{alizadeh2015activation} warn about. Had the share been read live within the
tick, results would have depended on activation order and the two decisions would have become
entangled. The model is nonetheless re-run under uniform and under fully synchronous activation,
and any material movement is reported as activation-order sensitivity; asserting robustness
without testing it would not be defensible in a model containing a positive feedback loop.
