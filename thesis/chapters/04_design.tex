\chapter{Model Design}
\label{ch:design}

This chapter specifies the model using the \ac{ODD} protocol \cite{grimm2006odd, grimm2020odd}. The
second update's \emph{purpose and patterns} element is used as intended: the patterns that judge
the model's realism are stated before any result is presented, so that the validation criteria
cannot be chosen after the fact. Every behavioural rule carries a citation, parameters carry either
a source or an explicit sweep, and each rule carries a validation hook.
Table~\ref{tab:submodels} gives the full specification, and the submodels that carry the argument
are discussed after it. Appendix~\ref{app:rationale} records the alternatives considered and
rejected for each rule.

\section{Overview}
\label{sec:design-overview}

\subsection{Purpose and patterns}
\label{sec:purpose}

The model exists to answer the question set out in Section~\ref{sec:rqs}: how the availability of
multiple concurrent \ac{BNPL} facilities affects the trajectory of consumer financial distress
among \ac{LMI} South African households, and which platform-level interventions reduce
population-level default. The design that answers it is the counterfactual injection of
Section~\ref{sec:injection}, so no model output describes an actual year after 2017 and results are
read as directional and mechanistic.

Four patterns are registered in advance as the criteria for realism.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Complementarity, not substitution.} deHaan et al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl} find that
    \ac{BNPL} adoption is followed by rising credit-card interest and late fees. Enabling \ac{BNPL}
    in the model should therefore increase arrears and interest burden on traditional debt, and if
    traditional stress instead falls, the lender-choice submodel is wrong. deHaan et al. measure
    fees and interest rather than balances, so the model analogue is servicing stress and not debt
    stock.

    \item \textbf{Baseline arrears.} With \ac{BNPL} disabled, the population's arrears profile
    should approximate the 2017 first-quarter \ac{CCMR} figures for unsecured credit and credit
    facilities combined, reproduced in Table~\ref{tab:ccmr} \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017}.

    \item \textbf{Non-monotonic debt burden.} Hamill et al. \cite{hamill2023creditcard} report a
    non-monotonic relationship between income and debt-to-income ratio, with middle-income
    households carrying the highest balance-to-income values. A model producing debt-to-income
    rising monotonically in income would disagree with an independent model of the same phenomenon.

    \item \textbf{Savings exhaustion.} The share of agents reaching zero liquid savings should be
    broadly consistent with the TransUnion Consumer Pulse finding that 36\% of South African
    consumers anticipated missing a bill payment \cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025}.
\end{enumerate}

Three of these targets are weaker than they appear. The \ac{CCMR} counts accounts and the model
counts households, so pattern~2 is an order-of-magnitude target and not a point target
(Section~\ref{sec:lim-units}). The income-shock probability is fitted so that baseline arrears
reproduce pattern~2, which makes the baseline calibrated rather than validated
(Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}). Pattern~1 is not fully independent of the design, for reasons
given in Section~\ref{sec:lim-pattern1}, though its magnitude remains open and a fall in
traditional servicing stress would still falsify the lender-choice rule. Patterns 3 and 4 are
independent, since no parameter is tuned to either.

\subsection{Entities, state variables and scales}
\label{sec:entities}

The model contains four entity types.

\textbf{Household agents} are the only adaptive entities. Each is a real \ac{NIDS} Wave 5 household
carried into the model, with the static attributes of Table~\ref{tab:agent-mapping}. Dynamic state
comprises the cash balance, outstanding traditional debt, outstanding \ac{BNPL} obligations,
accrued arrears, a consecutive-distress counter and a default flag, and one behavioural type
assigned at initialisation: minimum-payer or scheduled-payer.

The \textbf{traditional lender} is a single aggregate entity holding all traditional debt. It does
not compete and does not adapt, and its only behaviour is a deterministic affordability gate. The
\textbf{credit bureau} is not an agent but a shared information structure recording traditional
debt only, whose contents define what the traditional lender can observe. The \textbf{\ac{BNPL}
platforms} are the injected entities, four active in the baseline to match the South African
market. Each holds its own receivables, applies its own light eligibility screen and per-order cap,
and cannot observe any other platform's exposure.

\textbf{Reference groups} are the model's social structure. Each household belongs to one, defined
as its income quintile crossed with its province, which is the cell already used for the
\ac{FinScope} donor match. Over 5{,}000 agents this yields 45 groups, with a minimum of 23 agents,
a median of 84 and a maximum of 353. Three groups in the Northern Cape fall between 23 and 31
agents and are therefore noise-prone, which is noted wherever group-level results are reported.

The environment is homogeneous and exogenous at 2017 conditions. The time step is 14 days, which
aligns with the pay-in-four \ac{BNPL} cadence and divides cleanly into monthly survey income. The
horizon is 52 ticks, of which the first 12 are burn-in and are discarded.

\subsection{Process overview and scheduling}
\label{sec:scheduling}

Scheduling is synchronous at the level of the tick. Within a tick, households act in random
asynchronous order reseeded each tick, then the lender processes applications, then state updates
and observation occur. The order of operations inside a household's tick is priority-ordered as
follows.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Income arrives, subject to the shock process.
    \item Committed expenditure is paid first. This is the minimum consumption floor and it
    outranks debt service.
    \item Scheduled debt service is attempted.
    \item Discretionary consumption is set.
    \item If cash is insufficient at step 2 or step 3, the household seeks credit.
    \item The household observes the \ac{BNPL} adoption share of its reference group \emph{as it
    stood at the end of the previous tick}, and its want-driven \ac{BNPL} propensity is set
    accordingly.
    \item Balances, arrears, the distress counter and default flags update, and group adoption
    shares are recomputed for the next tick.
\end{enumerate}

Two features of this ordering carry the model's mechanism. Committed expenditure precedes debt
service because Madeira's default condition is the failure to finance minimum consumption
\cite{madeira2018chile}, which is only coherent if consumption has priority; placing servicing
first would make default impossible by construction. Borrowing occurs last because in D'Orazio and
Giulioni \cite{dorazio2017micro} credit answers a realised shortfall and not an anticipated one,
and placing it at step 5 permits a household to borrow in order to service existing debt, which is
the first of the two self-reinforcing spirals the thesis exists to observe. The second is social
and enters at step 6, where the one-tick lag on the group adoption share keeps the synchronous
update well defined and removes any dependence on activation order.

\textbf{Emergence.} Nothing in the rules specifies a default rate, a stacking depth, or a tipping
point, so the quantities the research questions ask about are the quantities not imposed. Two
distinct positive feedback loops can generate that behaviour, and the model is constructed so they
can be separated. The \emph{financial} loop runs through the balance sheet: a household short of
cash borrows to meet its obligations, which raises future servicing, which deepens the shortfall.
The \emph{social} loop runs through the reference group: a household that adopts \ac{BNPL} raises
its group's adoption share, which raises its neighbours' propensity to adopt. Setting the peer
coefficient to zero disables the second loop while leaving the first intact, so the contribution of
each can be measured.

\textbf{Sensing.} Households sense their own balance sheet exactly, and sense one aggregate
property of their reference group: the share of its members currently holding a \ac{BNPL} balance,
lagged one tick. They do not observe any individual neighbour, nor any neighbour's income, debt or
distress. The lender senses the household's declared gross income, its own outstanding loans, and
all other traditional debt through the bureau, but not \ac{BNPL} obligations or informal debt. That
second asymmetry is the model's central mechanism and is the subject of Submodel~10.

\section{Details}
\label{sec:details}

\subsection{Initialisation and input data}
\label{sec:initialisation}

The population is loaded from the static data layer of Chapter~\ref{ch:data}. Opening balance
sheets are the survey values, and monthly servicing on opening traditional debt is constructed as
described in Section~\ref{sec:servicing}. Opening \ac{BNPL} balances are zero in every run, which
is the injection design. The model uses no external time series: the macro environment is static
and exogenous at 2017 conditions, so all input is the initialised population and the parameter set.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 9: the credit-granting gate}
\label{sec:gate}

The lender applies a residual-income test and not a debt-service-to-income cap, because Regulation
23A \cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015} prescribes no such ratio. Credit is granted only where gross
income, less statutory deductions, less the Regulation 23A necessary expenses for the applicant's
income band, less visible existing obligations, covers the new instalment. The resulting ceiling
varies with income, from 10.4\% of income at R900 per month to 83.2\% at R7{,}712 per month.
Regulation 23A applies to an individual consumer and is applied here to household income, which is
a stated simplification (Section~\ref{sec:lim-reg23a}).

\subsubsection*{Submodel 10: information asymmetry}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

The bureau records traditional debt only. The lender observes declared gross income, its own loans,
and other traditional debt, but not \ac{BNPL} obligations, which fall outside the \ac{NCA} and
carry no bureau-reporting obligation \cite{nortonrose_bnpl_sa, transunion_cps_sa_2025}. Nor does it
observe informal debt: South African informal finance runs substantially through stokvels, burial
societies and mashonisa lending, none of which reports to a credit bureau \cite{finscope2019}, so
informal obligations enter the model only as static balance-sheet state carried from \ac{NIDS}.

The Regulation 23A test is consequently computed over an incomplete liability set. The lender is
neither unlawful nor careless, since it complies with the regulation using the record the
regulation gives it, and that is the point. Bureau visibility of \ac{BNPL} is a boolean parameter,
disabled by default, and is also the first intervention lever of Submodel~15. Running with it
enabled should recover affordability outcomes close to the no-\ac{BNPL} baseline, so the gap
between the two runs measures the cost of the reporting gap directly.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 11: peer influence}
\label{sec:peer-influence}

Each household belongs to one reference group, defined as its income quintile crossed with its
province. Let $s_g(t-1)$ denote the share of households in group $g$ holding a non-zero \ac{BNPL}
balance at the end of the previous tick. The want-driven \ac{BNPL} propensity of household $i$ is

\begin{equation}
    q_i(t) = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(q_{\text{base}} + \beta \, s_{g(i)}(t-1),\; 0,\; 1\right).
    \label{eq:peer}
\end{equation}

A strictly positive $q_{\text{base}}$ is structurally required, since every household begins with
no \ac{BNPL} balance and under a purely multiplicative rule adoption could never begin, so the two
coefficients play the roles that $p$ and $q$ play in Bass diffusion. Three literatures license the
submodel: Ackert et al. \cite{ackert2025bnpl} supply the domain claim that \ac{BNPL} uptake is
norm-driven, Cardaci \cite{cardaci2018inequality} the methodological precedent, and Granovetter
\cite{granovetter1978threshold} the canonical account of how share-dependent adoption with
heterogeneous thresholds yields discontinuous aggregate outcomes.

Neither coefficient can be calibrated from any South African source, and the second research
question asks for a non-linearity that a peer channel is liable to produce, so $\beta = 0$ is
treated as a control arm rather than a corner of the parameter space. At $\beta = 0$ the model
reduces exactly to independent agents, and results for the first and second research questions are
reported as a surface over the swept parameter and $\beta$ jointly, with the $\beta = 0$ row always
shown. The claim available at the end is then not that a threshold exists, but that population
default responds non-linearly to \ac{BNPL} access \emph{only where social transmission is present},
which is both stronger and falsifiable. The same device separates the financial feedback loop from
the social one. Section~\ref{sec:lim-uncalibrated} states what the control arm does not solve.

Should the linear coupling of Equation~\ref{eq:peer} yield only a smooth response,
Granovetter-style heterogeneous individual thresholds are registered here, in advance, as the
structural robustness check. The submodel is inert in the baseline, since with \ac{BNPL} disabled
$s_g$ is identically zero and the calibration of baseline arrears is untouched by anything in it.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 13: stacking}
\label{sec:stacking}

A household may hold concurrent facilities across several platforms, with no aggregate limit.
Platforms cannot observe one another, which follows from Submodel~10 rather than being a fresh
assumption: with no bureau-reporting obligation there is no shared infrastructure through which one
platform could see another's exposure, so each screens against a liability set it cannot fully see,
exactly as the traditional lender does.

The platform count is swept from one to six, with the single-platform arm isolating single-platform
accumulation from genuine cross-platform stacking. Stacking depth is emergent, and at a realistic
access rate the model should approximate the \ac{CFPB} shares of roughly 63\% of adopters holding
simultaneous loans and 32\% holding across different firms \cite{cfpb2025bnpl}. Those are United
States figures, so the comparison is an order-of-magnitude check.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 15: intervention levers}
\label{sec:levers}

Four levers answer the third research question, and three correspond to instruments that exist in
other jurisdictions.

The first is \textbf{bureau visibility}, a boolean that closes the reporting gap of Submodel~10.
The difference between the two settings measures the cost of the reporting exemption directly.

The second is a \textbf{mandatory affordability check}, applying the Regulation 23A test to
\ac{BNPL} as well as to traditional credit. This expresses the \ac{FCA}'s requirement of
proportionate affordability checks \cite{fca_ps26_1}, which descends from the Woolard Review
\cite{woolard2021}, in South African statutory terms.

The third is a \textbf{cool-off period}, set at one tick because section 66A of the United Kingdom
Consumer Credit Act 1974 gives a 14-day right of withdrawal, which falls exactly on a tick
boundary. After a want-driven purchase a household cannot initiate another for the cool-off
duration, while the shortfall-driven path remains open, since a cool-off on emergency borrowing
would answer a different question. The third research question asks whether such interventions
change outcomes or whether agents simply defer, and the comparison that answers it is cumulative
\ac{BNPL} volume over the full horizon with and without the lever. Unchanged volume with a shifted
time distribution means agents defer; lower cumulative volume means they desist.

The fourth is a \textbf{cap on concurrent facilities}. No jurisdiction currently imposes such a
cap, so this lever is hypothetical and is labelled as such wherever it is reported.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 17: activation order}
\label{sec:activation}

Households act in random asynchronous order, reseeded every tick. The peer channel of Submodel~11
is activation-order independent by construction, since it reads the reference-group adoption share
lagged one tick, so every household within a tick sees the same settled share regardless of when it
acts. The model is nonetheless re-run under uniform and under fully synchronous activation, and any
material movement is reported as activation-order sensitivity, since Comer and Loerch
\cite{comer2013activation} and Alizadeh and Cioffi-Revilla \cite{alizadeh2015activation} both find
emergent population behaviour to be sensitive to the activation regime.
