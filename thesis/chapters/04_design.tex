\section{Model Design and Implementation}
\label{ch:model}

This section specifies the ABM following the \ac{ODD} protocol \cite{grimm2006odd, grimm2020odd} and outlines its computational implementation. Adhering to the protocol's \emph{purpose and patterns} element. Tables~\ref{tab:submodels} and~\ref{tab:design-concepts} provide the comprehensive specification, while the submodels driving the primary causal mechanisms are detailed in the text. Appendices~\ref{app:rationale} and~\ref{app:supplementary} document rejected structural alternatives and supplementary technical details.

\subsection{Purpose and Patterns}
\label{sec:purpose}

To evaluate the research questions outlined in Section~\ref{sec:rqs}, the simulation employs a \textbf{counterfactual injection} methodology: every household agent is initialised with a \ac{BNPL} balance of zero, and the macroeconomic environment is held static at 2017 conditions. The baseline model is calibrated with \ac{BNPL} disabled. \ac{BNPL} is subsequently introduced into the environment with all other parameters held constant. Consequently, the model outputs do not forecast a specific chronological year post-2017; rather, they provide directional and mechanistic insights into the isolated impact of \ac{BNPL} introduction.

Table~\ref{tab:patterns} establishes four empirical patterns as predefined validation criteria for the model's realism. Pattern 2, the 2017 \ac{CCMR} arrears profile, is the primary calibration target, as it is strictly South African and vintage-matched to the underlying population data. The remaining three patterns rely on international evidence or subsequent surveys and serve as secondary structural checks.

\subsection{Entities, Scales and Scheduling}
\label{sec:entities}

The model consists of five core entities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Household agents} are the adaptive entities. Each maps to an empirical NIDS Wave 5 household (Table~\ref{tab:agent-mapping}) and tracks its dynamic state: cash reserves, traditional debt, \ac{BNPL} obligations, arrears, financial distress, default status, and a fixed repayment archetype (minimum-payer or scheduled-payer).
    \item \textbf{The traditional lender} is a static aggregate entity holding all traditional debt. It acts strictly as a deterministic affordability gate.
    \item \textbf{The credit bureau} is a shared registry of traditional debt that defines the traditional lender's observational scope.
    \item \textbf{\ac{BNPL} platforms} are the injected entities. Four platforms operate in the baseline to mirror the South African market structure. Each maintains isolated receivables, applies automated screening, sets per-order caps, and operates with zero visibility into competitor exposures.
    \item \textbf{Reference groups} define the social topology, each household belongs to a single reference group, defined by its income quintile and province.
\end{itemize}

The environment is static and fixed at 2017 macroeconomic conditions. Time advances in 14-day ticks. This interval matches the standard ``pay-in-four'' \ac{BNPL} cadence and cleanly subdivides monthly survey income. The simulation runs for 52 ticks, discarding the first 12 as burn-in to clear initialisation artefacts.

Scheduling is synchronous at the tick level. Within each tick, households act in a randomised, asynchronous order that reseeds every step. After household actions, the lender processes credit applications, and global states update. A household's intra-tick execution follows this strict sequence:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Receive income, subject to exogenous shocks.
    \item Deduct committed expenditures.
    \item Execute scheduled debt servicing.
    \item Allocate discretionary consumption.
    \item Generate credit applications if liquidity fails at step 2 or 3.
    \item Update want-driven consumption propensity by observing the reference group's \ac{BNPL} adoption rate from the previous tick.
    \item Update balances, arrears, distress counters, and default flags.
\end{enumerate}

This execution order isolates two mechanisms. First, prioritising committed expenditure over debt servicing ensures the default condition (defined as the failure to finance minimum consumption \cite{madeira2018chile}) is mathematically possible. Second, positioning borrowing late in the sequence ensures credit directly addresses a realised liquidity shortfall \cite{dorazio2017micro}. This allows agents to borrow to service existing debt, which drives the primary financial feedback loop. Finally, the one-tick lag on social observation stabilises the norm-driven feedback loop, keeping it independent of the asynchronous activation order.

\textbf{Emergence.} The model does not exogenously impose default rates, stacking depths, or systemic tipping points. Instead, these metrics emerge dynamically from two distinct feedback loops. The \emph{financial} loop is driven by liquidity-constrained households borrowing to satisfy existing obligations, which inflates future servicing requirements. The \emph{social} loop is driven by aggregate adoption rates positively influencing individual adoption propensities within peer networks. Setting the peer-influence coefficient to zero neutralises the social loop while preserving the financial loop, permitting isolated causal analysis.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 10: Information Asymmetry}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

Agents possess perfect internal knowledge of their balance sheets and observe the aggregate \ac{BNPL} adoption rate of their reference group with a one-tick lag. In contrast, the traditional lender acts on restricted information: it observes an applicant's declared gross income, its own institutional exposure, and external traditional debt listed on the credit bureau. Crucially, it cannot observe \ac{BNPL} obligations (which currently operate outside South African statutory bureau reporting requirements \cite{nortonrose_bnpl_sa, transunion_cps_sa_2025}) nor informal debt structures such as stokvels or mashonisa loans \cite{finscope2019}.

Consequently, the statutory Regulation 23A affordability test evaluates an incomplete liability profile. This dynamic reflects a systemic regulatory gap rather than non-compliant lender behaviour. Bureau visibility is implemented as a boolean parameter (default disabled) and serves as the primary policy intervention lever. Activating visibility allows the affordability gate to assess the complete liability stack. The divergence in outcomes between the visible and blind states provides a direct quantitative measure of the systemic risk introduced by the reporting exemption.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 11: Peer Influence}
\label{sec:peer-influence}

Let $s_g(t-1)$ denote the proportion of households within reference group $g$ maintaining a non-zero \ac{BNPL} balance at the conclusion of the previous tick. The want-driven \ac{BNPL} consumption propensity for household $i$ is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    q_i(t) = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(q_{\text{base}} + \beta \, s_{g(i)}(t-1),\; 0,\; 1\right).
    \label{eq:peer}
\end{equation}

A strictly positive base propensity ($q_{\text{base}}$) is structurally necessary to initiate adoption. Under a purely multiplicative specification, an initial state of zero balances would preclude adoption permanently. The coefficients function analogously to the innovation and imitation parameters in Bass diffusion models. This formulation is grounded in empirical observations that \ac{BNPL} consumption is heavily norm-driven \cite{ackert2025bnpl}, aligns with established macroeconomic ABM methodologies \cite{cardaci2018inequality}, and maps directly to Granovetter's threshold models of collective behaviour \cite{granovetter1978threshold}.

Because neither coefficient can be precisely calibrated using existing South African data, the $\beta = 0$ condition acts as an independent control arm rather than a standard parameter bound. Presenting this null-social row across all results isolates the mechanical financial feedback loop from the sociological transmission mechanism.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 13: Stacking}
\label{sec:stacking}

Agents can maintain concurrent credit facilities across multiple \ac{BNPL} platforms without an aggregate limit. The inability of platforms to observe one another is a direct, endogenous consequence of the information asymmetry established in Submodel~10. Lacking a centralised reporting infrastructure, each platform screens applicants against an incomplete liability set. The total number of active platforms is swept from one to six, with the single-platform environment serving as a control to differentiate internal rolling accumulation from genuine cross-firm stacking. Systemic stacking depth emerges endogenously and is benchmarked against \ac{CFPB} observations, which indicate that approximately 63\% of borrowers hold simultaneous loans and 32\% hold them across multiple firms \cite{cfpb2025bnpl}. 

\subsubsection*{Submodel 15: Intervention Levers}
\label{sec:levers}

The simulation incorporates four distinct policy intervention levers to address the study's third research question:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bureau visibility} eliminates the reporting gap established in Submodel 10.
    \item \textbf{Mandatory affordability checks} enforce the rigorous Regulation 23A assessment on \ac{BNPL} transactions, operationalising international regulatory recommendations \cite{fca_ps26_1, woolard2021} within a South African context.
    \item \textbf{Cooling-off periods} impose a mandatory one-tick (14-day) moratorium on subsequent want-driven \ac{BNPL} originations following a transaction, targeting impulsive consumption while leaving emergency borrowing unhindered.
    \item \textbf{Concurrent facility caps} physically restrict cross-platform stacking. This lever acts as a hypothetical test, as it currently lacks a direct jurisdictional precedent.
\end{itemize}
The structural efficacy of these interventions is evaluated by analysing cumulative \ac{BNPL} volume over the simulation horizon. An intervention that merely shifts transaction timing (deferral) will exhibit stable cumulative volume, whereas one that structurally alters behaviour (desistance) will demonstrably depress cumulative volume.

\subsection{Implementation and Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:implementation}

The ABM is implemented programmatically in Python using the Mesa framework, compartmentalised within the \texttt{simulation/} directory. The architecture maps one class to each \ac{ODD} entity, governed by a central model class managing the scheduler and data aggregation. The synthetic population is instantiated directly from the parquet files generated during the data construction phase (Section~\ref{ch:population}).

Model verification (ensuring the code faithfully executes the conceptual specification) is distinct from empirical validation and relies on a strict automated test suite. This suite asserts core invariances: balance-sheet conservation, chronological arrears ageing across \ac{CCMR} cohorts, faithful execution of Regulation 23A mathematical worked examples, precise tick-alignment of Pay-in-4 amortisation schedules, and statutory caps on late fees. The architecture is further verified against degenerate edge cases. Disabling \ac{BNPL} perfectly reconstructs the baseline model, setting $\beta = 0$ isolates the independent-agent paradigm, and restricting the environment to a single platform mathematically forces cross-firm stacking to zero. 

Two parameters are empirically fitted: the probability of an income shock and the payment-inattention rate. Each is calibrated against a single \ac{CCMR} arrears band, leaving the remaining bands entirely free to serve as out-of-sample structural checks. All other parameters are empirically sourced or systematically swept across predefined spaces. Executions are strictly seeded, replicated, and aggregated, with statistical dispersion transparently reported. The parameter sweep grid and noise floors are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}, with the complete parameter register documented in Appendix~\ref{app:specification}.
