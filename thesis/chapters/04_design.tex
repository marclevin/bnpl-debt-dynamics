\section{Model Design and Implementation}
\label{ch:model}

This section specifies the agent-based model following the \ac{ODD} (Overview, Design concepts, and Details) protocol \cite{grimm2006odd, grimm2020odd} and outlines its computational implementation. Adhering to the protocol's updated \emph{purpose and patterns} element, the empirical criteria used to evaluate the model's realism are established prior to the presentation of any simulation results. Tables~\ref{tab:submodels} and~\ref{tab:design-concepts} provide the comprehensive specification, while the submodels driving the primary causal mechanisms are detailed in the text. Appendices~\ref{app:rationale} and~\ref{app:supplementary} document rejected structural alternatives and supplementary technical details.

\subsection{Purpose and Patterns}
\label{sec:purpose}
\label{sec:injection}

The primary objective of the model is to evaluate the research questions outlined in Section~\ref{sec:rqs}. To achieve this, the simulation employs a \textbf{counterfactual injection} methodology: every household agent is initialised with a \ac{BNPL} balance of zero, and the macroeconomic environment is held static at 2017 conditions. The baseline model is calibrated with \ac{BNPL} disabled. \ac{BNPL} is subsequently introduced into the environment with all other parameters held constant. Consequently, the model outputs do not forecast a specific chronological year post-2017; rather, they provide directional and mechanistic insights into the isolated impact of \ac{BNPL} introduction.

Table~\ref{tab:patterns} establishes four empirical patterns as predefined validation criteria for the model's realism. Pattern 2—the 2017 \ac{CCMR} arrears profile—is the primary calibration target, as it is strictly South African and vintage-matched to the underlying population data. The remaining three patterns rely on international evidence or subsequent surveys and serve as secondary structural checks (detailed further in Section~\ref{sec:lim-validation}).

\subsection{Entities, Scales and Scheduling}
\label{sec:entities}

\textbf{Household agents} represent the sole adaptive entities within the simulation. Each agent corresponds to an empirical NIDS Wave 5 household, inheriting the static attributes defined in Table~\ref{tab:agent-mapping}. Agents maintain a dynamic internal state tracking cash reserves, traditional debt, \ac{BNPL} obligations, accrued arrears, consecutive periods of financial distress, default status, and a deterministic behavioural archetype (minimum-payer or scheduled-payer) assigned at initialisation. The \textbf{traditional lender} functions as a static, aggregate entity holding all traditional debt; it exhibits no adaptive behaviour and operates strictly as a deterministic affordability gate. The \textbf{credit bureau} acts as a shared information structure recording traditional debt, dictating the traditional lender's observational scope. The \textbf{\ac{BNPL} platforms} represent the injected entities. Four platforms operate in the baseline to mirror the South African market structure; each maintains isolated receivables, applies a lightweight automated screening process, imposes per-order caps, and operates with zero visibility into competitor exposures. \textbf{Reference groups} constitute the model's social topology, defined by the intersection of income quintile and province. This creates 45 distinct groups over the 5{,}000 agents. 

The simulated environment remains homogeneous and exogenously fixed at 2017 conditions. The temporal scale operates on a 14-day tick, aligning with the standard ``pay-in-four'' \ac{BNPL} repayment cadence while cleanly subdividing monthly survey income. The simulation horizon spans 52 ticks, with the initial 12 ticks discarded as burn-in to eliminate initialisation artefacts.

Scheduling operates synchronously at the tick level. Within each tick, households execute actions in a randomised, asynchronous order that is reseeded every step. Following household actions, the lender processes credit applications, after which global states update. A household's intra-tick execution strictly follows this sequence: income is received subject to exogenous shocks; committed expenditures are deducted; scheduled debt servicing is attempted; discretionary consumption is allocated; if liquidity is exhausted at either payment step, credit applications are generated; the agent then observes the \ac{BNPL} adoption rate of its reference group from the \emph{previous} tick to update its want-driven consumption propensity; finally, balances, arrears, distress counters, and default flags are updated.

This specific execution order isolates two critical mechanisms. First, prioritising committed expenditure over debt servicing ensures that the default condition (defined as the failure to finance minimum consumption \cite{madeira2018chile}) is logically coherent; placing debt service first would mathematically preclude this definition of default. Second, positioning borrowing late in the sequence ensures credit is utilised to address a realised, rather than anticipated, liquidity shortfall \cite{dorazio2017micro}. This allows agents to borrow to service existing debt, driving the primary financial feedback loop. The one-tick lag on social observation stabilises the secondary, norm-driven feedback loop, ensuring it remains independent of the asynchronous activation order.

\textbf{Emergence.} The model does not exogenously impose default rates, stacking depths, or systemic tipping points; rather, these metrics emerge dynamically from two distinct feedback loops. The \emph{financial} loop is driven by liquidity-constrained households borrowing to satisfy existing obligations, thereby inflating future servicing requirements. The \emph{social} loop is driven by aggregate adoption rates positively influencing individual adoption propensities within peer networks. Setting the peer-influence coefficient to zero effectively neutralises the social loop while preserving the financial loop, permitting isolated causal analysis.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 10: Information Asymmetry}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

Agents possess perfect internal knowledge of their balance sheets and observe the aggregate \ac{BNPL} adoption rate of their reference group with a one-tick lag. In contrast, the traditional lender acts on restricted information: it observes an applicant's declared gross income, its own institutional exposure, and external traditional debt listed on the credit bureau. Crucially, it cannot observe \ac{BNPL} obligations—which currently operate outside South African statutory bureau reporting requirements \cite{nortonrose_bnpl_sa, transunion_cps_sa_2025}—nor informal debt structures such as stokvels or mashonisa loans \cite{finscope2019}.

Consequently, the statutory Regulation 23A affordability test is executed over an incomplete liability profile. This dynamic reflects a systemic regulatory gap rather than non-compliant lender behaviour. Bureau visibility is implemented as a boolean parameter (default disabled) and serves as the primary policy intervention lever. Activating visibility allows the affordability gate to assess the complete liability stack. The divergence in outcomes between the visible and blind states provides a direct quantitative measure of the systemic risk introduced by the reporting exemption.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 11: Peer Influence}
\label{sec:peer-influence}

Let $s_g(t-1)$ denote the proportion of households within reference group $g$ maintaining a non-zero \ac{BNPL} balance at the conclusion of the previous tick. The want-driven \ac{BNPL} consumption propensity for household $i$ is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    q_i(t) = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(q_{\text{base}} + \beta \, s_{g(i)}(t-1),\; 0,\; 1\right).
    \label{eq:peer}
\end{equation}

A strictly positive base propensity ($q_{\text{base}}$) is structurally necessary to initiate adoption; under a purely multiplicative specification, an initial state of zero balances would preclude adoption permanently. Therefore, the coefficients function analogously to the innovation and imitation parameters in Bass diffusion models. This formulation is grounded in empirical observations that \ac{BNPL} consumption is heavily norm-driven \cite{ackert2025bnpl}, aligns with established macroeconomic ABM methodologies \cite{cardaci2018inequality}, and maps to Granovetter's threshold models of collective behaviour \cite{granovetter1978threshold}.

Given that neither coefficient can be precisely calibrated using existing South African data, the $\beta = 0$ condition is treated as an independent control arm rather than a standard parameter bound. Presenting this null-social row across all results successfully isolates the mechanical financial feedback loop from the sociological transmission mechanism.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 13: Stacking}
\label{sec:stacking}

Agents are permitted to maintain concurrent credit facilities across multiple \ac{BNPL} platforms without an aggregate limit. The inability of platforms to observe one another is a direct, endogenous consequence of the information asymmetry established in Submodel~10. Lacking a centralised reporting infrastructure, each platform screens applicants against an incomplete liability set, mirroring the traditional lender's vulnerability. The total number of active platforms is swept from one to six. The single-platform environment serves as a control to differentiate internal rolling accumulation from genuine cross-firm stacking. Systemic stacking depth emerges endogenously and is benchmarked against \ac{CFPB} observations indicating that approximately 63\% of borrowers hold simultaneous loans and 32\% hold them across multiple firms \cite{cfpb2025bnpl}. 

\subsubsection*{Submodel 15: Intervention Levers}
\label{sec:levers}

The simulation incorporates four distinct policy intervention levers to address the study's third research question. \textbf{Bureau visibility} eliminates the reporting gap established in Submodel 10. A \textbf{mandatory affordability check} enforces the rigorous Regulation 23A assessment on \ac{BNPL} transactions, operationalising international regulatory recommendations \cite{fca_ps26_1, woolard2021} within a South African context. A \textbf{cooling-off period} imposes a mandatory one-tick (14-day) moratorium on subsequent want-driven \ac{BNPL} originations following a transaction, explicitly targeting impulsive consumption while leaving shortfall-driven emergency borrowing unhindered. Finally, a \textbf{concurrent facility cap} physically restricts cross-platform stacking; this lever is explicitly noted as a hypothetical test, as it currently lacks a direct jurisdictional precedent. The structural efficacy of these interventions is evaluated by analysing cumulative \ac{BNPL} volume over the simulation horizon: an intervention that merely shifts transaction timing (deferral) will exhibit stable cumulative volume, whereas one that structural alters behaviour (desistance) will demonstrably depress cumulative volume.

\subsection{Implementation and Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:implementation}

The ABM is implemented programmatically in Python using the Mesa framework, compartmentalised within the \texttt{simulation/} directory. The architecture maps one class to each \ac{ODD} entity, governed by a central model class managing the scheduler and data aggregation. The synthetic population is instantiated directly from the parquet files generated during the data construction phase (Section~\ref{ch:population}).

Model verification—ensuring the computational implementation faithfully executes the conceptual specification—is distinct from empirical validation and is enforced via a rigorous automated test suite. This suite asserts core invariances: balance-sheet conservation, chronological arrears ageing across \ac{CCMR} cohorts, faithful execution of Regulation 23A mathematical worked examples, precise tick-alignment of Pay-in-4 amortisation schedules, and statutory caps on late fees. Furthermore, the architecture is verified against degenerate edge cases: disabling \ac{BNPL} perfectly reconstructs the baseline model, setting $\beta = 0$ isolates the independent-agent paradigm, and restricting the environment to a single platform mathematically forces cross-firm stacking to zero. 

Two parameters are empirically fitted: the probability of an income shock and the payment-inattention rate. Each is calibrated against a single \ac{CCMR} arrears band, leaving the remaining bands entirely free to serve as out-of-sample structural checks. All other parameters are empirically sourced or systematically swept across predefined spaces. Executions are strictly seeded, replicated, and aggregated, with statistical dispersion transparently reported. The parameter sweep grid and noise floors are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}, with the complete parameter register documented in Appendix~\ref{app:specification}.
