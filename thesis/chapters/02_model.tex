\section{The Model}
\label{sec:model}

We model the South African household credit market using survey-derived household agents that earn, spend, borrow, repay and default.
A traditional lender applies a residual-income test based on Regulation 23A, while \ac{BNPL} platforms use a lighter screen.
This section describes the agents, information flows, tick sequence and decision rules; Section~\ref{sec:calibration} explains the parameter choices.
The specification follows the \ac{ODD} protocol \cite{grimm2006odd, grimm2020odd}.
Table~\ref{tab:submodels} summarises the rules, Appendix~\ref{app:specification} records the design concepts and exclusions, and Appendices~\ref{app:pseudo-init} and~\ref{app:pseudo-main} give the pseudocode.
Rejected alternatives are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:rationale}.

\label{sec:purpose}
We calibrate the baseline to 2017 conditions with \ac{BNPL} disabled, then introduce \ac{BNPL} while holding the other parameters fixed.
Every household starts with a zero \ac{BNPL} balance.
Differences between the runs estimate the effect of the added credit channel within the model, rather than forecasting outcomes for a later calendar year.

\subsection{Agents and Institutions}
\label{sec:entities}

The model consists of five entities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Household agents} are the adaptive entities. Each maps to an empirical
    \ac{NIDS} Wave 5 household (Table~\ref{tab:agent-mapping}) and tracks cash reserves,
    traditional debt, \ac{BNPL} obligations, arrears, distress, default status, and a fixed
    repayment archetype (minimum-payer or scheduled-payer).
    \item \textbf{The traditional lender} is a single aggregate entity holding all traditional
    debt. It is non-adaptive and acts only as a deterministic affordability gate.
    \item \textbf{The credit bureau} is a shared registry of traditional debt and defaults that
    defines what the traditional lender can observe.
    \item \textbf{\ac{BNPL} platforms} are the injected entities, four in the baseline to mirror
    the South African market. Each holds isolated receivables, applies an automated screen with a
    per-order cap and per-household rolling limits, and has no visibility into competitors.
    \item \textbf{Reference groups} define the social topology: each household belongs to one
    group, its income quintile crossed with its province.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:agent-mapping} lists the state variables each household is initialised with. Variables map directly to microdata observations, structural imputations, or derived accounting rules; unused survey fields are excluded.

\subsection{Balance Sheets and Information}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

The household balance sheet is the accounting core of the model.
Each tick a household receives income, pays committed expenditure (food and rent, an incompressible floor), owes scheduled service on its traditional debt and instalments on any \ac{BNPL} facilities, and spends what remains on discretionary consumption, which compresses to zero before any instalment is missed.
Liquid savings absorb shortfalls.
Traditional debt and \ac{BNPL} balances are carried separately, because different institutions observe them.

Figure~\ref{fig:visibility} illustrates the model's information structure.
The dashed connections highlight the reporting gaps of the pre-2026 position: platforms lack visibility into competitor extensions, and \ac{BNPL} liabilities are not reported to the central bureau.

The traditional lender assesses credit applications using self-reported gross income, existing internal exposures, and active traditional balances logged at the central bureau.
Unregulated liabilities, such as \ac{BNPL} commitments \cite{nortonrose_bnpl_sa, transunion_cps_sa_2025} and informal loans (e.g., stokvels or mashonisa credit) \cite{finscope2019}, remain unobserved. Consequently, statutory Regulation 23A affordability checks are applied to incomplete liability profiles, capturing a key regulatory oversight gap rather than lender non-compliance.

Bureau reporting is toggled via a boolean parameter (\texttt{bnpl\_bureau\_visible}), set to false in baseline runs to reflect the South African position before the 2026 reporting requirement.
Enabling bureau visibility adds active \ac{BNPL} obligations to the lender's affordability calculation, representing one channel of the reporting requirement (Section~\ref{sec:background}).

The model has no credit score and cannot estimate how reported \ac{BNPL} behaviour changes scores, loan pricing or credit refusals.
FINASA reports that one bureau's preliminary analysis found potential score reductions affecting roughly a quarter of credit-active consumers \cite{finasa_bnpl_2026}.
The comparison in Section~\ref{sec:scenarios} therefore addresses the affordability channel only.
Even a small effect in that comparison would leave the scoring channel untested (Section~\ref{sec:future-work}).

\subsection{Within-Tick Sequence and State Changes}
\label{sec:sequence}

The simulation advances in discrete 14-day ticks, so that the biweekly pay-in-four instalment schedule falls directly on tick boundaries; monthly survey cash flows are scaled to ticks by a factor of $12/26$.
Household activation order is randomised uniformly at each tick, credit decisions execute as applications occur, and the social signal updates once, at the end of the tick.
A household's tick follows seven steps (Algorithm~\ref{alg:tick}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Receive income, adjusted for employment shocks.
    \item Pay committed expenditure from income and savings.
    \item Accrue interest and calculate traditional and \ac{BNPL} payments due, including arrears. Minimum-payers owe the larger of interest and the minimum balance fraction; scheduled-payers owe their contractual instalment.
    \item Seek credit for a shortfall, using \ac{BNPL} first where eligible and traditional credit for the remainder. Assess distress before payment friction.
    \item Collect \ac{BNPL} payments, then pay traditional debt subject to payment friction.
    \item Spend remaining cash on discretionary consumption.
    \item Draw any want-driven \ac{BNPL} purchase and update balances, arrears, distress and default.
\end{enumerate}

Three sequence choices govern transmission dynamics within the model.
First, prioritising committed consumption over debt service enables cash-flow default driven by living expense shortfalls \cite{madeira2018chile}.
Second, allowing credit applications prior to debt settlement models debt-servicing liquidity feedback \cite{dorazio2017micro}.
Third, evaluating distress prior to payment friction ensures that inadvertent payment delays do not misclassify solvent households as insolvent.
Lagging the reference group signal by one tick ensures that peer influences remain independent of intra-tick execution order.

A household is distressed if it cannot pay committed expenditure from opening cash and income, or cannot meet scheduled service after seeking credit.
The implementation retains an initial committed-expenditure shortfall as distress even if later borrowing raises enough cash to cover it.
Unpaid amounts age through the \ac{CCMR} arrears bands, and seven consecutive distressed ticks, 98 days and the first tick boundary past the 90-day impairment convention, constitute default, after which the household is refused all further credit.

\subsubsection*{Submodels 4 and 5: Borrowing on a Shortfall}
\label{sec:amount}

Submodel~4 sets the amount requested after a shortfall.
No located study determines this amount, so the sensitivity analysis compares all three rules in Table~\ref{tab:submodels} (Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}).
Submodel~5 then routes the request.
An eligible household turns to \ac{BNPL} first, but only up to $\kappa$ times its monthly discretionary budget, the quantity that also sizes a want-driven purchase: \ac{BNPL} finances retail goods in particular categories and not general-purpose cash, so it can relieve a shortfall only to the extent of what the household spends in those categories.
Whatever the cap excludes goes to the traditional lender and faces the Regulation 23A gate of Submodel~9, which grants or refuses in full.
The cap has no literature anchor either, so the three amount rules are also run with it removed.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 11: Peer Influence}
\label{sec:peer-influence}

Let $s_g(t-1)$ represent the proportion of \ac{BNPL}-eligible households in reference group $g$ holding active \ac{BNPL} balances at tick $t-1$. Restricting the calculation denominator to eligible group members prevents adoption signals from scaling directly with overall financial inclusion rates. Household $i$'s discretionary origination propensity is defined by:

\begin{equation}
    q_i(t) = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(q_{\text{base}} + \beta \, s_{g(i)}(t-1),\; 0,\; 1\right).
    \label{eq:peer}
\end{equation}

A positive $q_{\text{base}}$ allows adoption to begin when no household yet holds \ac{BNPL}.
The coefficient $\beta$ controls the response to peer adoption, motivated by evidence on social approval \cite{ackert2025bnpl, cardaci2018inequality}.
Both parameters are swept; $\beta = 0$ removes peer influence from the comparison.

To address potential sensitivity to linear specifications, we pre-register a Granovetter-style heterogeneous threshold model as a structural robustness check \cite{granovetter1978threshold}.
Under this formulation, household propensity rises to $q_i = \mathrm{clip}(q_{\text{base}} + \gamma,0,1)$ when lagged group adoption reaches an agent-specific threshold.
Thresholds are drawn from $\mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta, \sigma_\theta)$ and clipped to $[0,1]$; draws below zero become zero, allowing some households to respond immediately.
Threshold parameters are generated using fixed initialisation seeds to maintain exact comparability across control settings.
Parameter sweeps focus primarily on threshold dispersion ($\sigma_\theta$), testing whether cascade dynamics depend on variance in peer susceptibility rather than mean thresholds.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 13: Stacking}
\label{sec:stacking}

Absent centralised reporting, households can maintain concurrent credit lines across multiple platforms without aggregate exposure caps (Submodel~10). We evaluate platform multiplicity by sweeping the available provider count from one to six, treating the single-platform setting as a benchmark to separate multi-platform credit stacking from intra-firm credit expansion. Emerging credit stacking patterns are benchmarked against consumer credit data reported by the \ac{CFPB} (Section~\ref{sec:background}).

Two switches act on these mechanisms and define the scenarios of Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}: $\mathbb{1}^{\text{bur}}$ adds \ac{BNPL} obligations to what the Regulation 23A gate reads, and $\mathbb{1}^{\text{aff}}$ applies that gate to \ac{BNPL} originations themselves.
Algorithms~\ref{alg:gate} and~\ref{alg:bnpl} show the single line each one changes.

\subsection{Implementation and Verification}
\label{sec:implementation}

The model is implemented in Python on the Mesa framework.
Households, the lender, the bureau and the platforms are each a class; reference groups are keys
on the model.
The population loads directly from the parquet files of
Section~\ref{sec:population}, and the Regulation 23A gate reuses the same function that
constructed scheduled servicing, so the gate and the data layer are consistent.
The parameter register of Appendix~\ref{app:specification} is generated from the code's parameter declarations and must be regenerated after parameter changes.

Automated tests cover balance-sheet accounting, arrears transitions, Regulation 23A worked examples, pay-in-four repayments and contractual late-fee caps.
Boundary checks disable \ac{BNPL}, remove peer influence ($\beta = 0$ or $\gamma = 0$), and restrict access to one platform.
These checks test the implementation; the final experiment and sensitivity runs provide the evidence for the results sections.
