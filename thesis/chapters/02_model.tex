\section{The Model}
\label{sec:model}

The model follows households as they receive income, spend, borrow and repay debt.
Each household begins with financial characteristics drawn from survey microdata.
Traditional credit is subject to the Regulation 23A residual-income test,
while \ac{BNPL} platforms apply a light screen. The \ac{BNPL} light screen refuses unbanked and defaulted households
and limits each order and balance, without comparing income with living costs.

\label{sec:purpose}
The experiments begin with a baseline without \ac{BNPL}, calibrated to 2017
conditions. We then enable \ac{BNPL} while holding the other parameters fixed.
Traditional arrears start from the 2017 profile, but every household starts with zero
\ac{BNPL} balances, so any \ac{BNPL} balance arises during the simulation. Comparing the runs isolates the effect of the
additional credit channel within the model. We call the run with \ac{BNPL}
enabled and no regulatory change the benchmark.

The model moves in steps of 14 days, called ticks, because pay-in-four instalments fall due
every fortnight. A year has 26 fortnights, so a monthly survey amount $M$ becomes $12M/26$,
about $0.46M$, per tick. Annual
interest rates are divided by 26. Stocks, such as savings and debt balances, are not converted.

The specification follows the \ac{ODD} protocol~\citep{grimm2006odd,grimm2020odd}.
Table~\ref{tab:submodels} summarises the rules and Section~\ref{sec:calibration} sets the
parameters. Appendix~\ref{app:rationale} gives the alternatives considered,
Appendix~\ref{app:specification} the design concepts and exclusions, and
Appendices~\ref{app:pseudo-init} and~\ref{app:pseudo-main} the pseudocode.

The model contains five types of entity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Households}: each is based on a \ac{NIDS} Wave 5 household
    (Table~\ref{tab:agent-mapping}) and tracks savings, traditional debt,
    \ac{BNPL} obligations, arrears and their age, consumption distress and default. Its repayment archetype,
    minimum-payer or scheduled-payer, is fixed at initialisation.
    \item \textbf{The traditional lender} is one consolidated institution
    holding all traditional debt. It decides each application with the Regulation 23A
    test, applied to the obligations the bureau records, and refuses households the bureau
    shows in default. The rule is fixed; the lender does not tighten its test, prices or terms
    when default rises.
    \item \textbf{The credit bureau} records each household's traditional debt service and
    whether it has defaulted. In the benchmark it does not record \ac{BNPL} balances or
    instalments, but it records every default, so a household that defaults on \ac{BNPL} alone
    gets no further credit from the lender or any platform.
    \item \textbf{\ac{BNPL} platforms} provide the additional credit channel.
    The benchmark assumes four platforms. Each maintains its own receivables and applies the
    light screen. Platforms cannot observe one another's balances.
    \item \textbf{Reference groups} define whose adoption a household observes.
    Each household belongs to the group formed by its income quintile and province.
\end{itemize}

The traditional lender assesses an application on the household's surveyed income
and the scheduled service on its traditional debt, as the bureau records it. Surveyed income is
not reduced during a spell of unemployment (Submodel~1), so the test counts wages the household
is not receiving. In the benchmark the assessment excludes \ac{BNPL}
obligations, reflecting the reporting position of Section~\ref{sec:background}
\citep{dcasa_bnpl_2026,finasa_bnpl_2026}. The model has no informal lender, and
the informal loans that \ac{NIDS} records, 2.7\% of traditional debt, are carried as debt the
bureau sees.
% 2.7%: results/summary/reference_audit_checks.json (notebooks/scripts/check_reference_audit_figures.py).

A switch, bureau visibility ($\mathbb{1}^{\text{bur}}$), controls whether \ac{BNPL} obligations enter the
test. Switching it on represents one channel of the
reporting arrangements of Section~\ref{sec:background}. A second switch,
mandatory screening ($\mathbb{1}^{\text{aff}}$), applies the same test to each
\ac{BNPL} request before it reaches a platform. That test sees the household's traditional
service and its \ac{BNPL} obligations on every platform, whether or not bureau visibility is
on. Screening is therefore the most complete check the model allows, though it still uses
surveyed income.

The Regulation 23A test works in monthly amounts, whereas \ac{BNPL} instalments fall due
every fortnight. Wherever the test counts \ac{BNPL}, under bureau visibility or screening, it
counts each live agreement's fortnightly instalment, multiplied by $26/12$, as a monthly
obligation. Unpaid \ac{BNPL} instalments and late fees are counted the same way until paid, as
if owed again every month, which overstates the burden of a single missed instalment. The test
never counts traditional arrears; it sees only scheduled traditional service.

Bureau visibility acts only through the Regulation 23A test. The model has no credit
score and no risk-based pricing, so it cannot represent score-based refusals. This omission
may matter, given the projected score reductions reported by \ac{FINASA}
\citeyearpar{finasa_bnpl_2026}; Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} discusses adding a score.

\subsection{Within-Tick Sequence and State Changes}
\label{sec:sequence}

Households act in a random order, redrawn each tick, and credit applications are decided as
they occur. The peer signal is updated once all households have acted, so adoption does not
depend on which group member acts first.

Each household follows seven steps (Algorithm~\ref{alg:tick}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Receive income, adjusted for job losses and hires.
    \item Pay committed expenditure from income and savings.
    \item Accrue interest and calculate traditional and \ac{BNPL} payments due, including arrears (Submodel~6).
    \item Seek credit for a shortfall, using \ac{BNPL} first where eligible and traditional credit for the remainder.
    \item Pay the \ac{BNPL} instalments that fall due, then traditional debt subject to payment friction.
    \item Spend remaining cash on discretionary consumption, up to the budget, and save the rest.
    \item Draw any want-driven \ac{BNPL} purchase and update balances, arrears ages and default.
\end{enumerate}

A household has a shortfall when its income and savings do not cover committed expenditure
plus the payments due, \ac{BNPL} instalments included, so a new agreement can free the cash for
an old one's instalment. Because committed expenditure is paid first, it can leave too little
cash for debt service, consistent with a cash-flow approach to distress~\citep{madeira2018chile}.
Credit applications precede repayment, so borrowing can cover that gap.
\ac{BNPL} instalments are paid before traditional debt, an assumption based on platforms
debiting a card automatically, so any \ac{BNPL}
obligation leaves less cash for traditional repayment. For the same reason, payment friction and arrears persistence (Submodel~6) apply
to traditional debt only.

Every unpaid instalment, traditional or \ac{BNPL}, is recorded with the tick it fell due, and
payments clear the oldest first. The arrears age of the traditional balance or of a \ac{BNPL}
agreement is the age of its oldest unpaid instalment. A household's arrears age is the largest of
these and places it in a \ac{CCMR} age band. Credit-active households are those
owing any traditional or \ac{BNPL} balance or arrears.

Instalments fall due at the start of a tick and are aged at its end, so each unpaid tick
adds 14 days. A household defaults when its arrears age reaches seven ticks, or 98 days, the first tick
boundary beyond 90 days past due. This is close to the threshold at which the \ac{NCR}'s Credit Bureau
Monitor classes a record as impaired, ``three or more payments or months in
arrears''~\citep[p.~8]{ncr_cbm_2017}. Under this rule a household that falls two months behind
and catches up in the third month never defaults. Default is permanent and recorded by the bureau, and a defaulted
household receives no further credit. Its debts are non-performing: nothing more is due or
collected, no interest accrues, and each tick they are written off with a probability derived in
Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}. A written-off household leaves the book but stays in default.

Food and rent are not debts, so leaving them unpaid cannot cause default. Credit raised in the same
tick pays them first, and what stays unpaid is not owed later; we report it as consumption
distress.

\subsection{Credit and Adoption Mechanisms}
\label{sec:submodels}


\subsubsection*{Submodels 4 and 5: Borrowing on a Shortfall}
\label{sec:amount}

Under Submodel~4 a household short of cash requests exactly its shortfall. Two
alternatives add 25\% of the shortfall or one tick of committed expenditure. We found no study that
identifies the right rule, so the sensitivity analysis compares all three
(Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}).

Under Submodel~5 an eligible household uses \ac{BNPL} first. Because \ac{BNPL} finances retail
purchases, the amount it finances for a shortfall is capped at the mean want-driven purchase,
$\kappa$ times the monthly discretionary budget; this is the shortfall cap. \ac{BNPL} cannot pay rent or an instalment directly; it
relieves a shortfall because the household finances a purchase, such as food, that it would
otherwise have paid for in cash. An agreement of value $F$ takes $F/4$ at checkout and leaves $3F/4$ in
the household's hands, so it covers $3F/4$ of the shortfall. We assume the household asks the
traditional lender for the rest, and the lender grants or refuses that
amount in full under Submodel~9. A refused household leaves the remainder unpaid, as
consumption distress or arrears.

The shortfall path, \ac{BNPL} used against a shortfall, reflects evidence that \ac{BNPL} users
are more likely to be financially constrained~\citep{hayashi2025constraints}. No study measures how much of a shortfall users finance with \ac{BNPL}, so the
sensitivity analysis repeats each borrowing-amount rule with the shortfall cap removed, and a further
arm removes \ac{BNPL} from the shortfall path altogether.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 11: Peer Influence}
\label{sec:peer-influence}

Peer influence changes the probability of a want-driven purchase. Let
$s_g(t-1)$ be the share of eligible households in reference group $g$ holding
a \ac{BNPL} balance at the previous tick, counting a defaulted household's frozen balance until
write-off. Using eligible households
as the denominator separates this signal from the group's overall banking
rate. Household $i$'s purchase propensity is:

\begin{equation}
    q_i(t) = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(q_{\text{base}} + \beta \, s_{g(i)}(t-1),\; 0,\; 1\right).
    \label{eq:peer}
\end{equation}

Because $q_{\text{base}}$ is positive, borrowing can begin before anyone in the group has
adopted, and $\beta$ sets how strongly the household responds to others' adoption. Experimental evidence on social approval~\citep{ackert2025bnpl} and a model of
consumption emulation~\citep{cardaci2018inequality} support the channel but do not
identify its strength, so both parameters are swept. The reference values are
$q_{\text{base}}=0.05$ and $\beta=0$, the no-peer control.

The alternative is a threshold rule~\citep{granovetter1978threshold}: once the lagged group
share reaches the household's threshold, the propensity rises by a step $\gamma$, to
$q_i = \mathrm{clip}(q_{\text{base}} + \gamma,0,1)$. Thresholds are drawn from
$\mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta, \sigma_\theta^2)$, with reference values $\mu_\theta=0.30$ and
$\sigma_\theta=0.20$, and clipped to $[0,1]$, so households with a negative draw
respond immediately. The sweeps vary threshold dispersion ($\sigma_\theta$),
and separate arms vary the mean. The threshold arms use $\gamma=0.3$; $\gamma=0$ removes the response.

\subsubsection*{Submodel 13: Stacking}
\label{sec:stacking}

A household stacks when it owes more than one platform at once. Under random routing, a
household sends each \ac{BNPL} request to the platforms in a random order, redrawn for every
request. The first platform finances as much as its order cap
and its limit for that household allow, and any remainder passes to the next.
Each platform limits only its own exposure, and the benchmark has no aggregate
limit. A household that borrows again before an earlier agreement is repaid will
therefore usually owe more than one platform: with $P$ platforms, a second
request reaches a different platform first with probability $(P-1)/P$.

No household chooses how many platforms to use; random routing alone spreads its overlapping
agreements across platforms. The stacking measures therefore show how often
agreements overlap and say nothing about why households use several platforms. We found
no evidence on how South African households choose among providers, so random routing is
an assumption. An alternative arm uses loyal routing: a household tries the platforms it
already owes first and moves to another only when a limit binds
(Section~\ref{sec:results-stacking}).

We vary the number of platforms from one to six. One platform rules out stacking but
still permits several agreements.

\subsection{Implementation and Verification}
\label{sec:implementation}

The model is implemented in Python using Mesa. The Regulation 23A test reuses the
function that constructs scheduled debt service, and the parameter register in
Appendix~\ref{app:specification} is generated from the code. Automated checks, several against
values calculated by hand, cover cash and debt accounting, oldest-first arrears ageing and the
tick of default on each facility, the Regulation 23A test, pay-in-four repayments, fee caps, the
shortfall path and the platform cap. Boundary checks disable
\ac{BNPL}, remove peer influence and restrict access to one platform. All checks pass on
the submitted code.
