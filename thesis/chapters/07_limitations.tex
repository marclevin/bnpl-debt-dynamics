\chapter{Limitations}
\label{ch:limitations}

Several of the limitations below were adopted knowingly, as the price of a design decision made
for good reasons elsewhere. Others are genuine weaknesses. This chapter distinguishes between the
two, because a reader is entitled to know which constraints were chosen and which were merely
inherited.

A general rule applies throughout: wherever a caveat is recorded against a data artefact in this
project, it has a home in this chapter. Nothing material is documented only in a configuration
file or a notebook.

\section{Limitations of the Population}
\label{sec:lim-population}

\subsection{FinScope 2019 stands in for 2017}
\label{sec:lim-finscope}

No \ac{FinScope} wave was available for 2017, so the 2019 wave supplies the four
financial-inclusion flags for a population otherwise fixed at 2017. The imported variables are
categorical --- banked or not, holds formal credit or not --- so no deflation is required and the
2017-Rand commitment is not violated. The two-year gap is nonetheless real: financial inclusion in
South Africa was changing over that period, and banked status in particular gates \ac{BNPL}
eligibility in Submodel~12. The gap is recorded and not corrected, because any correction would
require an assumption about the direction and pace of change that this thesis cannot source.

\subsection{The liquid savings field is weak}
\label{sec:lim-savings}

\texttt{liquid\_savings} is the least reliable quantity carried into the model. It derives from
the \ac{NIDS} financial-assets field, which measures reported financial assets and not an
accessible cash balance, and which is among the most under-reported items in household surveys
generally. It required winsorisation at the 99th percentile to remove implausible outliers.

The consequence is specific and uncomfortable. Validation pattern~4 measures the share of agents
reaching zero liquid savings, so the model is being judged partly on its handling of its own
weakest input. Section~\ref{sec:lim-targets} takes up whether pattern~4 should carry the weight of
a validation target at all.

\subsection{Regulation 23A is applied per household}
\label{sec:lim-reg23a}

Regulation 23A prescribes a minimum-expense norm for an individual consumer. The model applies it
to household income and household expenditure, because \ac{NIDS} is a household survey and the
agent is a household. For a single-earner household the two coincide. For a household with several
earners, applying a per-consumer norm to pooled income makes the affordability test more
permissive than the regulation would be in practice, since the norm is not scaled by the number of
consumers in the household. The direction of the bias is therefore known --- the model's lender is
somewhat more willing to lend than a compliant lender would be --- even though its magnitude is
not.

\subsection{Servicing is constructed, not observed}
\label{sec:lim-servicing}

Neither survey records a monthly repayment amount, so Section~\ref{sec:servicing} constructs one.
Three assumptions enter. Statutory maximum prescribed rates are \emph{ceilings}, not observed
average rates, and the \ac{NCR} \ac{CCMR} does not publish realised interest rates, so no
observed alternative exists; using the ceiling makes the model's opening servicing burden an upper
bound. Loan terms are assumed, not observed, with a six-month floor. And the product mix
implied by assigning sub-sector rates to a consolidated debt balance is itself an assumption,
since \ac{NIDS} records a single debt figure and not a portfolio.

A related diagnostic is reported in Section~\ref{sec:servicing} and repeated here as a limitation
of the data and not of the model: 53 households carrying debt have income at or below the
Regulation 23A expense norm for their band, so no compliant lender could lawfully have granted the
debt they hold. They were retained. Whether this reflects informal borrowing that falls outside
the \ac{NCA}, misreporting in the survey, or non-compliant lending cannot be determined from the
data available.

\subsection{The resample is slightly more unequal than the population}
\label{sec:lim-resample}

Weighted resampling to 5{,}000 agents raises the per-capita income Gini from 0.651 to 0.671. The
distortion passes its tolerance with very little margin, and it runs in the direction that would,
if anything, modestly overstate distress among the lowest-income agents. It is small, it is
measured, and it is not corrected.

\section{Limitations of the Validation Strategy}
\label{sec:lim-validation}

\subsection{The baseline is calibrated, not validated}
\label{sec:lim-calibration}

This is the most important limitation in the thesis and it deserves to be stated without
softening. The income-shock probability of Submodel~1 cannot be estimated from the data, because
\ac{NIDS} Wave 5 enters this design as a single wave and within-household income volatility is not
identified from one wave. The probability is therefore \emph{fitted} so that baseline arrears
reproduce validation pattern~2.

It follows that the no-\ac{BNPL} baseline is calibrated rather than validated. Its agreement with
the 2017 \ac{CCMR} is not evidence that the model is right; it is the result of having tuned a
parameter until it agreed. Only the results obtained after \ac{BNPL} is injected --- with that
parameter then held fixed --- are genuine out-of-sample predictions of the model. Patterns 3 and 4
remain independent, since nothing is tuned to either.

\subsection{The model counts households and the CCMR counts accounts}
\label{sec:lim-units}

Validation pattern~2 compares the model's arrears profile against \ac{CCMR} figures computed on an
\emph{account} basis. The model's unit is the \emph{household}. A household may hold several
credit accounts, and accounts in arrears are not distributed one per household, so an account-level
arrears share is not a household-level distress rate and the two cannot be equated.

The comparison is therefore an order-of-magnitude check and not a point target, and pattern~2
should be read as asking whether the model's arrears profile is of the right shape and scale, not
whether it reproduces 71.63 percent current. Since pattern~2 is also the target the income-shock
probability is fitted to, the imprecision propagates into the calibration itself.

\subsection{Three of the four targets are foreign or off-vintage}
\label{sec:lim-targets}

Only pattern~2 is both South African and vintage-matched to the 2017 population. Pattern~1 rests
on United States banking data for 2015 to 2021. Pattern~3 rests on a United Kingdom credit-card
model. Pattern~4 rests on a South African survey fielded in 2025, and comparing a 2017 population
against a 2025 survey is precisely the cross-period comparison that
Section~\ref{sec:injection} otherwise forbids.

Pattern~4 has a second weakness. It compares the share of agents at zero liquid savings against
the share of consumers who said they anticipated missing a bill payment. These are different
constructs --- a realised stock condition against a stated expectation --- with no established
mapping between them, and the pattern carries no explicit tolerance band. Combined with the
weakness of the underlying savings field (Section~\ref{sec:lim-savings}), pattern~4 is best read
as a plausibility check, not as a test the model could meaningfully fail.

\subsection{Pattern 1 is not fully independent of the design}
\label{sec:lim-pattern1}

Section~\ref{sec:purpose} sets this out and it is restated here for completeness. The want-driven
borrowing path of Submodel~3 was adopted partly because a purely shortfall-driven \ac{BNPL} agent
would treat the product as a pure substitute for traditional credit and could not reproduce the
deHaan et al. complementarity result under any parameterisation. The direction of pattern~1 is
therefore partly built into the lender-choice rule. Its magnitude is not, and the pattern can
still fail if the Regulation 23A gate crowds out traditional borrowing sufficiently, but it is a
weaker test than it appears.

\section{Limitations of the Model}
\label{sec:lim-model}

\subsection{Behavioural parameters are transferred from other markets}
\label{sec:lim-transfer}

The behavioural micro-foundations of Section~\ref{sec:lit-behavioural} are drawn from United
States evidence and applied to South African borrowers. The minimum-payer share of 0.29 comes from
Keys and Wang's analysis of United States credit-card accounts and is applied to South African
unsecured credit. Present bias is established on United States samples. Ackert et al., which
motivates the entire want-driven borrowing path, is an incentivised experiment conducted with
United States participants.

No developing-market behavioural evidence of comparable quality was located for any of the three.
Two responses are made, and neither fully discharges the problem. First, the transferred
parameter that matters most is swept: the minimum-payer share runs from 0.20 to 0.40, so results
are reported across a range that brackets the transferred point estimate instead of depending on
it. Second, the mechanisms being transferred --- present bias, anchoring on a salient minimum
payment --- are claims about general features of human decision-making under credit contracts, not
about institutional arrangements specific to the United States, and the burden of the transfer is
correspondingly lighter than it would be for, say, a transferred default rate. Neither argument
establishes that the parameters are right for South Africa, and this remains the model's most
significant unresolved external-validity problem.

\subsection{Two parameters cannot be calibrated at all}
\label{sec:lim-uncalibrated}

The peer-influence coefficient $\beta$ and the spontaneous adoption rate $q_{\text{base}}$ of
Equation~\ref{eq:peer} are fixed by no South African source. This creates two distinct problems.

The first is circularity, and Section~\ref{sec:peer-influence} addresses it: the peer channel was
added partly because the second research question asks about non-linearity, and non-linearity
requires feedback. Reporting non-linearity as a finding of a model built to produce it would be
worthless. The resolution is the $\beta = 0$ control arm, reported in every result, which reduces
the model exactly to independent agents and converts the claim into a conditional one.

The second problem is plausibility, and the control arm does not solve it. A reader can see which
results depend on social transmission, but cannot tell which region of the $(q_{\text{base}},
\beta)$ surface corresponds to the actual South African market. The available anchors are indirect:
the 83.1 percent banked ceiling, the TransUnion finding that 20 percent of consumers intending to
apply for credit named \ac{BNPL}, and the \ac{CFPB} stacking shares. Using the last of these to
bound $q_{\text{base}}$ rather than only to check output after the fact, would convert a free
parameter into a partly calibrated one, and is registered in Section~\ref{sec:future-work} as the
most valuable single improvement available to this model.

\subsection{The borrowing-amount rule is an assumption}
\label{sec:lim-amount}

Submodel~4 has no literature anchor. The sweep of consumer-credit agent-based models and
behavioural studies located no work that fixes a borrowing-amount rule, and D'Orazio and Giulioni
use the shortfall without defending the choice. The model requests exactly the shortfall with no
precautionary buffer. Sensitivity analysis across three specifications is mandatory and not
optional for this rule, and any material movement of results across them is reported as
amount-rule sensitivity.

\subsection{Structural exclusions}
\label{sec:lim-structural}

The following were excluded by design, and each bounds what the model can be asked.

\textbf{No macroeconomic dynamics.} Submodel~16 holds the environment fully static, because any
time-variation would confound the effect of injecting \ac{BNPL} with a macro effect and destroy
the experimental control. The cost is that the model cannot speak to how \ac{BNPL} stress
interacts with a downturn --- which matters, since \ac{BNPL} grew in South Africa through a period
of rising rates.

\textbf{No contagion.} There is one aggregate traditional lender with no balance sheet that can
fail, no interbank exposure, and no feedback from household default to employment or aggregate
income. Default is an individual cash-flow event. The only household-to-household channel
transmits \ac{BNPL} adoption, not distress. The model therefore produces correlated individual
defaults and not systemic cascades, which is why Section~\ref{sec:rqs} does not ask about the
latter.

\textbf{No competition among traditional lenders.} The traditional side is a single non-adaptive
stub. Hamill et al. show that lender conduct is itself a driver of household indebtedness, so
holding it fixed suppresses a mechanism known to matter.

\textbf{No learning and no scarring.} Households do not update their rules from experience within
the horizon, and default carries no rehabilitation path and no lasting credit-record damage beyond
cut-off. Over 24 months both are tolerable; over a longer horizon neither would be.

\textbf{No peer effects on consumption.} Cardaci's expenditure-cascade mechanism, in which
inequality raises the consumption norms of lower-income households, is not implemented. Peer
influence in this model acts only on \ac{BNPL} adoption.

\textbf{Static household composition.} Households do not form, dissolve or change size over the
horizon, and reference groups are fixed at initialisation.

\section{How Results Should Be Read}
\label{sec:lim-reading}

Taken together, these limitations imply a specific reading. Results are directional and
mechanistic. They describe what happens to a population of this kind when a lender class with
these properties is introduced, and they are informative about the sign, the rough magnitude and
the mechanism of that change. They are not forecasts of South African outcomes in any actual year,
they do not identify a threshold value of \ac{BNPL} access that would apply outside the model, and
no number reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} should be transferred to a policy calculation
without the qualifications above attached.

What survives all of it is the comparison the model was built to make: the difference between two
runs that are identical except for the presence of a bureau-reporting obligation. That contrast is
internal to the model, holds every uncalibrated parameter fixed across both arms, and is the
result this thesis rests on.
