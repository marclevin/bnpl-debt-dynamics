\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Background and Context}
\label{sec:background}

South African households face substantial credit distress despite a regulated lending market.
The \ac{NCA} \cite{sa_nca_2005} requires affordability assessments, and Regulation 23A
\cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015} specifies a residual-income test. Credit-bureau records help
registered lenders assess existing obligations. Yet 14.2\% of unsecured and credit-facility
accounts were more than 90 days in arrears in early 2017 \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017}.
In a separate late-2025 survey, 36\% of consumers expected to miss a bill or loan payment
\cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025}.

\ac{BNPL} adds credit that these assessments have not consistently captured. Providers have
argued that interest-free agreements fall outside the \ac{NCA}, and \ac{BNPL} payment histories
were absent from South African credit reports in 2025
\cite{nortonrose_bnpl_sa, transunion_cps_sa_2025}.
A household could therefore borrow from several providers without any one lender assessing the
combined obligation. The \ac{SARB}'s June 2026 Financial Stability Review identifies concurrent
\ac{BNPL} obligations and less rigorous affordability checks as risks to household indebtedness
\cite{sarb_fsr_2026_1}.

Reports of the \ac{NCR}'s response describe an agreement with \ac{SACRRA} and the Credit Bureau
Association to bring \ac{BNPL} payment behaviour into bureau records \cite{businessday_bnpl_2026}.
They also report the \ac{NCR}'s position that an agreement may become incidental credit when
default charges are levied. Providers reportedly began submitting records in 2026 while the
reporting format was being finalised \cite{dcasa_bnpl_2026}. Press reports give February 2027 as
the expected date for records to appear on consumer profiles \cite{businessday_bnpl_2026};
we treat this as a reported timetable, not a confirmed statutory commencement date.
FINASA reports that one bureau's preliminary analysis found potential score reductions for
roughly a quarter of credit-active consumers \cite{finasa_bnpl_2026}.

International evidence suggests that the additional credit can worsen financial outcomes.
deHaan et al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl} study banking records for 10.6 million United States consumers
and find higher overdraft charges, credit-card interest and late fees following \ac{BNPL}
adoption, particularly among liquidity-constrained consumers. The \ac{CFPB} \cite{cfpb2025bnpl}
reports that 63\% of borrowers held simultaneous \ac{BNPL} loans at some point and 32\% borrowed
concurrently across firms. Comparable South African evidence is limited, particularly for the
period before bureau reporting.

This thesis develops an \ac{ABM} of 5{,}000 households constructed from South African survey
microdata. We calibrate the credit market to 2017 conditions, then introduce \ac{BNPL} into the
same environment over a simulated two-year horizon. Comparisons between these runs estimate the
effect of \ac{BNPL} within the model. We then vary bureau visibility, affordability screening
and peer influence to examine how each changes household distress. The model provides a way to
study these mechanisms before local outcome data become available; it does not estimate the
historical effect of \ac{BNPL} in South Africa.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

\begin{quote}
    In a household population already carrying substantial credit distress, what does the
    addition of a \ac{BNPL} lending channel outside the affordability and reporting regime do to
    that distress, and what is the mandated extension of credit-bureau reporting to \ac{BNPL}
    likely to achieve?
\end{quote}

The model represents reporting through lenders' affordability assessments. It has no credit
score, so it answers the second part of this question for the affordability channel only
(Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry}). Three subsidiary questions guide the experiments:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can a synthetic household population built from South African survey microdata reproduce
    credit-market behaviour it was not fitted to?
    \item Does the mutual invisibility of \ac{BNPL} platforms produce concurrent-facility
    stacking, and does population default rise with the number of platforms a household can stack
    across?
    \item How do three scenarios for the \ac{BNPL} lending environment, bureau visibility,
    mandatory affordability screening, and socially transmitted adoption, change the resulting
    distress and default?
\end{enumerate}

Results are reported by income quintile because existing evidence places \ac{BNPL} harm among
lower-income and liquidity-constrained consumers \cite{hayashi2025constraints, wang2026buynow}.
Section~\ref{sec:calibration} assesses the population and baseline behaviour for RQ1.
Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:scenarios} address RQ2 and RQ3.

The findings, in descending order of importance, are these.
Mutually invisible platforms produce concurrent-facility stacking that no rule programs, of the order the \ac{CFPB} reports, yet population default does not rise with the number of platforms: the harm is the obligation, not its spread across firms.
Default rises smoothly with \ac{BNPL} access, with no tipping point under either a linear or a heterogeneous-threshold adoption rule, even where the threshold rule produces an adoption cascade; peer influence amplifies the rise without changing its shape.
In the affordability channel, neither bureau visibility nor mandatory screening changes default, with or without peer adoption, because each acts on a single agreement and neither changes how many a household holds.
The population reproduces the calibrated arrears profile and most of the checks it was not fitted to, and the failures are named.

\subsection{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

\subsubsection*{Agent-Based Models of Household Credit}
\label{sec:lit-abm}

Household credit models show how individual borrowing decisions can produce aggregate distress.
Cardaci \cite{cardaci2018inequality} models rising inequality and consumption emulation, which
lead to debt-financed spending and eventually a banking crisis. The debt is collateralised,
so the mechanism differs from unsecured pay-in-four lending. D'Orazio and Giulioni
\cite{dorazio2017micro} likewise show how unsustainable debt can emerge from local borrowing
rules. These models support an agent-based approach to obligations spread across lenders with
different information.

Madeira \cite{madeira2018chile} models Chilean households facing labour-income shocks and
defaulting when they cannot finance minimum consumption. We adopt this cash-flow approach to
default because it is consistent with the residual-income basis of the affordability test.
Madeira's comparison with observed default rates also provides a validation precedent for a
household credit model in a middle-income economy.

Hamill et al. \cite{hamill2023creditcard} find that lender promotional strategies raise
indebtedness in a model of the United Kingdom credit-card market. Their model produces a
non-monotonic debt-to-income profile, with the highest burden among middle-income households.
We use that profile as a cross-model plausibility check.
Following Windrum, Fagiolo and Moneta \cite{windrum2007validation} and the pattern-oriented
approach in the \ac{ODD} protocol \cite{grimm2020odd,grimm2006odd}, we distinguish patterns
used for fitting from those reserved for checking the model.

\subsubsection*{Empirical Evidence on Buy Now, Pay Later}
\label{sec:lit-bnpl}

The evidence on increased spending complements the financial-distress findings above.
Di Maggio et al. \cite{dimaggio2022bnpl} find that \ac{BNPL} access raises spending for both
constrained and unconstrained users, beyond what intertemporal substitution explains.
Together with deHaan et al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl}, this motivates the model's complementarity
check: whether enabling \ac{BNPL} raises traditional debt-servicing stress
(Pattern~1, Table~\ref{tab:patterns}).
Hayashi and Routh \cite{hayashi2025constraints} find that users are more financially constrained
than non-users, while Wang \cite{wang2026buynow} reports use concentrated among lower-income
and lower-credit-score consumers.

Ackert et al. \cite{ackert2025bnpl} find that participants in an incentivised experiment are
more willing to finance purchases with \ac{BNPL} than with credit cards, and generally expect
peer approval. This motivates a want-driven borrowing path and peer influence on adoption.
Granovetter's threshold model \cite{granovetter1978threshold} shows how heterogeneous individual
thresholds can produce abrupt aggregate adoption. We use it as an alternative to the linear
peer rule, testing whether the adoption mechanism changes the default response.

\subsubsection*{Behavioural Foundations for the Decision Rules}
\label{sec:lit-behavioural}

Present bias predicts credit-card borrowing \cite{meier2010present}, and present-biased
borrowers often fail to make planned repayments \cite{Kuchler2021}. These findings motivate
shortfall-based borrowing and departures from scheduled repayment.
Keys and Wang \cite{Keys2019} find that 29\% of United States credit-card accounts pay at or
near the contractual minimum. Responses to changes in the payment formula also indicate
anchoring beyond liquidity constraints. We therefore assign households either a minimum-payment
or scheduled-payment rule, with the minimum-payer share set to 0.29 and varied in sensitivity
analysis.
These estimates come from United States borrowers. Their transfer to South African households
remains a limitation (Section~\ref{sec:lim-transfer}).

\subsubsection*{The Regulatory Setting, at Home and Abroad}
\label{sec:lit-regulatory}

The local regulatory framework determines the traditional lender's test and product costs.
Alongside the affordability rules described above, the maximum rate for unsecured transactions
has been the repurchase rate plus 21\% since May 2016 \cite{sa_ncr_ratecaps_2016}.
The model uses the applicable 2017 rate ceilings to construct debt service.

International regulation motivates the scenario comparison. The United Kingdom's Woolard Review
\cite{woolard2021} informed the \ac{FCA}'s rules for deferred payment credit, including
proportionate affordability checks \cite{fca_ps26_1}.
Australia's \ac{ASIC} review \cite{asic2020rep672} found that one in five surveyed users had
missed a payment in the preceding year under industry self-regulation.
We compare bureau visibility with affordability screening at origination, and test both with
and without peer influence on adoption.
