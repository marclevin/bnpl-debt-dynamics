\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Background}
\label{sec:background}

South Africa had substantial household credit distress before \ac{BNPL} spread. In early 2017,
14.2\% of unsecured and credit-facility accounts were more than 90 days in
arrears~\citep[computed from Tables 21 and 23]{ncr_ccmr_2017}, although the \ac{NCA} requires
lenders to assess affordability and Regulation 23A specifies a residual-income test for
it~\citep{sa_nca_2005,sa_ncr_affordability_2015}. By late 2025, 57\% of South African respondents
to a TransUnion consumer survey held a \ac{BNPL} product~\citep{transunion_cir_sa_2026}.
\ac{BNPL} providers often argue that
interest-free agreements fall outside the \ac{NCA}~\citep{sarb_fsr_2026_1}, a view legal
commentary shares~\citep{nortonrose_bnpl_sa}, and the agreements are not displayed on consumer
credit profiles~\citep{dcasa_bnpl_2026,finasa_bnpl_2026}. A lender that relies on the bureau record
therefore does not see them, although their instalments appear on bank statements, and a household
can owe several providers without any one of them seeing all its debts. The \ac{SARB} identifies
concurrent \ac{BNPL} obligations as a risk to household indebtedness and notes that approval rests
on less rigorous credit checks than traditional unsecured credit~\citep{sarb_fsr_2026_1}.

Reporting arrangements are changing. A press report describes an agreement between the \ac{NCR},
the \ac{SACRRA} and the Credit Bureau Association to include \ac{BNPL} payment behaviour in bureau
records, and the \ac{NCR}'s position that a \ac{BNPL} contract may become incidental credit when
default charges are levied~\citep{businessday_bnpl_2026}. The \ac{NCR} reportedly requires
reporting from February 2027, though no published \ac{NCR} instrument sets a date. We found no
South African study of how \ac{BNPL} affects household distress and no agent-based model of South
African household credit, and the new arrangements are too recent to evaluate. It is therefore not
known how much an unreported, lightly screened \ac{BNPL} channel adds to distress, or what making
it visible would change.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

The main research question is:

\begin{quote}
    In a model of South African households already exposed to credit distress, how does
    adding a \ac{BNPL} channel change that distress, and how do bureau visibility and
    affordability screening affect the outcome?
\end{quote}

Three subsidiary questions, RQ1 to RQ3, follow:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item How well does a synthetic household population built from South African survey
    microdata hold the 2017 arrears profile it starts from, and how does it perform
    against external comparisons not used in fitting or construction?
    \item How does enabling \ac{BNPL} change population default, and how does that change
    vary with access, peer adoption and platform count? How often do households owe several
    platforms at once when platforms cannot observe one another's exposures?
    \item How do bureau visibility and mandatory affordability screening change simulated
    borrowing and distress, both with and without peer influence on adoption?
\end{enumerate}

Our main measure of distress is default, in two forms (Section~\ref{sec:sim-params}). A household
defaults when an instalment on any of its credit, \ac{BNPL} included, is more than 90 days past due
(Section~\ref{sec:sequence}). We also report the 90+ share of credit-active households, the
counterpart of the 2017 account figure, and consumption distress, food and rent left unpaid.

\subsection{Approach and Findings}

We answer these questions with an \ac{ABM} of 5{,}000 households built from South African survey
microdata. A traditional lender applies the Regulation 23A test to the obligations the credit
bureau records, while four \ac{BNPL} platforms apply a light screen and cannot see one another's
balances. Each run starts in 2017, the year of \ac{NIDS} Wave~5 and before \ac{BNPL} spread, from
that year's arrears profile. With \ac{BNPL}
disabled, we fit two parameters that govern missed traditional instalments, payment friction and
arrears persistence, to the profile's five bands from 1--30 to more than 120 days in arrears. We
then enable \ac{BNPL} and compare outcomes over two years. Further arms, runs that differ in one
setting, vary access to \ac{BNPL}, peer influence on adoption, the number of platforms, bureau
visibility and screening. The experiments show which assumptions an effect depends on. They do not
identify the mechanisms at work in South African households, estimate the historical effect of
\ac{BNPL} or forecast default.

Enabling \ac{BNPL} raises default during the run by $1.03 \pm 0.06$ points without peer influence,
from 14.37\% (Section~\ref{sec:results-injection}). The rise comes from households that use
\ac{BNPL} to bridge cash shortfalls and then fall more than 90 days behind on it, most of them among
the 3.6\% whose surveyed income does not cover their food and rent. Those the lender's test never
lends to hold no debt and cannot default without \ac{BNPL}, but the platforms lend to them; they
account for most of the effect. For about two-thirds of the group's survey respondents, though, the
household's answer
to a single income question does cover food and rent, so the effect may partly reflect how the
survey records income (Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}).

The effect therefore depends most on two features of the model, that households can borrow with

out of the shortfall path, it is $-0.05 \pm 0.16$ points (Section~\ref{sec:results-effect}).

Bureau visibility, which adds \ac{BNPL} obligations to the lender's test, raises refusals by
about 13\% and default by $0.20 \pm 0.07$ points without peer influence, since in the model a
refused household has no other source of credit (Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}). With peer influence
the rise is not detectable, but the two estimates do not differ detectably. Visibility also leaves
more households short of food and rent.

Screening every \ac{BNPL} request with the same test lowers default by 0.93 to 1.03 points, about as
much as \ac{BNPL} adds and almost all in the poorest quintile, because it applies the test that
already keeps these households from the lender. Without peer influence it also leaves more
households short of food and rent, so whether screening makes households better off is open.

Peer influence acts only on want-driven purchases, those made without a cash shortfall. At the
strongest setting of the linear rule it raises
\ac{BNPL} volume per eligible household from R862 to R2{,}014 a year, but the effect on default
shows no detectable slope (Section~\ref{sec:results-beta}). Under the assumed routing, 28\% of
\ac{BNPL} users owe two or more platforms at some point, and default shows no systematic rise with
the number of platforms (Section~\ref{sec:results-stacking}).

The constructed population is close to external figures for household size and income inequality
and holds about half the
traditional debt of the corresponding \ac{CCMR} book. The arrears profile, an input and a
calibration target, holds as a share while the credit-active book halves. Opening debt service is above what households report, traditional
interest does not rise with \ac{BNPL} as it does in United States data, and no external figure
measures default directly (Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}). We therefore interpret mainly
differences between runs, each conditional on the assumptions the runs share.

\subsection{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

\subsubsection*{BNPL, Spending and Financial Strain}
\label{sec:lit-bnpl}

Di Maggio et al.~\citeyearpar{dimaggio2022bnpl} find that \ac{BNPL} access increases spending among
both constrained and unconstrained users, beyond what intertemporal substitution explains. If
\ac{BNPL} adds to spending, it may also strain other credit. Using banking records for 10.6 million
consumers, deHaan et al.~\citeyearpar{dehaan2024bnpl} find higher overdraft charges, credit-card
interest and late fees after adoption, with larger increases among heavier users. The users are
more financially constrained than non-users~\citep{hayashi2025constraints}, use is highest among
low-to-middle-income consumers~\citep{dimaggio2022bnpl}, and borrowers with subprime or deep
subprime credit scores accounted for most United States originations in 2021 and
2022~\citep[p.~2]{cfpb2025bnpl}. The model takes from this evidence the use of \ac{BNPL} against a
shortfall and reporting by income quintile, and uses the deHaan et al.\ result as an external check
(Table~\ref{tab:bnpl-on-checks}).

\subsubsection*{Behavioural Reasons for Borrowing and Adoption}
\label{sec:lit-behavioural}

Borrowing and repayment need not follow a fully forward-looking plan. Present bias predicts
credit-card borrowing~\citep{meier2010present}, and present-biased borrowers may fail to make their
planned repayments~\citep{Kuchler2021}. Keys and Wang~\citeyearpar{Keys2019} find that 29\% of
United States credit-card accounts regularly pay at or near the contractual minimum, and their
evidence on responses to changes in payment formulas suggests anchoring beyond what liquidity
constraints explain. Adoption may also depend on others. Students offered interest-free \ac{BNPL} in an incentivised experiment
buy a discretionary item more often than students offered a credit card, and \ac{BNPL} buyers
expect a small majority of others to approve~\citep{ackert2025bnpl}. In
Granovetter's~\citeyearpar{granovetter1978threshold} threshold model, heterogeneous individual
thresholds can produce abrupt changes in collective behaviour. The model takes from this evidence
want-driven borrowing, payment friction, a minimum-payment rule (Section~\ref{sec:lim-transfer})
and peer influence under a linear and a threshold rule.

\subsubsection*{Screening, Reporting and Borrowing from Several Lenders}
\label{sec:lit-regulatory}

Lenders that cannot see one another's exposures can each stay within their own limits while a
household's total debt grows. The \ac{CFPB} documents United States borrowers who hold several
\ac{BNPL} loans at once, including loans from different providers~\citep{cfpb2025bnpl}. The
Woolard Review~\citeyearpar{woolard2021} informed how the United Kingdom's \ac{FCA} regulates
deferred payment credit, including proportionate affordability checks~\citep{fca_ps26_1}, while
South Africa is moving to bureau reporting (Section~\ref{sec:background}). The model's platforms
cannot see one another's balances, and its scenarios compare the two responses.

\subsubsection*{Agent-Based Models of Household Credit}
\label{sec:lit-abm}

Axtell and Farmer~\citeyearpar{axtell2025abm} describe an \ac{ABM} as a population of agents,
usually heterogeneous, that follow explicit rules of behaviour. Models more common in economics
derive behaviour from utility maximisation and impose an equilibrium, often with a representative
agent. Agents in an \ac{ABM} need not optimise or hold rational expectations, and no equilibrium
is imposed. Aggregate outcomes follow from the agents' interactions and are found by simulation
over runs with different random seeds. Axtell and Farmer treat the method as a complement to
equilibrium models. The method suits this thesis because its households differ in income and
debt, and the traditional lender and the platforms observe different obligations. \acp{ABM} of
household credit derive aggregate distress from individual
borrowing decisions. In Cardaci's~\citeyearpar{cardaci2018inequality} model, rising inequality and
consumption emulation lead to debt-financed spending and eventually a financial crisis, but its
debt is collateralised. Two models are closest to this one (Table~\ref{tab:gap}). Hamill et al.~\citeyearpar{hamill2023creditcard} suggest, in a model of the
United Kingdom credit-card market, that longer interest-free promotions could raise indebtedness.
In Madeira's~\citeyearpar{madeira2018chile} model of Chilean households exposed to labour-income
shocks, a household defaults when it cannot finance minimum consumption. We take this cash-flow
view of a shortfall, but default here requires an instalment more than 90 days past due.

\subsubsection*{Contribution}

The thesis makes three contributions. First, it finds that where a regulated lender already tests
affordability, \ac{BNPL} raises default in the model mostly by lending to households that lender
refuses. Default that involves traditional arrears, the other credit on which deHaan et
al.~\citeyearpar{dehaan2024bnpl} find strain in United States data, does not rise detectably
(Section~\ref{sec:results-injection}). Second, it builds a model of South African household credit
from survey data in which the traditional lender and the \ac{BNPL} platforms observe and screen
under different rules, so the reporting gap can be switched on and off. Third, it compares bureau
reporting, acting through the lender's affordability test, with affordability checks on \ac{BNPL}
itself~\citep{fca_ps26_1}.
