\section{Background and Context}
\label{sec:background}

South Africa regulates consumer credit tightly. The \ac{NCA} \cite{sa_nca_2005} requires every
registered credit provider to assess whether an applicant can afford a proposed agreement before
granting it, and Regulation 23A of the Affordability Assessment Regulations
\cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015} prescribes a residual-income test for doing so. The Act also caps
the cost of credit by sub-sector \cite{sa_ncr_ratecaps_2016}. Registered lenders report their
exposures to the credit bureaus, so each lender assesses an applicant against a record of what
every other registered lender has advanced.

\ac{BNPL} providers sit outside that regime. Because they levy neither interest nor fees on the
deferred amount, they argue that they do not transact credit within the meaning of the \ac{NCA},
and so do not register with the \ac{NCR}, do not perform the Regulation 23A assessment, and do not
report what they have lent to any credit bureau \cite{nortonrose_bnpl_sa}. TransUnion, a consumer
credit bureau, confirms the absence of \ac{BNPL} information from South African credit reports \cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025}.
A registered lender running a lawful and diligent affordability assessment therefore computes
residual income from a liability record that is incomplete by regulatory construction. It cannot
see \ac{BNPL} obligations, and neither can any \ac{BNPL} provider see another's, so a household can
hold several concurrent facilities that no party to any of those agreements has assessed in
aggregate.

deHaan et al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl}, tracking banking records for 10.6 million United States consumers,
find that adopting \ac{BNPL} is followed by higher overdraft charges, credit-card interest and late fees,
with the effects concentrated among liquidity-constrained consumers.
The \ac{CFPB} \cite{cfpb2025bnpl} found that across 2021 and 2022, 63\% of \ac{BNPL} borrowers across
five of the largest \ac{BNPL} platforms in the United States, held simultaneous loans at some point and 32\% held them across different firms.
South Africa has no equivalent evidence base, and the reporting exemption for \ac{BNPL} firms makes it
unobservable, since no party, including the regulator, can measure aggregate \ac{BNPL} exposure
from administrative data.

\section{Research Questions and Objectives}
\label{sec:rqs}

\subsection{Research questions}

This thesis is guided by the following primary research question:

\begin{quote}
    How does the availability of multiple concurrent \ac{BNPL} facilities affect the trajectory of
    consumer financial distress among \ac{LMI} South African households, and which platform-level
    interventions reduce population-level default?
\end{quote}

This is decomposed into three subsidiary questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Under what conditions does debt stacking become self-reinforcing?
    \item Does the population-level default rate respond non-linearly to the level of \ac{BNPL}
    access, and if so, under what conditions?
    \item Do ``cool-off period'' or ``anti-stacking'' interventions meaningfully alter outcomes, or do agents simply
    defer rather than desist?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research objectives}

The questions above resolve into five objectives.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Construct a representative household population for South Africa in 2017 from survey microdata, with a
    complete balance sheet per household.
    \item Specify an agent-based model of that population containing a regulated, bureau-informed
    lender class and a lender class exempt from both requirements.
    \item Calibrate the population's credit behaviour, with \ac{BNPL} disabled, against the 2017
    first-quarter \ac{CCMR} \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017}.
    \item Inject the \ac{BNPL} lender class and measure the change in arrears, servicing burden,
    stacking depth and population default across sweeps of \ac{BNPL} access, platform count and
    peer-influence strength.
    \item Evaluate four platform-level interventions against that baseline.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This work proceeds in three stages.

The first constructs the household population. \ac{NIDS} Wave 5, conducted in 2017, supplies the
balance sheet and demographics for 10{,}841 valid households, and \ac{FinScope} 2019 supplies four
financial-inclusion flags through a cell-donor match. The matched population is resampled with
probability proportional to survey weight to a fixed 5{,}000 agents, all in 2017 Rands
(Chapter~\ref{ch:data}).

The second stage specifies the model under the \ac{ODD} protocol \cite{grimm2006odd, grimm2020odd},
with the patterns that judge its realism registered before any result is presented. Households
borrow on realised shortfall, repay by salience instead of by optimisation, and adopt \ac{BNPL}
partly in response to adoption within their reference group. The traditional lender applies
Regulation 23A against a bureau record containing traditional debt only, while \ac{BNPL} platforms
apply a light screen, cannot see one another, and are invisible to the bureau.

The third stage runs the experiment. The baseline is calibrated with \ac{BNPL} disabled, \ac{BNPL}
is injected, and outcomes are swept over access rate, platform count, peer-influence strength and
the four intervention levers. Because the peer-influence coefficient cannot be calibrated from any
South African source, every result is reported as a surface in which the zero-influence row is
shown, so that non-linearity attributable to social transmission can be distinguished from
non-linearity that is not.

\section{Research Outcomes}
\label{sec:outcomes}

The thesis is expected to deliver four things: a reusable, survey-grounded synthetic population of
South African households with complete balance sheets in 2017 Rands and its validation record; an
agent-based model containing a regulated and an unregulated lender class over that population,
specified to \ac{ODD} standard; an estimate, internal to the model, of how much of the change in
household distress under \ac{BNPL} is attributable to the reporting exemption itself, obtained by
comparing runs with bureau visibility switched on and off; and a ranking of four platform-level
interventions by their effect on population default.
