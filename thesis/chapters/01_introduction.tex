\section{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\subsection{Background and Context}
\label{sec:background}

South Africa entered the \ac{BNPL} era with a household sector already heavily indebted under a
tightly regulated credit regime. The \ac{NCA} \cite{sa_nca_2005} requires every registered credit
provider to assess whether an applicant can afford a proposed agreement, Regulation 23A of the
National Credit Regulations \cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015} prescribes a residual-income test for
doing so, and registered lenders report their exposures to the credit bureaus, so each assesses an
applicant against what every other has advanced. Even so, 14.2\% of unsecured and credit-facility
accounts were more than 90 days in arrears in early 2017 \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017}, and in late 2025
36\% of surveyed consumers expected to miss a bill or loan payment \cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025}.
This thesis asks what adding a further credit channel to such a population does, and what the
regulatory response now under way is likely to achieve.

\ac{BNPL} providers have operated outside that regime. Levying neither interest nor fees on the
deferred amount, they have argued that they do not transact credit within the meaning of the
\ac{NCA}, and so have neither registered with the \ac{NCR}, nor performed the Regulation 23A
assessment, nor reported what they lend to any bureau, from whose files \ac{BNPL} was still absent
in 2025 \cite{nortonrose_bnpl_sa, transunion_cps_sa_2025}. A registered lender running a diligent affordability assessment
has therefore computed residual income from a liability record incomplete by regulatory
construction, and since no \ac{BNPL} provider could see another's, a household could hold several
concurrent facilities that no party had assessed in aggregate. The \ac{SARB}'s June 2026
Financial Stability Review names exactly this mechanism: the ability to hold multiple concurrent
\ac{BNPL} obligations, approved on less rigorous checks than traditional unsecured credit,
``increases the risk of over-indebtedness'' \cite{sarb_fsr_2026_1}.

That position is now changing. The \ac{NCR} has taken the view that a \ac{BNPL} agreement, while
not a credit agreement at inception, may become an incidental credit agreement once default
charges are levied, and has concluded a memorandum of agreement with \ac{SACRRA} and the Credit
Bureau Association under which providers are to report consumer payment behaviour to the bureaus
\cite{businessday_bnpl_2026}. Providers began submitting records in 2026, but the data are not
yet displayed on consumer profiles while the format is finalised \cite{dcasa_bnpl_2026}; press
reports put their appearance at February 2027, a date not yet gazetted
\cite{businessday_bnpl_2026}. One bureau's preliminary analysis suggests the new data could lower
the scores of roughly a quarter of credit-active consumers under current scoring models
\cite{finasa_bnpl_2026}. The requirement arrives before any evidence of what it will do.

Such evidence exists abroad. deHaan et al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl}, tracking banking records for
10.6 million United States consumers, find that adopting \ac{BNPL} is followed by higher overdraft
charges, credit-card interest and late fees, concentrated among the liquidity-constrained; the
\ac{CFPB} \cite{cfpb2025bnpl} finds that 63\% of \ac{BNPL} borrowers held simultaneous loans at
some point and 32\% held them across different firms. South Africa has no equivalent evidence
base, and years of non-reporting mean none can be assembled from administrative data for the
period in which the exposure built up.

This thesis builds an \ac{ABM} of the South African household credit market: 5{,}000 synthetic
households, constructed from national survey microdata and subject to the statutory rules above,
earn, spend, borrow, repay and default over a simulated two-year horizon. \ac{BNPL} platforms with
the observed South African contract structure are injected into an otherwise unchanged 2017
environment, so that any difference between the two runs is attributable to the new lender class.
Platforms invisible to the bureau and to one another are the model's status quo; the reporting
requirement is a switch. The thesis contributes a survey-grounded model of South African household
credit, a quantified account of what non-reporting did to household distress, and an ex-ante
evaluation of the reporting requirement against the affordability screening adopted abroad,
including whether either answer depends on how adoption spreads.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

\begin{quote}
    In a household population already carrying substantial credit distress, what does the
    addition of a \ac{BNPL} lending channel outside the affordability and reporting regime do to
    that distress, and what is the mandated extension of credit-bureau reporting to \ac{BNPL}
    likely to achieve?
\end{quote}

The model represents the reporting requirement as the visibility of \ac{BNPL} obligations to a
lender's affordability assessment. It carries no credit score, so the second channel through which
the requirement may act, a change in consumers' scores, is outside its scope
(Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry}); the second half of the question is answered for the affordability
channel only. Three subsidiary questions decompose it.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can a synthetic household population built from South African survey microdata reproduce
    credit-market behaviour it was not fitted to?
    \item Does the mutual invisibility of \ac{BNPL} platforms produce concurrent-facility
    stacking, and does population default rise with the number of platforms a household can stack
    across?
    \item How do three scenarios for the \ac{BNPL} lending environment, bureau visibility,
    mandatory affordability screening, and socially transmitted adoption, change the resulting
    distress and default?
\end{enumerate}

Where distress concentrates in the income distribution is a finding, not a scope restriction: the
empirical record places \ac{BNPL} harm among lower-income, liquidity-constrained consumers
\cite{hayashi2025constraints, wang2026buynow}, so results are reported by income quintile
throughout.

The first question is a precondition for the other two: a model whose households do not behave
like South African households cannot support a claim about what happens when a new lender class is
introduced to them. It is answered by the population construction of Section~\ref{ch:population}
and the baseline results of Section~\ref{ch:results}; the second and third are answered in
Section~\ref{ch:results}.
