\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Background and Context}
\label{sec:background}

South Africa already had substantial household credit distress before the recent expansion of
\ac{BNPL}. In early 2017, 14.2\% of unsecured and credit-facility accounts were more than
90 days in arrears \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017}. This was within a regulated lending market: the
\ac{NCA} requires affordability assessments, and Regulation 23A specifies a residual-income
test for whether a borrower can afford additional credit
\cite{sa_nca_2005,sa_ncr_affordability_2015}. In a late-2025 survey, 36\% of consumers
expected to miss a bill or loan payment \cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025}. The two figures
measure different outcomes, account arrears and expected missed payments, but each
indicates households with limited capacity to absorb new obligations.

The concern examined here is that \ac{BNPL} credit may sit outside the information
lenders use to assess affordability. Providers have
argued that interest-free agreements fall outside the \ac{NCA}, and \ac{BNPL} payment
histories were absent from South African credit reports in 2025
\cite{nortonrose_bnpl_sa,transunion_cps_sa_2025}. A household could therefore take on
obligations with several providers without any one lender seeing the full amount it had to
repay. The \ac{SARB}'s June 2026 Financial Stability Review identifies concurrent \ac{BNPL}
obligations and less rigorous affordability checks as risks to household indebtedness
\cite{sarb_fsr_2026_1}.

Reporting arrangements are now changing. Reports describe an agreement between the
\ac{NCR}, \ac{SACRRA} and the Credit Bureau Association to include \ac{BNPL} payment
behaviour in bureau records \cite{businessday_bnpl_2026}. They also describe the
\ac{NCR}'s position that an agreement may become incidental credit when default charges
are levied. Providers reportedly began submitting records in 2026 while the reporting
format was being finalised \cite{dcasa_bnpl_2026}. February 2027 has been reported as the
expected date for records to appear on consumer profiles \cite{businessday_bnpl_2026};
we treat this as a reported timetable, not a confirmed statutory commencement date.
FINASA also reports preliminary analysis by one bureau suggesting potential score
reductions for roughly a quarter of credit-active consumers \cite{finasa_bnpl_2026}.
These developments raise the question of how far making \ac{BNPL} credit visible to
lenders changes household distress.

Using banking records
for 10.6 million United States consumers, deHaan et al.\ \cite{dehaan2024bnpl} find higher
overdraft charges, credit-card interest and late fees following \ac{BNPL} adoption,
particularly among liquidity-constrained consumers. The \ac{CFPB} \cite{cfpb2025bnpl}
also documents widespread simultaneous borrowing, including borrowing across providers.
Comparable South African evidence is limited, especially for the period before bureau
reporting, so the size of these effects in South Africa, and the likely effect of local
reporting arrangements, remain unmeasured.

This thesis examines these mechanisms using an \ac{ABM} of 5{,}000 households constructed
from South African survey microdata. We first calibrate a traditional-credit baseline to
selected features of the 2017 arrears profile, with \ac{BNPL} disabled. We then introduce
\ac{BNPL} into the same environment and compare outcomes over a simulated two-year
horizon. Further experiments vary the number of platforms, access to \ac{BNPL}, peer
influence, bureau visibility and affordability screening.

The experiments isolate the effect of these changes within a specified model of household
finances. Estimating the historical effect of \ac{BNPL} in South Africa, or forecasting
default under the new reporting arrangements, is outside the scope of this thesis.
The model allows us to examine
how additional obligations interact with existing debt, cash buffers and income shocks,
while making the assumptions behind those interactions explicit.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

The main research question is:

\begin{quote}
    In a model of South African households already exposed to credit distress, how does
    adding a \ac{BNPL} channel change that distress, and how do bureau visibility and
    affordability screening affect the outcome?
\end{quote}

Because the model has no credit score, its reporting experiment covers only the
affordability channel of bureau reporting (Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry}).
Three subsidiary questions, referred to as RQ1 to RQ3, guide the analysis:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item How well does a synthetic household population built from South African survey
    microdata reproduce the selected baseline arrears bands, and how does it perform
    against benchmarks not used in fitting or construction?
    \item When \ac{BNPL} platforms cannot observe one another's exposures, does
    cross-platform stacking emerge, and how do platform count, access and peer adoption
    affect population default?
    \item How do bureau visibility and mandatory affordability screening change simulated
    borrowing and distress, both with and without peer influence on adoption?
\end{enumerate}

We report outcomes by income quintile because empirical studies identify financial
constraints and lower income as relevant characteristics of \ac{BNPL} users
\cite{hayashi2025constraints,wang2026buynow}. Section~\ref{sec:calibration} addresses
the first question through population checks, calibration and validation.
Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:scenarios} address the remaining questions
through the simulation experiments.

\subsection{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

\subsubsection*{Agent-Based Models of Household Credit}
\label{sec:lit-abm}

Household credit models provide a way to connect individual borrowing decisions to
aggregate financial distress. Cardaci \cite{cardaci2018inequality} models rising
inequality and consumption emulation, leading to debt-financed spending and eventually
a banking crisis. That model concerns collateralised debt, so its mechanism differs
from unsecured pay-in-four lending. D'Orazio and Giulioni \cite{dorazio2017micro}
show how unsustainable debt can arise from local borrowing rules. These
studies support examining aggregate outcomes through explicit household decisions,
rather than assuming that the consequences of additional credit are uniform.

Madeira \cite{madeira2018chile} models Chilean households exposed to labour-income
shocks, with default arising when they cannot finance minimum consumption. We adopt
a cash-flow approach for the same reason: a household's ability to meet its
obligations depends on the income and reserves available when payments fall due.
This is also consistent with the residual-income basis of the affordability test.
Madeira's comparison with observed default rates provides a precedent for checking
a household credit model against evidence from a middle-income economy.

Hamill et al.\ \cite{hamill2023creditcard} find that promotional strategies increase
indebtedness in a model of the United Kingdom credit-card market. Their model also
produces a non-monotonic debt-to-income profile, with the highest burden among
middle-income households. We use this result only as a cross-model plausibility check. Following Windrum, Fagiolo and
Moneta \cite{windrum2007validation} and the pattern-oriented approach discussed in
the \ac{ODD} literature \cite{grimm2020odd,grimm2006odd}, we distinguish patterns
used to fit the model from those used to assess it afterwards.

\subsubsection*{Empirical Evidence on Buy Now, Pay Later}
\label{sec:lit-bnpl}

Di Maggio et al.\ \cite{dimaggio2022bnpl} find that \ac{BNPL} access increases
spending among both constrained and unconstrained users, beyond what intertemporal
substitution explains. Together with the financial-distress findings of deHaan et al.\
\cite{dehaan2024bnpl}, this motivates examining whether additional \ac{BNPL}
obligations increase stress on traditional debt. The model's complementarity check
assesses this relationship through traditional arrears and interest payments
(Pattern~1, Table~\ref{tab:patterns}).

Hayashi and Routh
\cite{hayashi2025constraints} find that \ac{BNPL} users are more financially
constrained than non-users, while Wang \cite{wang2026buynow} reports use concentrated
among lower-income and lower-credit-score consumers. These findings motivate the
distributional analysis.

Ackert et al.\ \cite{ackert2025bnpl} find that participants in an incentivised
experiment are more willing to finance purchases with \ac{BNPL} than with credit
cards, and generally expect peer approval. This supports allowing purchases that
are not driven solely by an immediate cash shortfall, and exploring peer influence
on adoption. Granovetter's threshold model \cite{granovetter1978threshold} provides a second
adoption mechanism: heterogeneous individual thresholds can produce abrupt changes
in collective behaviour. We compare a threshold rule with a linear peer rule to
examine whether the default response depends on how adoption spreads.

\subsubsection*{Behavioural Foundations for the Decision Rules}
\label{sec:lit-behavioural}

Borrowing and repayment need not follow a fully forward-looking plan. Present bias
predicts credit-card borrowing \cite{meier2010present}, and present-biased borrowers
may fail to make their planned repayments \cite{Kuchler2021}. This evidence motivates
allowing departures from scheduled repayment. The borrowing-amount rule and the
payment-friction probability are set separately, in Sections~\ref{sec:amount}
and~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}.

Keys and Wang \cite{Keys2019} find that 29\% of United States credit-card accounts
pay at or near the contractual minimum. Responses to changes in payment formulas
also suggest anchoring beyond what liquidity constraints alone explain. We represent
this distinction by assigning households either a minimum-payment or a
scheduled-payment rule. Applying this account-level estimate from United States
credit-card borrowers to South African households is a transfer assumption
(Section~\ref{sec:lim-transfer}).

\subsubsection*{The Regulatory Setting}
\label{sec:lit-regulatory}

South African regulation informs the traditional lender's affordability test and
the construction of debt service. The applicable unsecured-credit interest ceiling
in 2017 was the repurchase rate plus 21 percentage points
\cite{sa_ncr_ratecaps_2016}. We use the statutory ceilings as proxies for the
product rates households paid.

International regulation motivates the comparison of alternative lending rules.
The United Kingdom's Woolard Review \cite{woolard2021} informed the \ac{FCA}'s
approach to deferred payment credit, including proportionate affordability checks
\cite{fca_ps26_1}. Australia's \ac{ASIC} review \cite{asic2020rep672} found that
one in five surveyed users had missed a payment in the preceding year under
industry self-regulation. These sources motivate comparing a lightly screened
\ac{BNPL} channel with mandatory screening, which the model implements through the
South African residual-income test. We separately test bureau visibility, and run both comparisons with and without
peer influence.

\subsubsection*{Synthesis and Gap}
\label{sec:lit-gap}

The literature establishes reasons for concern about additional borrowing,
uneven household vulnerability and incomplete lender information. The empirical
\ac{BNPL} and behavioural evidence comes mainly from the United States, the United
Kingdom and Australia and supplies no South African parameter values; where the
model draws on it, the transfer is treated as an assumption
(Section~\ref{sec:lim-transfer}). The reviewed studies do not examine how these
mechanisms interact in a South African household population in which traditional
lenders and \ac{BNPL} platforms operate under different information and screening
rules. This thesis addresses that question by combining
survey-derived household finances with explicit lender-specific decisions.
