\section{Background and Context}
\label{sec:background}

South Africa regulates consumer credit tightly. The \ac{NCA} \cite{sa_nca_2005} requires every
registered credit provider to assess whether an applicant can afford a proposed agreement before
granting it, and Regulation 23A of the Affordability Assessment Regulations
\cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015} prescribes how. The test is one of residual income: gross income,
less statutory deductions, less a prescribed table of minimum necessary living expenses, less
every other committed obligation the consumer discloses or the credit bureau records. What
survives that subtraction is what the new instalment must fit inside. The Act also caps the cost
of credit by sub-sector \cite{sa_ncr_ratecaps_2016}. Registered lenders report their exposures to
the credit bureaux, so each lender assesses an applicant against a record of what every other
registered lender has advanced.

\ac{BNPL} providers sit outside that regime. Because they levy neither interest nor fees on the
deferred amount, they argue that they do not transact credit within the meaning of the \ac{NCA}.
The consequences follow directly: no registration with the \ac{NCR}, no Regulation 23A
assessment, and no obligation to report what they have lent to any credit bureau
\cite{nortonrose_bnpl_sa}. TransUnion confirms the last point from inside the bureau system,
listing \ac{BNPL} history among the categories of information absent from the standard South
African credit report \cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025}.

This produces an unusual market structure. A registered lender running a lawful and diligent
affordability assessment computes residual income from a liability record that is incomplete by
regulatory construction. It cannot see \ac{BNPL} obligations, and neither can any \ac{BNPL}
provider see another's. A household can therefore hold several concurrent facilities that no
party to any of those agreements has assessed in aggregate.

The international evidence suggests this matters. deHaan et al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl}, tracking
banking records for 10.6 million United States consumers, find that adopting \ac{BNPL} is followed
by higher overdraft charges, credit-card interest and late fees, with the effects concentrated
among liquidity-constrained consumers. The \ac{CFPB} \cite{cfpb2025bnpl} finds concurrent
borrowing to be the norm and not the exception: across 2021 and 2022, 63 percent of \ac{BNPL}
borrowers held simultaneous loans at some point and 32 percent held them across different firms.
South Africa has no equivalent evidence base, and the reporting exemption that makes the question
urgent is the same feature that makes it unobservable. Nobody, including the regulator, can
measure aggregate \ac{BNPL} exposure from administrative data.

That is the situation this thesis models.

\section{Research Questions and Objectives}
\label{sec:rqs}

\subsection{Research questions}

This thesis is guided by the following primary research question:

\begin{quote}
    How does the availability of multiple concurrent \ac{BNPL} facilities affect the trajectory of
    consumer financial distress among \ac{LMI} South African households, and which platform-level
    interventions reduce population-level default?
\end{quote}

This is decomposed into three subsidiary questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Under what conditions does debt stacking become self-reinforcing?
    \item Does the population-level default rate respond non-linearly to the level of \ac{BNPL}
    access, and if so, under what conditions?
    \item Do ``cool-off period'' interventions meaningfully alter outcomes, or do agents simply
    defer rather than desist?
\end{enumerate}

A word on what these questions deliberately do not ask. An earlier formulation asked about
\emph{systemic default cascades}, and the model cannot answer that. It contains a single
non-adaptive traditional lender with no balance sheet that can fail, no interbank exposure, no
feedback from household default to employment or income, and a fully static macro environment
(Submodel~16). Default here is an individual cash-flow event, and the only household-to-household
channel transmits \ac{BNPL} \emph{adoption}, not distress. What the model can produce is a
correlated rise in independent defaults across a population, which is the phenomenon the questions
above name. Genuine contagion channels are set out as future work in
Section~\ref{sec:future-work}.

\subsection{Research objectives}

The questions above resolve into five objectives.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Construct a household population for South Africa in 2017 from survey microdata, with a
    complete balance sheet per household, and not from assumed distributions.
    \item Specify an agent-based model of that population containing two lender classes: one
    regulated, bureau-informed and subject to Regulation 23A, and one exempt from all three.
    \item Calibrate the population's credit behaviour, with \ac{BNPL} disabled, against the 2017
    first-quarter \ac{CCMR} \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017}.
    \item Inject the \ac{BNPL} lender class and measure the change in arrears, servicing burden,
    stacking depth and population default across sweeps of \ac{BNPL} access, platform count and
    peer-influence strength.
    \item Evaluate four platform-level interventions against that baseline, three of which
    correspond to instruments that exist in other jurisdictions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Claim: A Counterfactual Injection}
\label{sec:injection}

This model is a counterfactual experiment, not a historical fit, and the distinction governs every
result reported in this thesis.

A household population is calibrated to observed South African conditions in 2017, a period in
which \ac{BNPL} was effectively absent from the South African market. That population's credit
behaviour is validated against the 2017 first-quarter \ac{NCR} \ac{CCMR} \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017}. A
\ac{BNPL} lender class is then \emph{injected} into the calibrated economy and the change in
dynamics is observed. The model therefore answers what \ac{BNPL} does to a household population of
this kind, and not what happened in South Africa in any particular later year.

The 2017 anchor is a control, not a limitation. \ac{BNPL} reached the South African market at
scale from roughly 2021, so any attempt to fit the model to its actual arrival would have to
separate that arrival from pandemic income shocks, the 2020 credit contraction and post-pandemic
inflation, none of which this thesis is equipped to identify. The absence of \ac{BNPL} in 2017 is
what makes the injection interpretable.

Three restrictions follow, and they are binding. No model output is claimed to describe an actual
year after 2017. No model output is compared to post-2020 observed arrears. Results are read as
directional and mechanistic --- ``stacking becomes self-reinforcing under conditions $X$'' ---
and never as forecasts.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The work proceeds in three stages.

The first constructs the household population. \ac{NIDS} Wave 5, conducted in 2017, supplies the
backbone: income and its dominant source, committed and discretionary expenditure, a liquid
savings proxy, consolidated debt, household composition, head demographics and survey weights, for
10{,}841 valid households. \ac{FinScope} 2019 supplies four financial-inclusion flags through a
cell-donor match on per-capita income quintile crossed with province. The matched population is
then resampled with probability proportional to survey weight to a fixed 5{,}000 agents. Monthly
debt servicing, which neither survey records, is constructed by amortising each household's
balance at the \ac{NCA} statutory maximum prescribed rate for the relevant sub-sector, evaluated
at the 2017 repurchase rate, and capping the result by the Regulation 23A residual-income test.
Everything stays in 2017 Rands. Chapter~\ref{ch:data} gives the construction and its validation.

The second stage specifies the model. Chapter~\ref{ch:design} does this using the \ac{ODD}
protocol \cite{grimm2006odd, grimm2020odd}, with the patterns that judge the model's realism
registered before any result is presented. Households borrow on realised shortfall, repay by
salience instead of by optimisation, and adopt \ac{BNPL} partly in response to adoption within
their reference group. The traditional lender applies Regulation 23A against a bureau record
containing traditional debt only. \ac{BNPL} platforms apply a light screen, cannot see one
another, and are invisible to the bureau.

The third stage runs the experiment. The baseline is calibrated with \ac{BNPL} disabled, \ac{BNPL}
is injected, and outcomes are swept over access rate, platform count, peer-influence strength and
the four intervention levers. Because the peer-influence coefficient cannot be calibrated from any
South African source, every result is reported as a surface in which the zero-influence row is
always shown, so that any non-linearity attributable to social transmission can be distinguished
from non-linearity that is not.

\section{Research Outcomes}
\label{sec:outcomes}

The thesis is expected to deliver four things.

A reusable, survey-grounded synthetic population of South African households with complete balance
sheets in 2017 Rands, together with its validation record. An agent-based model containing a
regulated and an unregulated lender class over that population, specified to \ac{ODD} standard.
An estimate, within the model, of how much of the change in household distress under \ac{BNPL} is
attributable to the reporting exemption itself, obtained by comparing runs with bureau visibility
switched on and off. And a ranking of four platform-level interventions by their effect on
population default, with the cool-off lever evaluated specifically on whether it reduces cumulative
borrowing volume or merely redistributes it over time.

What the thesis will not deliver is a forecast of South African \ac{BNPL} outcomes in any actual
year, a calibrated estimate of peer-influence strength, or any claim about interaction between
\ac{BNPL} stress and a macroeconomic downturn. Chapter~\ref{ch:limitations} sets out the full
boundary.

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:outline}

Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} reviews four literatures: agent-based models of household credit,
which establish the method; the empirical evidence on \ac{BNPL}, which establishes the phenomenon;
the behavioural economics of borrowing and repayment, which supplies the decision rules; and the
South African regulatory setting, which supplies the mechanism. It closes by identifying the gap
this thesis occupies.

Chapter~\ref{ch:data} constructs and validates the household population.
Chapter~\ref{ch:design} specifies the model under the \ac{ODD} protocol.
Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} describes the implementation and the experimental protocol.
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports results against each research question in turn.
Chapter~\ref{ch:limitations} states the limitations, several of which are structural and were
adopted knowingly. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} concludes and sets out future work.
Appendix~\ref{app:rationale} records the design alternatives considered and rejected, and
Appendix~\ref{app:specification} holds the full parameter register.
