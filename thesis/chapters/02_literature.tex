\section{Existing Literature on Household Credit and Buy Now, Pay Later}
\label{ch:literature}

\subsection{Agent-Based Models of Household Credit}
\label{sec:lit-abm}

Distress at the level of a population is more than the sum of distress at the level of a household,
which is the case for modelling it from the bottom up. Cardaci \cite{cardaci2018inequality} builds
a hybrid agent-based stock-flow-consistent model in which heterogeneous households and banks
interact through a credit network, and finds that the link between inequality, household debt and
financial instability runs through the structure of that network rather than through aggregate
leverage, with consumption following an \emph{expenditure cascade} rule so that a banking crisis
emerges endogenously. D'Orazio and Giulioni \cite{dorazio2017micro} reach a similar conclusion from
a different direction, unsustainable debt emerging from local rules rather than being written in.
Both supply the methodological premise of this thesis, since the mechanism under investigation, a
single household holding obligations to several lenders none of whom can observe the others, exists
only on the individual balance sheet and cannot be expressed by a representative agent.

Madeira \cite{madeira2018chile}, at the Central Bank of Chile, models households that absorb
labour-income shocks, choose among loan contracts, and default when they can no longer finance
minimum consumption. That default condition is a cash-flow insolvency test rather than a leverage
threshold, and it is adopted here because the \ac{NCA} affordability assessment is itself a
residual-income test. Madeira also validates against observed Chilean default rates, demonstrating
that a household-level credit \ac{ABM} in a middle-income economy can be held to external
behavioural targets.

Hamill et al. \cite{hamill2023creditcard} model the United Kingdom credit-card market and find that
promotional strategies chosen by profit-maximising lenders raise borrower indebtedness. Their model
also reproduces a non-monotonic relationship between income and debt-to-income ratio, with
middle-income households carrying the highest values, which is used here as an independent
validation target.

\subsection{Empirical Evidence on Buy Now, Pay Later}
\label{sec:lit-bnpl}
As established, deHaan et al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl} demonstrate that \ac{BNPL} adoption exacerbates financial distress among liquidity-constrained consumers.
Their conclusion, that \ac{BNPL} inherently facilitates overborrowing, provides the empirical rationale for testing these mechanisms in a South African context. 

While deHaan et al. outline the financial fallout, the \ac{CFPB} \cite{cfpb2025bnpl} illustrates how this exposure can build up. Concurrent borrowing is an established pattern: between 2021 and 2022, 63\% of \ac{BNPL} users held simultaneous loans, and nearly a third held them across entirely different providers. These figures form the foundation for the debt-stacking mechanism. When a consumer holds several obligations at once, across providers who cannot see each other's ledgers, what appears like an agrarian, interest-free deferral on a single transaction can become an aggregate servicing burden that no individual lender ever assessed.

We know this distress is heavily concentrated in \ac{LMI} populations \cite{hayashi2025constraints, wang2026buynow}, but Ackert et al. \cite{ackert2025bnpl} help explain the demand side. In an incentivised experiment, they found consumers prefer \ac{BNPL} over traditional credit cards for the same purchase, largely because they expect their social networks to approve of it. The idea that social approval directly drives up indebtedness provides the behavioral justification for both the want-driven borrowing path and the peer effects modeled in this paper.

\subsection{Behavioural Foundations for the Decision Rules}
\label{sec:lit-behavioural}

Experimentally elicited present bias is a strong predictor of credit-card borrowing \cite{meier2010present}. This justifies using a borrowing trigger that reacts to an immediate financial shortfall rather than a strict lifetime budget constraint. The exact same logic applies to repayment, as present-biased borrowers systematically fail to execute the debt paydowns they originally planned \cite{Kuchler2021}. 

Furthermore, Keys and Wang \cite{Keys2019} note that 29\% of United States credit-card accounts regularly pay at or near the contractual minimum. Crucially, at least 22\% of these near-minimum payers adjust their payments whenever the minimum formula changes, providing a clear signal of anchoring. 

Because of this, scheduled amortisation is rejected as a repayment rule in this model. A substantial minority of borrowers simply pay the salient minimum amount instead of the amount actually required to retire the debt, causing their obligations to persist and compound. Taken together, these findings describe an agent that borrows based on immediate shortfall, repays based on salience, and naturally varies in how strongly it does both.

\subsection{The South African Regulatory Setting}
\label{sec:lit-regulatory}

As outlined in Section~\ref{sec:background}, the \ac{NCA} \cite{sa_nca_2005} and Regulation 23A \cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015} define the mandatory residual income test that registered credit providers must use. While the mechanics of that affordability test are established, it is also important to note that the \ac{NCA} caps the cost of standard credit. For unsecured transactions, the maximum rate in force since May 2016 is the repurchase rate plus 21\% \cite{sa_ncr_ratecaps_2016}, which defines the cost boundaries for the traditional credit products in this simulation.

Building on the regulatory exemptions claimed by \ac{BNPL} providers discussed earlier, two specific points directly shape the architecture of this model. First, while local data is scarce, in 2025, TransUnion \cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025} reports that 20\% of South African consumers intend to apply for \ac{BNPL}, trailing only credit cards at 30\% and personal loans at 28\%. This provides the baseline for simulating local demand. Second, because the invisibility of \ac{BNPL} obligations is a structural feature of the South African regulatory perimeter, credit bureau visibility is programmed into the model as a switchable policy lever rather than a fixed constant. This allows the simulation to test exactly what happens when that regulatory blind spot is removed.

The South African regulatory perimeter effectively creates a dual credit market. Traditional lenders operate strictly within the bounds of mandatory affordability tests and shared data, while an invisible counterpart operates completely outside of it, lending to the exact same households. 

Because the administrative data required to measure this dynamic is not presently avaliable, and standard empirical methods are limited in their ability to capture the full scope of this phenomenon, bridging this gap requires a structural approach. If we cannot observe this aggregate exposure in the real world, we must simulate the environment where it occurs. To do this, the following section details the construction of a synthetic South African household population, designed from the bottom up to model how these overlapping, invisible liabilities actually behave.
