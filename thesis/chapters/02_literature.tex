\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literature}

Four bodies of work inform this thesis. Agent-based models of household credit establish the
method. The empirical work on \ac{BNPL} establishes the phenomenon, above all the concurrent
borrowing that motivates the research question. Behavioural economics supplies the agent decision
rules. The South African regulatory literature supplies the mechanism, since \ac{BNPL} occupies a
position here quite unlike the one it holds in the jurisdictions the empirical work describes.

\section{Agent-Based Models of Household Credit}
\label{sec:lit-abm}

Distress at the level of a population is more than the sum of distress at the level of a household,
which is the case for modelling it from the bottom up. Cardaci \cite{cardaci2018inequality} builds
a hybrid agent-based stock-flow-consistent model in which heterogeneous households and banks
interact through a credit network, and finds that the link between inequality, household debt and
financial instability runs through the structure of that network rather than through aggregate
leverage. Household consumption there follows a behavioural rule based on \emph{expenditure
cascades}, so that rising inequality exerts upward pressure on the consumption norms of lower and
middle-income households. That social pressure drives the debt-financed consumption boom, and the
accumulation of non-performing loans that follows produces a banking crisis endogenously. Peer
influence in this literature is part of the mechanism that generates the crisis, not an optional
embellishment.

D'Orazio and Giulioni \cite{dorazio2017micro} reach a compatible conclusion from a different
direction. Households in their model form beliefs about future employment, set desired consumption
accordingly, and approach the bank when consumption outruns income; the bank rations credit
proportionally once lending exceeds a multiple of deposits. Unsustainable debt and financial
fragility emerge from those local rules without being written into them. Both papers support the
methodological premise of this thesis, since the mechanism under investigation, a single household
holding obligations to several lenders none of whom can observe the others, exists only on the
individual balance sheet and cannot be expressed by a representative agent.

Two further models supply procedural precedent. Madeira \cite{madeira2018chile} is the closest
analogue in both method and setting. Writing at the Central Bank of Chile, he models households
that absorb labour-income shocks, choose among available loan contracts, and default when they can
no longer finance minimum consumption. His default condition is a cash-flow insolvency test rather
than a leverage threshold, and that is the definition this thesis adopts. He also validates the
model against observed Chilean default rates, demonstrating that a household-level credit \ac{ABM}
in a middle-income economy can be held to external behavioural targets.

Hamill et al. \cite{hamill2023creditcard} model the United Kingdom credit-card market and are
relevant for the lender side. Promotional strategies chosen by profit-maximising lenders raise
borrower indebtedness in their model, which reproduces the non-monotonic relationship between
income and debt-to-income ratio, with middle-income households carrying the highest
balance-to-income values, so lender conduct is a driver of household distress in its own right. Yun
and Moon \cite{yun2020housing} contribute the remaining piece, implementing loan-to-value and
\ac{DSTI} constraints as explicit rule-based credit gates and treating them as policy levers, which
establishes that regulatory ratios can be modelled as first-class parameters whose variation
produces non-linear population responses.

This thesis follows all four in treating the balance sheet as the accounting core, and describes
its model using the \ac{ODD} protocol \cite{grimm2006odd, grimm2020odd}. The second update's
requirement that a model state the patterns against which its realism should be judged is adopted
directly in the validation strategy of Chapter~\ref{ch:design}.

\section{Empirical Evidence on Buy Now, Pay Later}
\label{sec:lit-bnpl}

The strongest causal evidence comes from deHaan et al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl}, who track banking
records for 10.6 million United States consumers between 2015 and 2021. Adopting \ac{BNPL} is
followed by a 4.0\% rise in bank overdraft charges, a 1.1\% rise in credit-card interest and a
2.3\% rise in credit-card late fees. An instrumental-variables specification exploiting
pre-adoption shopping habits at \ac{BNPL} partner retailers produces larger estimates still: 8.9\%,
2.5\% and 8.4\% respectively. The effects arrive quickly and fall hardest on consumers without
credit cards or facing liquidity constraints. Their conclusion, that \ac{BNPL} facilitates
overborrowing, is the empirical claim this model is built to interrogate.

Where deHaan et al. establish that \ac{BNPL} damages financial health, the \ac{CFPB}
\cite{cfpb2025bnpl} explains the shape of the exposure. Its analysis of roughly 145 million
applications originated between 2017 and 2022 shows that \ac{BNPL} is not a marginal product, with
21\% of consumers holding a consumer record financing at least one purchase in 2022 against 17.6\%
in 2021. More consequentially, concurrent borrowing is normal: across 2021 and 2022, 63\% of
\ac{BNPL} borrowers held simultaneous loans at some point and 32\% held them across different
firms. Those figures are the empirical foundation of the debt-stacking mechanism, since holding
several obligations at once, particularly across providers who cannot see one another, converts
what looks at the level of a single transaction like a small interest-free deferral into an
aggregate servicing burden that no individual lender has assessed.

Two further findings sharpen the picture. Hayashi and Routh \cite{hayashi2025constraints} report
that \ac{BNPL} users are more financially constrained than non-users, and that 96\% of users who
paid late were at least mildly financially constrained against 86\% of those who did not, so
distress concentrates in the \ac{LMI} population this thesis models. Ackert et al.
\cite{ackert2025bnpl} add an explanation for the demand side: in an incentivised experiment,
consumers chose \ac{BNPL} over a credit-card loan for the same purchase and expected their social
networks to approve, from which the authors conclude that a payment scheme carrying social approval
raises indebtedness. Wang \cite{wang2026buynow} surveys developments since.

\section{Behavioural Foundations for the Decision Rules}
\label{sec:lit-behavioural}

Behaviour of this kind resists a rational-optimisation account, so the model draws its decision
rules from behavioural economics instead. Meier and Sprenger \cite{meier2010present} connect
experimentally elicited present bias to credit-card borrowing, which justifies a borrowing trigger
that answers to immediate shortfall and not to a lifetime budget constraint. Kuchler and Pagel
\cite{Kuchler2021} carry the argument to repayment, showing that present-biased borrowers
systematically fail to execute the paydown they planned, so intention and execution come apart.

Repayment needs a further ingredient. Keys and Wang \cite{Keys2019} find that 29\% of credit-card
accounts regularly pay at or near the contractual minimum, and that at least 22\% of near-minimum
payers shift their payments when the minimum-payment formula changes, which is the signature of
anchoring. Scheduled amortisation would be the wrong rule here, because a substantial minority of
borrowers pay the salient amount instead of the amount that retires the debt, and obligations
persist and compound as a result. Together these results describe a consumer agent that borrows on
shortfall, repays by salience, and varies across the population in how strongly it does both.

The transfer these papers require is not innocuous. All three are United States credit-card
studies, Ackert et al. is a United States experiment, and the model applies them to South African
unsecured credit and \ac{BNPL}. Section~\ref{sec:lim-transfer} takes the problem up, and the
minimum-payer share it turns on is swept rather than fixed.

\section{The South African Regulatory Setting}
\label{sec:lit-regulatory}

South Africa differs from those jurisdictions in a way that sharpens the question. The \ac{NCA}
\cite{sa_nca_2005} obliges registered credit providers to conduct a mandatory affordability
assessment, prescribed in Regulation 23A of the Affordability Assessment Regulations
\cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015}. That regulation does not prescribe a \ac{DSTI} ceiling. It
prescribes a residual-income test built on a table of minimum necessary monthly living expenses by
gross income band, from which discretionary income follows as gross income less statutory
deductions, less those necessary expenses, less every other committed obligation the consumer
discloses or the credit bureau records. Affordability is judged against what survives that
subtraction. The \ac{NCA} also caps the cost of credit by sub-sector, with maximum prescribed rates
in force from 6 May 2016 running from the repurchase rate plus 12\% for mortgages to the repurchase
rate plus 21\% for unsecured credit transactions \cite{sa_ncr_ratecaps_2016}.

\ac{BNPL} providers sit outside this regime. Levying neither interest nor fees on the deferred
amount, they argue that they do not transact credit within the meaning of the \ac{NCA}, and so are
not required to register with the \ac{NCR}, not required to perform the Regulation 23A assessment,
and, decisively for this thesis, not required to report obligations to credit bureaux
\cite{nortonrose_bnpl_sa}. TransUnion \cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025} confirms the last point from
inside the bureau system, listing \ac{BNPL} history among the categories of information absent from
the standard South African credit report. The same survey finds \ac{BNPL} named by 20\% of
consumers intending to apply for credit, behind only new credit cards at 30\% and new personal
loans at 28\%.

This is the mechanism the model formalises. The invisibility of \ac{BNPL} obligations to
traditional lenders is not a simplification adopted for tractability but a documented feature of
the South African regulatory perimeter, and the model treats bureau visibility accordingly, as a
switchable intervention lever.

\section{Synthesis and Gap}
\label{sec:lit-gap}

Each of these literatures answers a different part of the question and none answers it whole.
deHaan et al. \cite{dehaan2024bnpl} establish that \ac{BNPL} harms financial health, but observe a
United States market in which the product is at least partly visible to bureaux. The \ac{CFPB}
\cite{cfpb2025bnpl} measures concurrent borrowing at scale and describes it without explaining it.
The behavioural work \cite{meier2010present, Keys2019, Kuchler2021, ackert2025bnpl} explains why
individuals borrow and fail to repay, but stops at the individual. The agent-based models
\cite{cardaci2018inequality, dorazio2017micro, madeira2018chile, hamill2023creditcard} show how
individual credit behaviour aggregates into instability, yet none includes a lender class exempt
from affordability regulation and invisible to its competitors.

That last gap is where this thesis sits. The South African regulatory perimeter
\cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015, nortonrose_bnpl_sa, transunion_cps_sa_2025} produces a credit
market containing two lender types, one regulated and informed and the other neither, lending to
the same households. Modelling that market at the balance-sheet level, over a survey-derived
household population, is what makes the subsidiary questions of Section~\ref{sec:rqs} tractable.
