\chapter{Data and Population Construction}
\label{ch:data}

The model runs over 5{,}000 household agents, each carried from a real South African survey
household rather than drawn from an assumed distribution. This chapter describes how we built that
population and what its validation establishes.

\section{Design Principles}
\label{sec:data-principles}

Five commitments govern the construction, and each exists to remove a class of assumption that
would otherwise have to be defended.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{One reference year:} everything is in 2017 Rands, with no inflation forwarding
    and no second monetary reference year anywhere in the data layer or the model. This is also
    what makes the counterfactual injection of Section~\ref{sec:injection} interpretable.

    \item \textbf{One backbone source:} \ac{NIDS} Wave 5, fielded in 2017, supplies income,
    expenditure, assets, debt, household composition, head demographics and survey weights, so
    every monetary quantity in the model traces to a single survey instrument.

    \item \textbf{One donor source:} \ac{FinScope} 2019 supplies four financial-inclusion flags and
    nothing else.

    \item \textbf{A simple match, not a statistical fusion:} flags are transferred by copying them
    from a randomly chosen donor within a matching cell. A full statistical fusion buys finer joint
    structure at the price of a validation burden this thesis cannot discharge.

    \item \textbf{Validation is internal at this stage:} the population is checked against the
    survey distributions it was drawn from and against \ac{FinScope} marginals. It cannot be
    validated behaviourally, because it has no behaviour yet. External behavioural targets attach
    to the model in Section~\ref{sec:purpose}.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Backbone: NIDS Wave 5}
\label{sec:backbone}

\ac{NIDS} Wave 5 contains 13{,}719 household records. After dropping records with missing or
unusable income, expenditure or weight fields, 10{,}841 valid households remain, representing a
weighted population of approximately 18.67 million households.

Six quantities are derived per household. \textbf{Monthly income} is the \ac{NIDS} derived
household income aggregate. \textbf{Income source} is assigned by comparing the wage,
government-grant, remittance and other components and taking the dominant one, giving each
household one of \textsc{wage}, \textsc{grant} or \textsc{other}. The distinction matters because
Submodel~1 applies labour-income shocks only to wage-dominant households, South African social
grants being statutory transfers that do not vary with employment. \textbf{Committed expenditure}
is food plus rent, treated downstream as an incompressible floor, and \textbf{discretionary
expenditure} is non-food expenditure less rent, floored at zero. Splitting observed expenditure
this way, instead of imposing a propensity-to-consume rule, keeps the two-tier consumption
structure of Submodel~2 anchored to something the survey measured. \textbf{Liquid savings} proxies
the cash buffer using the \ac{NIDS} financial-assets field, winsorised at the 99th percentile
(R133{,}662); it is the weakest field in the backbone (Section~\ref{sec:lim-savings}).
\textbf{Consolidated traditional debt} is the \ac{NIDS} financial-debt field.

Households are then assigned to per-capita income quintiles, weighted by survey weight, with bounds
at R900, R1{,}801, R3{,}400 and R7{,}712 per person per month and weighted shares between 19.6\%
and 20.4\%. Head-of-household age, gender, race and education were joined from the \ac{NIDS}
individual-derived file through the household roster, matching 10{,}736 of 10{,}841 households. The
education gradient runs from 4\% tertiary attainment in Q1 to 54\% in Q5.

\section{The Donor Match: FinScope 2019}
\label{sec:match}

\ac{NIDS} does not record whether a household is banked, holds formal credit, holds a savings
product, or uses informal finance. \ac{FinScope} does, and those four flags gate behaviour in the
model: Submodel~12 makes \ac{BNPL} eligibility conditional on banked status, which caps \ac{BNPL}
access at 83.1\% of the population by an observed field rather than a free parameter.

We resolved the four flags to \ac{FinScope} question codes, being F1 for banked status, G5 and
G10--G14 for formal credit access, K7 for savings product holding, and the informal-finance
battery, and transferred them by a cell-donor match. Each \ac{NIDS} household is assigned a cell
defined by per-capita income quintile crossed with province, a randomly chosen \ac{FinScope}
respondent from the same cell is drawn, and that respondent's four flags are copied across.
Forty-five cells result, every one carrying at least 30 donors, so no household required a fallback
draw to a coarser cell.

The match reproduces cell marginals but not finer joint structure. Its diagnostics are the
differences between synthetic and \ac{FinScope} national rates: 0.4 percentage points for banked
status, 2.3 for formal credit access, 0.1 for informal finance and 1.8 for savings product holding.
The largest by-quintile discrepancy across all twenty flag-by-quintile combinations is 3.0
percentage points.

The matching cell does more work than a matching key normally would, since it is also the reporting
stratum for quintile-level results and the reference group through which peer influence operates in
Submodel~11. All three uses rest on the claim that income and geography together identify
households in comparable circumstances. \ac{FinScope} 2019 stands in for the 2017
financial-inclusion landscape because no 2017 wave is available, and the two-year gap is recorded
in Section~\ref{sec:lim-finscope}.

\section{Constructing Debt Servicing}
\label{sec:servicing}

Neither survey records what a household pays each month to service its debt, and the model cannot
run without it. We construct servicing in three steps.

First, each household's consolidated balance is amortised at the \ac{NCA} statutory maximum
prescribed interest rate for the relevant sub-sector, under the regime in force from 6 May 2016 and
therefore valid for 2017, evaluated at the 2017 repurchase rate of 7.00\%. This gives 21\% for
credit facilities, 24\% for other credit agreements and 28\% for unsecured credit transactions. A
minimum term of six months is imposed, which prevents small balances at short assumed terms from
generating implausible instalments.

Second, the resulting instalment is capped by the Regulation 23A residual-income test, the same
rule the lender applies in Submodel~9. The ceiling this implies varies sharply with income, from
10.4\% of income at R900 per month to 83.2\% at R7{,}712 per month, and both worked examples
published with the regulation are asserted in the construction notebook. The cap binds for 158
households, or 3.4\% of the 4{,}702 households carrying debt. That it binds rarely is the point:
the rule acts as a backstop against implausible instalments generated by the rate and term
assumptions, and if it were doing most of the work, those assumptions would be wrong.

Third, the construction produces a diagnostic worth reporting in its own right. Fifty-three
households carrying debt have gross income at or below the Regulation 23A minimum-expense norm for
their income band, so their residual income is zero or negative before any instalment is
considered, and no \ac{NCA}-compliant lender could lawfully have granted the debt they report. We
retain them, since the data cannot distinguish informal debt falling outside the \ac{NCA} from
misreporting or from non-compliant lending.

\section{Resampling to a Fixed Population}
\label{sec:resample}

The 10{,}841 weighted households are resampled with replacement, with probability proportional to
survey weight, to a fixed population of 5{,}000 agents drawn from 3{,}250 unique source households,
under seed 20240601. Weighting at this step is what makes the unweighted 5{,}000 agents behave like
the weighted national population, at a fixed size that can be re-run many times. The resample
introduces a small distortion: the weighted per-capita income Gini of the source population is
0.651 against 0.671 unweighted for the 5{,}000 agents, so the resample is more unequal than the
population it represents by 0.019 Gini points. Population-size stability was checked at 1{,}000,
5{,}000 and 10{,}000 agents.

The scorecard carries four qualifications. Check C is the only genuinely external one: the
per-capita income Gini of 0.651 falls inside the 0.63 to 0.70 band reported for South Africa by the
World Bank and Statistics South Africa, and no step in the construction targeted it. Every other
check in groups A, B and F asks whether the population reproduces the source it was drawn from,
which is a question about faithful sampling rather than external realism. Check D was rewritten
during development: it originally asserted that the maximum \ac{DSTI} was at or below 0.66, which
tested the cap the construction had just imposed and could not have failed, and it now tests how
often the affordability guard binds. Two checks pass with very little margin, the resample Gini gap
of 0.019 against its 0.02 tolerance and mean household size at 3.03 against a Statistics South
Africa benchmark of roughly 3.3. Finally, nothing here validates behaviour. Whether households
built from this population borrow, repay and default realistically is answered against the four
external patterns registered in Section~\ref{sec:purpose}.

Two fields are absent by design. Every household opens with a \ac{BNPL} balance of zero, which is
the injection design of Section~\ref{sec:injection}. No household carries a behavioural type from
the data either, since no South African survey records repayment behaviour of that kind, so the
minimum-payer distinction of Submodel~6 is assigned stochastically at initialisation.
