\section{The Household Population}
\label{ch:population}

We construct a synthetic population of 5{,}000 household agents, derived from South African survey microdata. This section details the primary data sources, the derivation of household-level variables, the imputation of the financial indicators the model requires, and the validation of the constructed population.

Establishing a representative baseline population is necessary to address the study's primary research questions. However, while this section validates the static structural characteristics of the population, the dynamic behavioural realism of these households is evaluated separately in Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}. All monetary values are denominated in 2017 South African Rand (ZAR) to align with the primary dataset, with no inflation adjustments applied to the data layer.

\subsection{Primary Dataset: NIDS Wave 5}
\label{sec:backbone}

The core structural data is drawn from the National Income Dynamics Study (NIDS) Wave 5 \cite{nids_w5_2018}. The raw dataset comprises 13{,}719 household records. Retaining those with an income, a household size, and a positive survey weight leaves 10{,}841 households. The 2{,}878 excluded records all carry a zero or missing weight, so the exclusion removes no weighted population. Applied to the national weights, the retained sample represents 18.67 million households and 56.5 million persons.

Six primary economic variables are derived for each household directly from the survey:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Monthly income:} The aggregated household income as derived in NIDS.
    \item \textbf{Income source:} Categorised by the dominant income stream (wage, government grant, or remittance/other).
    \item \textbf{Committed expenditure:} Food expenditure plus rent paid, establishing a minimum consumption floor.
    \item \textbf{Discretionary expenditure:} Non-food expenditure, which in NIDS is recorded separately from rent.
    \item \textbf{Liquid savings:} Proxied via the NIDS financial-assets field and winsorised at the 99th percentile (ZAR 133{,}662) to mitigate the influence of extreme outliers.
    \item \textbf{Traditional debt:} The consolidated financial-debt field as reported in NIDS.
\end{itemize}

NIDS total expenditure decomposes exactly into food, non-food, rent paid, and imputed rentals. Imputed rentals are the consumption value of owner-occupied housing rather than a cash outflow, and are excluded from both tiers; they would otherwise enter the model as compressible spending for the 82.6\% of households that carry them, at a median of ZAR 700 per month.

Four of the source fields are populated only where the household reports holding the item, and none of them ever records a zero. A missing value therefore denotes non-holding rather than an unreported amount, and is read as zero: financial assets (48.2\% of households), financial debt (56.6\%), total debt (54.4\%), and rent paid (80.2\%). The reading is testable, and Section~\ref{sec:data-validation} tests it against the aggregate credit book. Its practical consequence is that committed expenditure reduces to food alone for the four in five households that own their dwelling.

Households are subsequently divided into per-capita income quintiles, bounded at ZAR 900, ZAR 1{,}801, ZAR 3{,}400, and ZAR 7{,}712 per person per month. Demographic characteristics of the household head including age, gender, race, and educational attainment are joined from the NIDS individual-derived file, resulting in successful matches for 10{,}736 of the 10{,}841 households.

\subsection{Imputing Financial Inclusion: FinScope 2019}
\label{sec:match}

NIDS records neither bank access nor the composition of a household's credit, so six binary indicators are imputed from the FinScope 2019 survey \cite{finscope2019}. Each gates a model mechanism. Banked status admits a household to \ac{BNPL}, capping market access at the observed 82.8\% rather than at a chosen parameter. The five product flags, covering store cards, revolving credit, hire purchase, short-term loans and personal loans, set the interest rate and repayment horizon behind every debtor's instalment in Section~\ref{sec:servicing}. Indicators with no consumer in the simulation are not imputed.

Traits are assigned by cell-donor matching. The population is stratified on per-capita income quintile crossed with province, and each NIDS household draws a donor from its own cell with probability proportional to the donor's survey weight (seed: 42). All six flags come from the same donor, preserving the joint distribution over products. The 45 cells hold at least 30 donors each, so no household draws from a broader pool.

FinScope measures income in seven bands rather than continuously, so donors are placed in quintiles from band midpoints divided by household size. Deflating FinScope's 2019 income to 2017 shifts the donor pool's quintile composition by up to 3.7 percentage points but moves the imputed rates within each quintile by at most 2.4, and the undeflated key is retained.

Table~\ref{tab:imputation_errors} reports the resulting national rates. The largest gap is 1.8 percentage points, on store cards. Figure~\ref{fig:inclusion-fidelity} decomposes the comparison by income quintile, where the largest deviation across the thirty quintile-by-flag combinations is 2.3 percentage points. The final column confirms that the resampling step of Section~\ref{sec:resample} carries these rates through to the agent population, the largest shift being 0.8 percentage points.

\subsection{Debt Servicing Construction}
\label{sec:servicing}

\ac{NIDS} records a debt \emph{stock}, but the model requires a monthly \emph{flow}. Debt servicing is therefore derived through a three-step amortisation process using statutory interest rates and empirical repayment horizons.

First, consolidated debt balances are amortised using the maximum statutory interest rates set by the \ac{NCA} in 2016 \cite{sa_ncr_ratecaps_2016}. Evaluated against the 2017 repurchase rate of 7.00\%, these limits stand at 21\% for credit facilities, 24\% for standard credit agreements, and 28\% for unsecured credit. The \ac{CCMR} \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017} does not publish realised average \acp{APR} by product class, and no other 2017 source gives them for this population, so the statutory ceiling stands in. It is an upper bound, and it biases constructed servicing upward.

Second, each product class is assigned a specific repayment horizon, detailed in Table~\ref{tab:credit-terms}. For store cards and hire purchase, horizons are the median ratio of reported balance to observed monthly payment in \ac{NIDS}. Because the numerator is total financial debt while the denominator covers one product, these horizons are themselves upper bounds. For the remaining classes, horizons are set to match the \ac{CCMR} stock-flow implied life of the corresponding book (calculated as three times the gross debtors book divided by quarterly credit granted). This measures the average time a Rand remains outstanding and aligns with \ac{CCMR} term-of-agreement distributions to within approximately three months.

\ac{NIDS} reports one consolidated balance per household, so neither rate nor horizon can be weighted by product size. Each household takes the unweighted mean of the rates and horizons of the classes its donor holds, and households whose donor holds no flagged product are priced as unsecured.

Third, the resulting instalment is constrained by the \ac{NCA} Regulation 23A residual-income test. The regulation prescribes minimum necessary living expenses by gross monthly income band and treats only the residual as affordable, so the implied ceiling rises with income. Applied to the population it runs from zero to 93.2\% of gross income. The regulation governs an individual consumer and is applied here to household income, which is why the ceiling is loose for the large, low-per-capita households that dominate the bottom quintile: the mean ceiling in Q1 is 49.6\% of household income.

The guard binds for 178 of the 4{,}702 indebted households, or 3.8\%, but it removes 22.8\% of aggregate scheduled service, so its effect on the population is concentrated rather than marginal. For 53 households gross income falls at or below the Regulation 23A expense norm, leaving no affordable capacity at all and reducing their scheduled service to zero. Their debt is retained because the data cannot distinguish legacy credit, unrecorded informal loans, and non-compliant lending, but the consequence is that the most constrained debtors in the population enter the simulation carrying no servicing burden.

Constructed servicing is checked against the \ac{FinScope} budget-allocation exercise, which is measured as a share of monthly outlay rather than of income \cite{finscope2019}. Among holders of at least one modelled product, debt repayment takes 6.07\% of spending on clothing accounts and hire purchase alone and 9.78\% once bond, credit-card and vehicle finance are added. Placing the model on the same denominator and conditioning on the same population, debt service is 12.8\% of monthly outlay among debtors, above a benchmark whose upper figure already includes products outside the model's scope. The gap is consistent with the statutory ceiling standing in for realised \acp{APR}, and is recorded as a check the population does not pass in Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}. Expressed as a share of gross income, \ac{DSTI} among debtors has a weighted mean of 10.9\% and a median of 3.3\%; the population mean of 4.8\% is not comparable to either benchmark, since 56\% of households hold no debt.

\subsection{Population Resampling}
\label{sec:resample}

To generate a computationally feasible and consistent ABM environment, the 10{,}841 weighted NIDS households are resampled with replacement to construct a fixed population of 5{,}000 agents. This procedure, executed with probability proportional to the survey weight (seed: 20260818), yields a population comprising 3{,}221 unique source households. Each agent thereafter carries equal weight, so every downstream statistic is computed unweighted and must reproduce the weighted source distribution rather than being constructed to match it.

Figure~\ref{fig:pop-fidelity} shows that it does. The per-capita income distribution is reproduced across its full range, from the grant-dependent left tail to the top percentile, with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov gap of 0.016. The four categorical attributes that structure the simulation, namely income quintile, household size, province, and dominant income source, deviate by at most 1.4 percentage points across the twenty-four categories shown. The resampling introduces one distortion, visible in panel (b): drawing with replacement mildly amplifies inequality, and the per-capita income Gini coefficient rises by 0.015, from 0.651 in the weighted source to 0.667 in the unweighted agent population.

Group C carries the two external checks. The per-capita Gini of 0.651 sits inside the 0.63 to 0.70 range established for South Africa \cite{worldbank_gini_zaf}. The second compares the population's aggregate traditional debt of ZAR 191.4 billion against the \ac{CCMR} gross debtors book for the credit types the model carries, ZAR 392.0 billion \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017}. Recovering 49\% of an administrative aggregate is the expected order for self-reported survey debt, and the comparison also adjudicates the zero-fill of Section~\ref{sec:backbone}: reading the missing balances as non-response and imputing them at the holder mean would place the population at 111\% of the entire book, which the aggregate rules out.

The failing check is informative rather than fatal. Constructed servicing exceeds the only independent measure of its level by roughly three percentage points of monthly outlay, in the direction the statutory ceiling \acp{APR} predict. Model results that turn on the level of traditional debt service should therefore be read as an upper bound.

The remaining checks confirm fidelity to the underlying surveys rather than absolute accuracy. Two limitations are visible in Table~\ref{tab:validation} but not resolved by it. Mean household size of 3.03 passes against a benchmark of 3.3 but is low, and since the weights reproduce the 2017 person total almost exactly, the population carries roughly 10\% more households than the national estimate; per-household aggregates inherit that. Group B compares imputed rates back to the survey they were drawn from, stratified on the same key used to draw them, so it establishes that the match introduced no compositional error and not that the imputation is correct.

The population also arrives with stress already built in. At initialisation 3.9\% of households cannot meet committed expenditure from income, 5.2\% cannot meet committed expenditure plus scheduled service, and 44.3\% hold no liquid savings. Under the distress and default rules of Section~\ref{sec:submodels} this fraction is distressed from the first tick, independently of any shock or of \ac{BNPL}. Whether the agents borrow, repay and default realistically is established against out-of-sample dynamic targets in Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}.

Two dimensions of the model are absent from the underlying survey data. Every agent is initialised with a \ac{BNPL} balance of exactly zero, establishing a clean baseline for the controlled injection. And because the surveys do not capture individual psychological biases, the heuristics governing repayment behaviour are assigned stochastically at initialisation.

With the static household population fully constructed and mapped to empirical data, the focus now shifts to how these agents behave and interact over time.
