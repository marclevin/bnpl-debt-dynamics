\section{The Household Population}
\label{ch:population}

We construct a synthetic population of 5{,}000 household agents, derived from South African survey microdata. This section details the construction of this population framework, outlining the primary data sources, the derivation of household-level variables, the imputation of financial inclusion metrics, and the subsequent validation of the constructed population against external benchmarks. 

Establishing a representative baseline population is necessary to address the study's primary research questions. However, while this section validates the static structural characteristics of the population, the dynamic behavioural realism of these households is evaluated separately in Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}. All monetary values are denominated in 2017 South African Rand (ZAR) to align with the primary dataset, with no inflation adjustments applied to the data layer.

\subsection{Primary Dataset: NIDS Wave 5}
\label{sec:backbone}

The core structural data is drawn from the National Income Dynamics Study (NIDS) Wave 5 \cite{nids_w5_2018}. The raw dataset comprises 13{,}719 household records. Following the removal of observations with missing or anomalous income, expenditure, or survey weight variables, a robust sample of 10{,}841 households is retained. Applied to the national weights, this sample represents a population of approximately 18.67 million South African households.

Six primary economic variables are derived for each household directly from the survey:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Monthly income:} The aggregated household income as derived in NIDS.
    \item \textbf{Income source:} Categorised by the dominant income stream (wage, government grant, or remittance/other).
    \item \textbf{Committed expenditure:} The sum of food and rent expenditures, establishing a minimum consumption floor.
    \item \textbf{Discretionary expenditure:} Total non-food expenditure exclusive of rent, floored at zero.
    \item \textbf{Liquid savings:} Proxied via the NIDS financial-assets field and winsorised at the 99th percentile (ZAR 133{,}662) to mitigate the influence of extreme outliers.
    \item \textbf{Traditional debt:} The consolidated financial-debt field as reported in NIDS.
\end{itemize}

Households are subsequently divided into per-capita income quintiles, bounded at ZAR 900, ZAR 1{,}801, ZAR 3{,}400, and ZAR 7{,}712 per person per month. Demographic characteristics of the household head including age, gender, race, and educational attainment are joined from the NIDS individual-derived file, resulting in successful matches for 10{,}736 of the 10{,}841 households.

\subsection{Imputing Financial Inclusion: FinScope 2019}
\label{sec:match}

Since NIDS lacks granular data on financial product use, we impute four binary indicators from the FinScope 2019 survey \cite{finscope2019}: banked status, formal credit access, savings product ownership, and informal finance usage. These flags act as structural gates in the simulation. For example, restricting \ac{BNPL} eligibility to only banked households naturally caps market access at the observed rate of 82.8\%, avoiding the need for an arbitrary parameter.

To assign these traits, we apply a cell-donor matching algorithm. The population is divided into specific demographic subgroups, or strata, based on a cross-tabulation of per-capita income quintile and province. Each NIDS household looks into its matching FinScope subgroup and randomly adopts a donor's financial inclusion flags (draw seed: 42). This process creates 45 distinct demographic cells containing at least 30 donors each, ensuring every household finds a match without having to pull data from a broader, less accurate pool.

While this method preserves the local characteristics of each cell, slight deviations naturally emerge at the national level. As Table~\ref{tab:imputation_errors} details, these discrepancies are minor: the largest national gap is 2.3 percentage points, on formal credit access. Figure~\ref{fig:inclusion-fidelity} decomposes the same comparison by income quintile, where the largest deviation across the twenty quintile-by-flag combinations is 3.0 percentage points. The final column of Table~\ref{tab:imputation_errors} also confirms that the resampling step of Section~\ref{sec:resample} carries these rates through to the agent population essentially unchanged, the largest shift being 0.5 percentage points.

\subsection{Debt Servicing Construction}
\label{sec:servicing}

\ac{NIDS} records a debt \emph{stock}, but the model requires a monthly \emph{flow}. Debt servicing is therefore derived through a three-step amortisation process using statutory interest rates and empirical repayment horizons.

First, consolidated debt balances are amortised using the maximum statutory interest rates set by the \ac{NCA} in 2016 \cite{sa_ncr_ratecaps_2016}. Evaluated against the 2017 repurchase rate of 7.00\%, these limits stand at 21\% for credit facilities, 24\% for standard credit agreements, and 28\% for unsecured credit. Because the \ac{CCMR} \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017} does not publish realised average \acp{APR} by product class, the statutory maximum serves as the most rigorous available proxy. This inherently biases the estimated servicing costs upward, which is carried as a stated model limitation.

Second, each product class is assigned a specific repayment horizon, detailed in Table~\ref{tab:credit-terms}. For store cards and hire purchase, horizons are derived from \ac{NIDS} by dividing the reported balance by the observed monthly payment. For the remaining classes, horizons are set to match the \ac{CCMR} stock-flow implied life of the corresponding book (calculated as three times the gross debtors book divided by quarterly credit granted). This effectively measures the average time a Rand remains outstanding and aligns with \ac{CCMR} term-of-agreement distributions to within approximately three months.

Third, the resulting instalment is constrained by the \ac{NCA} Regulation 23A residual-income test. This ceiling scales with income, ranging from 10.4\% at the ZAR 900 threshold up to 83.2\% at ZAR 7{,}712. 

The data contains 53 indebted households whose gross income falls completely below the Regulation 23A minimum expense norm. While registered lenders could not legally extend new credit to these households, they are retained in the simulation. The data cannot definitively distinguish whether this debt represents legacy credit held prior to an income shock, unrecorded informal loans, or non-compliant formal lending. Retaining them ensures the simulation preserves the true empirical distribution of debt and income.

Debt servicing is verified against two independent measures. Among the 1,219 \ac{NIDS} households reporting a hire-purchase or clothing-account payment, observed debt service accounts for a median 5.36\% of gross household income. \ac{FinScope}'s budget-allocation exercise puts debt repayment at 9.78\% of monthly spending for users of formal credit \cite{finscope2019}. The model's weighted-mean \ac{DSTI} ranges from 2.8\% to 6.1\% across quintiles. This metric lies between the two benchmarks, which is the expected outcome given that the higher FinScope measure includes mortgage and vehicle finance excluded from this model's scope.

\subsection{Population Resampling}
\label{sec:resample}

To generate a computationally feasible and consistent ABM environment, the 10{,}841 weighted NIDS households are resampled with replacement to construct a fixed population of 5{,}000 agents. This procedure, executed with probability proportional to the survey weight (seed: 20260818), yields a population comprising 3{,}221 unique source households. Each agent thereafter carries equal weight, so every downstream statistic is computed unweighted and must reproduce the weighted source distribution rather than being constructed to match it.

Figure~\ref{fig:pop-fidelity} shows that it does. The per-capita income distribution is reproduced across its full range, from the grant-dependent left tail to the top percentile, with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov gap of 0.016. The four categorical attributes that structure the simulation, namely income quintile, household size, province, and dominant income source, deviate by at most 1.4 percentage points across the twenty-four categories shown. The resampling introduces one distortion, visible in panel (b): drawing with replacement mildly amplifies inequality, and the per-capita income Gini coefficient rises by 0.015, from 0.651 in the weighted source to 0.667 in the unweighted agent population.

With the exception of the Gini coefficient benchmark (Check C), which successfully aligns with the established 0.63 to 0.70 range for South Africa \cite{worldbank_gini_zaf}, these diagnostics primarily confirm sample fidelity rather than absolute real-world accuracy. The validation demonstrates that the synthetic population accurately reflects the underlying surveys, without introducing significant sampling errors. Establishing whether these agents engage in realistic borrowing, repayment, and default behaviours requires validation against out-of-sample dynamic targets, which is conducted in Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}.

Two dimensions of the model are deliberately absent from the underlying survey data. First, every agent is initialised with a \ac{BNPL} balance of exactly zero, which establishes a clean baseline for the controlled system injection. Second, because empirical surveys do not systematically capture individual psychological biases, the specific heuristics that govern repayment behaviour are assigned stochastically during model initialisation.

With the static household population fully constructed and mapped to empirical data, the focus now shifts to how these agents behave and interact over time.
