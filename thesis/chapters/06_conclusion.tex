\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This thesis asked how a lending channel outside the affordability and reporting regime affects a household population already in credit distress, and what bringing that channel inside the regime would change.
The answers come from a controlled counterfactual under 2017 conditions, not a forecast of South Africa after 2017.
RQ1 is answered in part.
The model reproduces the two arrears bands to which it was fitted and passes three of four external checks on the population, all under broad tolerances.
It misses the intermediate arrears bands, the servicing share, the \ac{BNPL} volume band and the interest measure of the complementarity pattern, and mean purchase size at $\beta = 0$ meets its tolerance by less than R1 (Tables~\ref{tab:validation} and~\ref{tab:bnpl-on-checks}).
These results support using the model to compare mechanisms, with limited confidence in its outcome levels.
For RQ2, cross-platform stacking emerges although no rule assigns households to several providers.
With four platforms and no peer influence, 45.0\% of final-tick holders have balances on two or more platforms.
Enabling \ac{BNPL} raises default by $1.22 \pm 0.11$ percentage points without peer influence and by $2.07 \pm 0.10$ points with it (one standard error; Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}).
Default does not change detectably with the number of platforms, and it rises approximately linearly with access under both adoption rules, with no tipping point on the tested grid (Tables~\ref{tab:stacking} and~\ref{tab:access}).
For RQ3, neither regulatory switch lowers default in the affordability channel.
Bureau visibility raises default by $0.26 \pm 0.11$ points without peer influence, which is detectable, and has no detectable effect with it ($0.04 \pm 0.12$ points).
Mandatory screening lowers adoption by 1.6 to 2.6 points and has no detectable effect on default (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}).

The most secure contribution is a household population that combines \ac{NIDS} financial data with imputed \ac{FinScope} inclusion flags and is checked against sources its construction did not use.
The second is a model in which two classes of lender differ in what they can observe, so that the visibility gap can be switched on and off.
The third is a set of reproducible comparisons of access, adoption rules and lending restrictions.
The level of default rests on two fitted parameters and on a borrowing-amount rule with no empirical estimate, which moves default by up to 2.15 points (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}).
Replacing the persistent income shock with a single-tick loss lowers it by 9.79 points (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}).
The \ac{BNPL} effect is positive in every setting tested, but its size depends most on the same borrowing-amount rule (Table~\ref{tab:effect}).

\label{sec:lim-reading}
The bureau experiment adds \ac{BNPL} obligations to the traditional lender's affordability calculation.
Credit scores, pricing and lenders' responses are excluded, so the scoring channel of the reporting requirement is untested.
The standard errors measure variation between replicates, not uncertainty in the inputs or in the model's structure.
The robustness analysis tests the level of default and the \ac{BNPL} effect; it does not test the scenario differences.
Default in the model responds to the number of obligations a household services at once.
A hypothetical cap of two concurrent agreements lowers default by $1.01 \pm 0.11$ points without peer influence, with cumulative volume within 0.4\% of the benchmark (Appendix~\ref{app:levers}).
Concurrent obligations are therefore a margin for further study.
The result does not establish a regulatory threshold, and the model does not measure household welfare.

\label{sec:future-work}
Four extensions follow.
\textbf{Credit scoring.} A rule linking reported \ac{BNPL} behaviour to scores, pricing and refusals would test the channel that the reporting requirement most directly opens (Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry}).
\textbf{South African behavioural evidence.} Local estimates of borrowing amounts, minimum-payment behaviour, present bias and the persistence of income shocks would replace the assumed and imported rules on which the outcome levels most depend (Sections~\ref{sec:amount} and~\ref{sec:lim-transfer}).
\textbf{Feedback and contagion.} A lender that restricts credit after losses, or a link from aggregate default to employment, would let distress spread through channels now excluded (Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}).
\textbf{Adoption parameters.} The \ac{CFPB} stacking shares could constrain $q_{\text{base}}$, but would then no longer serve as a check.
