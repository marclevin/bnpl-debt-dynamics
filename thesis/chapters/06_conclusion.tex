\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

% Numbers are read from the generated tables of Sections 3.5, 4 and 5 or from
% results/summary/results_numbers.json.

This thesis asked how adding a \ac{BNPL} channel changes credit distress in a model of South
African households, and how bureau visibility and affordability screening affect the outcome.
The answers are comparisons within the model under 2017 conditions, and each is conditional on
the assumptions the compared arms share.

\textbf{RQ1.} The population built from survey microdata matches external figures for
household size and income inequality and holds about half the household debt of the
corresponding \ac{CCMR} book. The baseline keeps its calibrated arrears profile as a share while
the credit-active book halves. Part of that stability is built into the calibration, and none of
it shows how arrears arise. Debt service is above what households report, and the default rate
has no external counterpart.

\textbf{RQ2.} Enabling \ac{BNPL} raises default by $1.03 \pm 0.06$ points without peer
influence. Most of the effect comes from the 3.6\% of households whose surveyed income does not
cover their food and rent, chiefly those the Regulation 23A test refuses and the platforms' light screen
accepts, and it is not detectable when \ac{BNPL} cannot be used against a shortfall. Peer
influence acts only on want-driven purchases, and the effect does not change detectably with
$\beta$ between 0 and 0.3 ($+0.003 \pm 0.010$ points per 0.1). Default rises with access under
both adoption rules and shows no systematic rise with the number of platforms. Stacking is set by
the assumed routing and by how often households borrow (Section~\ref{sec:results-stacking}).

\textbf{RQ3.} Bureau visibility raises default by $0.20 \pm 0.07$ points at $\beta=0$; at
$\beta=0.3$ the rise is not detectable, but the two do not differ detectably. Screening lowers
default by 0.93 to 1.03 points, about as much as \ac{BNPL} adds, because it applies the lender's
test to \ac{BNPL}. Visibility also raises the share of households short of food and rent, so it
worsens both measures at $\beta=0$. Screening raises that share at $\beta=0$ only, and whether it leaves
households better off is open (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios-distress}).

Better data would most improve four inputs: how much South African households borrow with
\ac{BNPL} when short of cash and who they are, the size and frequency of \ac{BNPL} purchases, how
households choose among providers, and where refused households borrow instead, including informal
lenders.

\label{sec:lim-reading}
Default follows the bureau's 90-day construct, and its level rests on the starting profile and
the threshold (Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}). The \ac{BNPL} effect depends on counting \ac{BNPL} arrears as default
(Section~\ref{sec:results-injection}).
Which households start in arrears is an assumption, and the survey cannot say whether the
households whose income is below their food and rent are persistently short or have
under-recorded income (Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}). The hire wage is imputed, and
hires move income between quintiles (Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}).

A rule linking reported \ac{BNPL} behaviour to credit scores, pricing
and refusals would test the channel that the reporting arrangements most directly open
(Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry}). Giving refused households another source of credit, following
them after refusal and running past two years would show whether the rise in default under
bureau visibility survives. Re-running the injection with the larger of the derived and
single-question incomes for the households short from the start would show how far the effect
rests on how \ac{NIDS} records their income. A lender that restricts credit
after losses, or a link from aggregate default to employment, would let distress spread through
channels the model now excludes (Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}). A flow of new borrowers would
keep the credit-active book from shrinking (Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}).
