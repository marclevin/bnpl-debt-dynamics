\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

% Numbers are read from the generated tables of Sections 3.5, 4 and 5 or from
% results/summary/results_numbers.json.

This thesis asked how adding a \ac{BNPL} channel changes credit distress in a model of South
African households, and how bureau visibility and affordability screening affect the outcome.
The answers are comparisons within the model under 2017 conditions.

\textbf{RQ1.} The population built from survey microdata matches external figures for
household size and income inequality and is of the right order for household debt, which
supports the inputs. With employment constant in expectation, the baseline reproduces the one
arrears band it was fitted to and about two-thirds of the untargeted 90+ day share. It misses the bands between them, its debt service
is above what households report, and its default rate has no external counterpart.

\textbf{RQ2.} Owing several platforms at once is common, at a rate set mostly by the assumed
routing rule and by how often households borrow; no arm tests platforms that see one another.
Enabling \ac{BNPL} does not change default detectably without peer influence
($-0.03 \pm 0.03$ points) and raises it by $0.17 \pm 0.04$ points at $\beta=1$, where
households borrow 13 times as much. Default does not change detectably with the routing rule
and does not rise with the number of platforms, and a cap on platforms owed lowers it by at
most about 0.1 points.

\textbf{RQ3.} Making \ac{BNPL} visible to the traditional lender raises its refusals by about
15\% and default by 0.16 to 0.18 points, most in the poorest quintile, because a refused
household in the model has no other credit. Mandatory screening reduces \ac{BNPL} borrowing
and does not change default detectably. Neither measure lowers aggregate default under the
model's rules.

These answers support four findings.

In a labour market as stable as that of 2017, \ac{BNPL} adds little to default. Enabling it
raises default by $0.17 \pm 0.04$ points, about 2\% of the baseline rate, and only with peer
influence that makes households borrow 13 times as much as without it. An
earlier version of the model, which differed only in Submodel~1 and in which the share of
earners out of work rose to 39\% within two years, gave an effect of 0.60 points
(Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}).

The effect grows with how often and how much households borrow. Widening access raises
default only with peer influence ($0.23 \pm 0.09$ points at $\beta=1$, against
$0.06 \pm 0.07$ without). At $\beta=1$ the effect ranges from 0.15 points with half the
reference purchase size to 0.41 with double it. The settings whose purchase size matches the
provider's show no detectable effect without peer influence. Caps on platforms owed and on
agreements remove as much volume and change default equally, so the number of platforms owed
matters only through the volume borrowed.

The effect rests on two modelling rules. It disappears when borrowing for food and rent is not
counted as distress, and when income losses last a single tick.

Bureau visibility acts on households through the traditional lender's refusals, and its rise
in default is larger when borrowing for food and rent is not counted as distress. The model
cannot say whether it helps or harms households, because a refused household has no informal
or alternative credit, and the rise it reports for the poorest quintile depends on that
omission.

These findings identify where better data would most improve the analysis: the distribution
of \ac{BNPL} purchase sizes and the frequency of repeat purchases among South African users,
how much households borrow when short of cash, how they choose among providers, and where
refused households borrow instead, including from informal lenders.

\label{sec:lim-reading}
The conclusions have further limits. The outcome is a cash-flow default, mostly a failure to
pay for food and rent (Section~\ref{sec:sequence}). Its level rests on one fitted parameter and
on survey records: about 3.5 of its 7.3 points come from households whose income is below their
food and rent from the start (Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}), and the wage of a hired
jobseeker is imputed. The bureau experiment excludes credit scores, pricing and lenders'
responses, and assesses surveyed rather than current income.

A rule linking reported \ac{BNPL} behaviour to credit scores, pricing
and refusals would test the channel that the reporting arrangements most directly open
(Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry}). Giving refused households another source of credit, following
them after refusal and running past two years would show whether the rise in default under
bureau visibility survives. A lender that restricts credit after losses, or a link from
aggregate default to employment, would let distress spread through channels the model now
excludes (Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}).
