\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

% Every number is read from the generated tables of Sections 3.5, 4 and 5 or from
% results/summary/results_numbers.json; nothing is typed.

This thesis asked how a lending channel outside the affordability and reporting regime affects a household population already in credit distress, and what bringing that channel inside the regime would change.
We answered with a controlled counterfactual under 2017 conditions, so the results describe mechanisms in the model and do not forecast South African default after 2017.

The model supports comparisons between mechanisms better than it supports statements about levels.
It reproduces the two arrears bands to which it was fitted and passes three of four external checks on the population, all under broad tolerances.
It misses the intermediate arrears bands, the servicing share, the purchase-size and volume bands of the \ac{BNPL} market, the interest measure of the complementarity pattern and the debt-to-income pattern (Tables~\ref{tab:validation} and~\ref{tab:bnpl-on-checks}).
RQ1 is therefore answered in part.

For RQ2, households come to owe several platforms at once: with four platforms and no peer influence, 52.0\% of final-tick holders have balances on two or more.
This share follows from the assumption that households choose among platforms at random, so it measures how often agreements overlap.
Default does not change detectably with the number of platforms (Table~\ref{tab:stacking}).
Without peer influence, enabling \ac{BNPL} raises default by about a tenth of a point: $0.12 \pm 0.05$ points across four seed blocks (one standard error).
With peer influence at $\beta = 1$ it raises default by $0.61 \pm 0.11$ points, in a market that borrows ten times as much (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}).
Default rises with access where peer influence is present, and the experiment is too imprecise to establish the shape of that rise or to exclude a tipping point smaller than a third of a point (Table~\ref{tab:access}).

For RQ3, neither bureau visibility nor mandatory screening changes default detectably in the affordability channel, alone or together (Table~\ref{tab:switches}).
Each comparison would have detected a change of about 0.25 points.
Bureau visibility raises the traditional lender's refusals by about a sixth, and screening lowers holding by 1.9 to 2.8 points.

The most secure contribution is a household population that combines \ac{NIDS} financial data with imputed \ac{FinScope} inclusion flags and is checked against sources its construction did not use.
The second is a model in which two classes of lender differ in what they can observe, so that the visibility gap can be switched on and off.
The third is a set of reproducible comparisons of access, adoption rules and lending restrictions.

\label{sec:lim-reading}
Four limits govern how these results should be read.
The size of the \ac{BNPL} effect depends on an assumption that no evidence fixes.
If a household cannot borrow the checkout quarter of an agreement that it uses for a shortfall, the effect without peer influence is $1.11 \pm 0.10$ points, against $0.15 \pm 0.07$ on the same seeds (Table~\ref{tab:effect}).
The level of default rests on two fitted parameters and on a borrowing-amount rule with no empirical estimate, which moves default by up to 1.02 points, and replacing the persistent income shock with a single-tick loss lowers it by 9.23 points (Tables~\ref{tab:robustness} and~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}).
The bureau experiment adds \ac{BNPL} obligations to the traditional lender's affordability calculation and excludes credit scores, pricing and lenders' responses, so the scoring channel of the reporting requirement is untested.
The standard errors measure variation between replicates and exclude uncertainty in the inputs and in the model's structure, and the scenario differences were not tested under the alternative borrowing rules.
The model does not measure household welfare.

\label{sec:future-work}
\textbf{Borrowing for a shortfall.} Evidence on how much households borrow against a shortfall, from which lender, and whether they can finance a checkout payment would settle the assumption on which the size of the \ac{BNPL} effect depends (Section~\ref{sec:amount}).
\textbf{Credit scoring.} A rule linking reported \ac{BNPL} behaviour to scores, pricing and refusals would test the channel that the reporting requirement most directly opens (Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry}).
\textbf{South African behavioural evidence.} Local estimates of minimum-payment behaviour, present bias, provider choice and the persistence of income shocks would replace the assumed and imported rules on which the outcome levels depend (Section~\ref{sec:lim-transfer}).
\textbf{Feedback and contagion.} A lender that restricts credit after losses, or a link from aggregate default to employment, would let distress spread through channels now excluded (Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}).
