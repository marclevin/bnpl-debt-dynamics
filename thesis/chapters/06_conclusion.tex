\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

% Numbers are read from the generated tables of Sections 3.5, 4 and 5 or from
% results/summary/results_numbers.json.

In the model the \ac{BNPL} effect is concentrated in households short from the start to whom the
traditional lender's Regulation 23A test never grants a loan. The platforms, whose screen does not
compare income with living costs, lend to them against a shortfall, and their \ac{BNPL} arrears
carry them into default. For households not short from the start the effect is small
($+0.11 \pm 0.07$ points). Over all households \ac{BNPL} adds $1.03 \pm 0.06$ points to default
without peer influence, and $1.13 \pm 0.06$ in the redrawn population. About half of the effect
depends on the platforms lending to households with no wage income
(Section~\ref{sec:results-injection}). Peer influence adds want-driven borrowing without
detectably changing the effect. Bureau visibility tightens only the lender's test, so the platforms still lend to the
households it refuses, while screening applies that test to every \ac{BNPL} request.

Every result covers two years, and each effect is a difference between arms, conditional on
their shared assumptions. Default follows the bureau's 90-day construct, and its level rests on the starting profile and the threshold (Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}). The
\ac{BNPL} effect depends on letting households borrow with \ac{BNPL} against a shortfall
(Section~\ref{sec:results-effect}) and on counting \ac{BNPL} arrears as default
(Section~\ref{sec:results-injection}). Which households start in arrears is an assumption. The
hire wage is imputed, and hires move income between quintiles (Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}).

For the reporting arrangements, the model gives only the sign of one channel, the lender's
test. Through it, bureau visibility raises default by
$0.20 \pm 0.07$ points without peer influence, because the households the lender refuses have no
other source of credit in the model. The model's rules favour that rise
(Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}). Screening's fall in default lies almost all in the poorest
quintile, but without peer influence more households go short of food
and rent. The runs record no amount unpaid (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios-distress}), so whether screening
leaves households better off is open.

Better data would most improve three inputs: how much South African households borrow with
\ac{BNPL} against a shortfall and who they are, the size and frequency of \ac{BNPL} purchases, and
how households choose among providers. The first would measure the shortfall path
(Section~\ref{sec:results-effect}), and the others would replace the assumptions that set volume
and stacking (Section~\ref{sec:results-stacking}).

A rule linking reported \ac{BNPL} behaviour to credit scores, pricing
and refusals would test the channel that the reporting arrangements most directly open
(Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry}). Giving refused households another source of credit, following
them after refusal and running past two years would show whether the rise in default under
bureau visibility survives. Re-running the injection with the larger of the derived and
single-question incomes for the households short from the start would show how far the effect
rests on how \ac{NIDS} records their income. A lender that restricts credit
after losses, or a link from aggregate default to employment, would let distress spread through
channels the model now excludes (Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}).
