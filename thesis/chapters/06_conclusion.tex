\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

% Numbers are read from the generated tables of Sections 3.5, 4 and 5 or from
% results/summary/results_numbers.json.

This thesis asked how adding a \ac{BNPL} channel changes credit distress in a model of South
African households, and how bureau visibility and affordability screening affect the outcome.
The answers are comparisons within the model under 2017 conditions, and each is conditional on
the assumptions the compared arms share.

\textbf{RQ1.} The population built from survey microdata matches external figures for
household size and income inequality and is of the right order for household debt. The
baseline holds its calibrated arrears profile as a share while the credit-active book halves,
which tests stability and says nothing about how arrears arise. Debt service is above what
households report, and the default rate has no external counterpart.

\textbf{RQ2.} Owing several platforms at once is common, at a rate set by the routing
assumption and by how often households borrow (Section~\ref{sec:results-stacking}). Enabling \ac{BNPL} raises default by
$1.03 \pm 0.06$ points without peer influence. The effect does not change detectably with
$\beta$ between 0 and 0.3 ($+0.003 \pm 0.010$ points per 0.1), although households borrow 2.4
times as much at 0.3. Default rises with access under both adoption rules and does not rise
detectably with the number of platforms. The effect runs through households that use \ac{BNPL}
to bridge cash shortfalls and then fall 91 or more days behind on it, and it is not detectable
when \ac{BNPL} cannot be used against a shortfall. Most of it comes from the 3.6\% of
households whose surveyed income does not cover their food and rent, to whom the platforms'
light screen lends where the Regulation 23A test would not.

\textbf{RQ3.} Bureau visibility raises default by $0.20 \pm 0.07$ points without peer
influence, because in the model refused households have nowhere else to borrow, and screening
lowers it by 0.93 to 1.03 points, about as much as \ac{BNPL} adds. Visibility, and screening without
peer influence, leave slightly more households short of food and rent (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios-distress}), and the runs do not
record how much goes unpaid, so the results do not show whether households are better off.

These findings identify where better data would most improve the analysis: how much South
African households borrow with \ac{BNPL} when short of cash and who they are, the size and
frequency of \ac{BNPL} purchases, how households choose among providers, and where refused
households borrow instead, including from informal lenders.

\label{sec:lim-reading}
The conclusions have further limits. Default follows the bureau's 90-day construct, but its
level rests on the starting profile and the threshold (Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}).
Which households start in arrears is an assumption, and the survey cannot say whether the
households whose income is below their food and rent are persistently short or have
under-recorded income (Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}). The wage of a hired jobseeker is
imputed, and hires move income between quintiles (Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}).

A rule linking reported \ac{BNPL} behaviour to credit scores, pricing
and refusals would test the channel that the reporting arrangements most directly open
(Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry}). Giving refused households another source of credit, following
them after refusal and running past two years would show whether the rise in default under
bureau visibility survives. Re-running the injection with the income of the households short
from the start set to the larger of their derived and single-question survey figures would show
how far the effect depends on how \ac{NIDS} records their income. A lender that restricts credit
after losses, or a link from aggregate default to employment, would let distress spread through
channels the model now excludes (Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}). A flow of new borrowers would
keep the credit-active book from shrinking over the run (Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}).
