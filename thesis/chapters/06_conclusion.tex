\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

% Numbers are read from the generated tables of Sections 3.5, 4 and 5 or from
% results/summary/results_numbers.json.

This thesis asked how adding a \ac{BNPL} channel changes credit distress in a model of South
African households, and how bureau visibility and affordability screening affect the outcome.
The answers are comparisons within the model under 2017 conditions. They do not estimate what
happened or forecast what the reporting arrangements will do.

\textbf{RQ1.} The population built from survey microdata matches external figures for
household size and income inequality and is of the right order for household debt, which
supports the inputs. The fitted baseline reproduces the two arrears bands it was fitted to but
not the bands between them, its debt service is above what households report, and its default
rate has no external counterpart, so its levels carry little weight.

\textbf{RQ2.} Owing several platforms at once is common, at a rate set mostly by the assumed
rule for choosing platforms and by how often households borrow; no arm tests platforms that
see one another. Enabling \ac{BNPL} raises default by $0.12 \pm 0.05$ points without peer
influence and by $0.60 \pm 0.07$ points at $\beta=1$, where households borrow ten times as
much. Default does not change detectably with the routing rule or the number of platforms. At
$\beta=1$ the rise is detectable in the middle quintiles and smaller in the poorest than in
Q4.

\textbf{RQ3.} Making \ac{BNPL} visible to the traditional lender raises its refusals by about
a sixth and default by 0.15 to 0.22 points, most in the poorest quintile, because a refused
household in the model has no other credit. Mandatory screening slightly reduces \ac{BNPL}
borrowing and does not change default detectably. Neither measure lowers aggregate default
under the model's rules.

These answers support four findings.

In the model, default follows how often households borrow through \ac{BNPL} more than how
many use it. Wider adoption under the threshold rule leaves default almost unchanged, whereas
more frequent purchases raise it. Whether the number of providers owed matters is not
settled: at $\beta=1$ a cap of two platforms owed lowers default by $0.35 \pm 0.09$ points,
against $-0.11 \pm 0.07$ for a cap on agreements that removes as much volume
(Appendix~\ref{app:levers}). Concern about concurrent obligations should therefore cover
repeat borrowing as well as the number of providers.

Purchase size sets the size of the effect when borrowing is frequent. At $\beta=1$ the effect
ranges from 0.25 points with half the reference purchase size to 1.22 points with purchases
sized on income, because larger purchases mean larger fortnightly instalments. Purchases that
match the provider figure give 0.9 to 1.2 points at $\beta=1$, but with volume more than four
times the provider's range. At $\beta=0$ the same purchase sizes keep volume within that range
and give no detectable effect.

How often households borrow drives both stacking and the effect on default. In the model that
frequency comes from a peer rule with no South African estimate, so the results at $\beta=1$
describe a market with far more repeat borrowing than the provider data show. The two values of $\beta$
bracket the provider's volume, and the effect lies between 0.1 and 0.6 points across them.

Bureau visibility acts on households through the traditional lender's refusals. Whether it
helps or harms them depends on what a refused household does next. The model cannot answer
that, because it has no informal or alternative credit, and the rise in default it reports
for the poorest quintile depends on that omission.

These findings identify where better data would most improve the analysis: the distribution
of \ac{BNPL} purchase sizes and the frequency of repeat purchases among South African users,
how much households borrow when short of cash, how they choose among providers, and where
refused households borrow instead, including from informal lenders. Each is an input on which a
headline result depends.

\label{sec:lim-reading}
The conclusions have further limits. The outcome is a cash-flow default, mostly a failure to
pay for food and rent, that the model defines and no external figure measures (Section~\ref{sec:sequence}). Its level rests on two
parameters fitted to an account-level arrears share that rises through the run, so the fitted
shock rate depends on the measurement window (Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}). It also rests
on the persistence of income shocks: with single-tick shocks default falls from 14.05\% to
4.81\% and the \ac{BNPL} effect disappears (Table~\ref{tab:effect}). The bureau experiment
excludes credit scores, pricing and lenders' responses, and assesses surveyed rather than
current income. The standard errors measure variation between replicates and exclude
uncertainty in the inputs, the drawn population and the model's structure.

\label{sec:future-work}
A rule linking reported \ac{BNPL} behaviour to credit scores, pricing
and refusals would test the channel that the reporting arrangements most directly open
(Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry}). Giving refused households another source of credit, following
them after refusal and running past two years would show whether the rise in default under
bureau visibility survives. A lender that restricts credit after losses, or a link from
aggregate default to employment, would let distress spread through channels the model now
excludes (Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}).
