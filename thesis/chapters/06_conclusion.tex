\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

% Numbers are read from the generated tables of Sections 3.5, 4 and 5 or from
% results/summary/results_numbers.json.

This thesis asked how adding a \ac{BNPL} channel changes credit distress in a model of South
African households, and how bureau visibility and affordability screening affect the outcome.
The answers are comparisons within the model under 2017 conditions.

\textbf{RQ1.} The population built from survey microdata matches external figures for
household size and income inequality and is of the right order for household debt. The
baseline starts from the 2017 arrears profile and, with two parameters fitted to its five bands,
holds that profile for two years. That tests stability, not how arrears arise. The book of
credit-active households halves over the run, debt service is above what households report, and
the default rate has no external counterpart.

\textbf{RQ2.} Owing several platforms at once is common, at a rate set by the routing
assumption (Section~\ref{sec:results-stacking}). Enabling \ac{BNPL} raises default by
$1.03 \pm 0.06$ points without peer influence. The effect is flat in $\beta$ between 0 and 0.3
($+0.003 \pm 0.014$ points per 0.1), although households borrow 2.4 times as much at 0.3.
Default rises with access under both adoption rules and does not rise detectably with the
number of platforms. The threshold adoption rule raises holding at full access by 14 to 34 points,
against 10 in its control, without changing the effect on default detectably.

\textbf{RQ3.} Making \ac{BNPL} instalments visible to the traditional lender raises its
refusals by about 13\% and default by $0.20 \pm 0.07$ points without peer influence, because in
the model refused households have nowhere else to borrow. With peer influence the rise,
$0.10 \pm 0.06$ points, is not detectable. Mandatory screening lowers default by
0.93 to 1.03 points, about as much as \ac{BNPL} adds, almost all in the poorest quintile.

The effect runs through households that use \ac{BNPL} to bridge cash shortfalls, in place of
purchases they would otherwise pay in cash, and then fall 91 or more days behind on it. It is
not detectable at either value of $\beta$ when \ac{BNPL} cannot be used against a shortfall
($-0.05 \pm 0.16$ and $+0.11 \pm 0.13$ points). Most of it comes from the 3.6\% of households
whose surveyed income does not cover their food and rent, to whom the platforms' light screen
lends where the Regulation 23A test would not. Screening applies that test to these requests.
Among the other households the effect is $0.11 \pm 0.07$ points at $\beta=0$, which is not
detectable, and $0.18 \pm 0.08$ at $\beta=0.3$. Purchase size, the borrowing rules, the platform limit and the minimum
payment leave the effect between 0.79 and 1.18 points.

These findings identify where better data would most improve the analysis: how much South
African households borrow with \ac{BNPL} when short of cash and who they are, the size and
frequency of \ac{BNPL} purchases, how households choose among providers, and where refused
households borrow instead, including from informal lenders.

\label{sec:lim-reading}
The conclusions have further limits. Default is 91 or more days past due on any facility, the
construct of the bureau figures, but its level rests on the starting profile and the definition:
6.36 of its 20.73 points are households placed in default at the start, and a 56-day definition
adds 13 points (Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}). Which households start in arrears is an
assumption, and the households whose income is below their food and rent come from the survey
records. The wage of a hired jobseeker is imputed, and hires move income between quintiles
(Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}).

A rule linking reported \ac{BNPL} behaviour to credit scores, pricing
and refusals would test the channel that the reporting arrangements most directly open
(Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry}). Giving refused households another source of credit, following
them after refusal and running past two years would show whether the rise in default under
bureau visibility survives. A lender that restricts credit after losses, or a link from
aggregate default to employment, would let distress spread through channels the model now
excludes (Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}). A flow of new borrowers would keep the credit-active
book from shrinking over the run (Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}).
