\section{Design Rationale: Alternatives Considered and Rejected}
\label{app:rationale}

The model's rules (Section~\ref{sec:model}) were chosen over the following alternatives.

\subsection{Population Construction}

\textbf{Statistical fusion of \ac{NIDS} and \ac{FinScope}.} A hot-deck or model-based fusion would
recover finer joint structure between the two instruments than copying flags within an
income-quintile-by-province cell, at the cost of its own validation burden and a risk of
fitting the idiosyncrasies of two surveys rather than the population behind them. The cell match
reproduces the weighted within-cell distribution of flag combinations, which is what the servicing
construction needs.

\textbf{\ac{CPI} forwarding to 2022.} An earlier design forwarded \ac{NIDS} monetary values to
2022, which required a second reference year, assumptions about how relative incomes moved between
the two, and 2022-vintage validation targets. Working entirely in 2017 Rands removes all three, and
the 2017 anchor is independently required by the counterfactual injection design.

\subsection{Household Decision Rules}

\textbf{A propensity-to-consume or buffer-stock consumption rule (Submodel~2).} Both require a
calibrated propensity, and we located no South African estimate suited to a fortnightly household
rule. Both also discard the split between committed and discretionary expenditure that the
survey data already record.

\textbf{Cost ranking as the lender-choice rule (Submodel~5).} \ac{BNPL} is nominally
interest-free, so a cost ranking would also send a shortfall to \ac{BNPL} first, but it would
attribute the order to price. The evidence does not support that attribution. In the one
experiment we found that compares \ac{BNPL} with a credit card, participants were offered one
or the other. \ac{BNPL} was interest-free and the card charged 24.99\% a year, although the
authors argue that neither option was clearly cheaper~\citep{ackert2025bnpl}. For groceries, the
one necessity, purchase rates were similar under the two. We therefore assume the
order directly and attribute it to no motive.

\textbf{Scheduled amortisation as the sole repayment rule (Submodel~6).} Keys and Wang
\citeyearpar{Keys2019} find that 29\% of credit-card accounts regularly pay at or near the contractual minimum, and
that at least 9\% of all accounts, and 22\% of near-minimum payers, respond to changes in the
minimum-payment formula in ways liquidity constraints alone cannot explain. A uniform amortisation rule would
suppress the behaviour by which obligations persist and compound.

\textbf{A debt-service-to-income threshold as the default condition (Submodel~7).} Any such
threshold would be arbitrary, and the \ac{NCA} prescribes none. The model uses the 90-days-past-due
convention instead, the construct of the bureau figures it is compared with~\citep{ncr_cbm_2017}. An
earlier version used a cash-flow test after Madeira~\citeyearpar{madeira2018chile}, under which seven
consecutive ticks of unpaid food, rent or debt service were default; it made food and rent
shortfalls the main route into default, and a household two months behind that caught up could
still default (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}).

\textbf{A continuous present-bias parameter (Submodel~8).} We located no South African distribution
against which to calibrate one, so it would add a free parameter with no discipline. The model
carries a binary repayment archetype instead, whose share is sourced and swept.

\subsection{Lender and Platform Rules}

\textbf{A flat \ac{DSTI} cap as the credit-granting gate (Submodel~9).} An earlier version of this
model used a ceiling of 0.65. It was unsourced, the \ac{NCA} prescribes no such ratio, and it was
over-permissive at the bottom of the distribution, since a 65\% rule would permit a household
earning R900 per month to service R585 per month. The Regulation 23A residual-income test produces
an income-varying ceiling, which across the population spans zero to 93.2\% of income.

\textbf{Making the \ac{BNPL} eligibility screen a real affordability test (Submodel~12).} The
asymmetry between the Regulation 23A test that binds registered lenders and the light screen
the platforms apply is one of the conditions the model studies, so a genuine test on
\ac{BNPL} is an intervention: the screening scenario of Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}.

\textbf{A purely multiplicative peer-influence rule (Submodel~11).} Every household starts with no
\ac{BNPL} balance, so under $q_i = \beta s_g$ want-driven borrowing could start only after
shortfall borrowing had created balances, and never where \ac{BNPL} relieves no shortfall. The
additive form of Equation~\ref{eq:peer} lets $q_{\text{base}}$ start adoption and $\beta$ amplify
it.

\subsection{Scheduling}

\textbf{Fixed activation order (Submodel~17).} A fixed order would let the same households act
first in every tick. A fixed-order arm does not change default detectably
(Appendix~\ref{app:activation}).
