\section{Calibration and Empirical Grounding}
\label{sec:calibration}

This section explains how the model is connected to South African household data.
We construct the population, assign debt-service and product parameters, and fit
two parameters to the baseline arrears profile. We then assess what the model
reproduces beyond those fitted targets.

The inputs differ in provenance. Some are observed directly, some are
constructed from survey fields, and others come from foreign studies or
modelling assumptions. Table~\ref{tab:param-taxonomy} separates these categories;
Appendix~\ref{app:specification} provides the full register. Sensitivity analysis
covers selected uncertain assumptions.

\subsection{Simulation Parameters}
\label{sec:sim-params}

Each run follows 5{,}000 households for 52 ticks, approximately two years.
The first 12 ticks are excluded from post-burn-in summaries, allowing the initial
state to develop before those measures are collected. Each configuration has
twenty replications, using seeds $s_0+i$ with a fixed starting seed for each
experiment suite. Reported dispersion measures variation across simulation runs
only and excludes uncertainty in the data and assumptions. Thresholds use a separate random
stream, allowing exact reproduction of control arms and paired comparisons across
threshold dispersion.

\subsection{The Household Population}
\label{sec:population}

The population contains 5{,}000 agents sampled from South African household
microdata. Monetary values are expressed in 2017 South African rand (R),
the year of the primary survey.

\subsubsection*{Primary Dataset: NIDS Wave 5}
\label{sec:backbone}

The starting point is \ac{NIDS} Wave 5 \cite{nids_w5_2018}. Filtering the
13{,}719 records for reported income, valid household size and positive sampling
weights leaves 10{,}841 households. All 2{,}878 excluded records have zero or
missing weights. Applying the survey weights gives
18.67 million households and roughly 56.5 million individuals.

We construct monthly income, its dominant source, committed expenditure,
discretionary expenditure, liquid savings and traditional debt. Income sources
are grouped as wage, grant, or remittance and other. Food and rent paid form
committed expenditure; other non-food cash spending is treated as discretionary.
Financial assets provide the savings proxy and are winsorised at the 99th
percentile. Outstanding financial debt supplies the traditional balance.

Imputed rent is excluded because it is not a cash payment. Missing asset, debt
and rent values in fields recorded for holders are set to zero. This is a
construction choice whose aggregate implications are examined in
Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}; the comparison cannot establish the correct
value for each missing observation. Households are grouped into per-capita income
quintiles. Appendix~\ref{app:variables} records the fields, formulas and
missing-data treatment, including the servicing calculation introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:servicing}.

\subsubsection*{Imputing Financial Inclusion: FinScope 2019}
\label{sec:match}

The model also needs banking status and a consistent set of credit-product
indicators. We impute these from the 2019 \ac{FinScope} survey \cite{finscope2019}.
Banked status determines eligibility for \ac{BNPL}, so the imputed banked share
of 82.8\% caps adoption in the constructed population. Five further flags identify store cards, revolving
credit, hire purchase, short-term loans and personal loans. These determine the
terms assigned in Section~\ref{sec:servicing}.

Figure~\ref{fig:cell-donor} shows the matching procedure. Both surveys are
partitioned by per-capita income quintile and province. Each \ac{NIDS} household
draws one donor from the corresponding cell, with probability proportional to
the donor's survey weight (seed: 42). All 45 cells contain at least 30 candidates,
so no draw requires a coarser match.

All six flags come from the same donor. This preserves combinations
observed in \ac{FinScope} and reproduces its weighted within-cell distribution
in expectation. It does not recover relationships with recipient characteristics
omitted from the match, such as urban or rural location. Exposure may therefore
be assigned differently within each cell. Using 2019 banking data may also
overstate access in 2017. Appendix~\ref{app:matching} explains these assumptions
and reports donor reuse.

\subsubsection*{Population Resampling}
\label{sec:resample}

We sample $N=5{,}000$ agents with replacement from the 10{,}841 households,
using probabilities proportional to survey weights (seed: 20260818). Simulation
statistics can then be calculated without applying weights again. The resample
approximates the weighted source distribution, with some sampling variation.
Figure~\ref{fig:pop-fidelity} shows the comparison: the per-capita income Gini
increases from 0.651 to 0.667.

\subsection{Debt-Service and Product Parameters}
\label{sec:servicing}

\ac{NIDS} provides the outstanding debt balance, but the simulation needs the
payment due each month. We reconstruct servicing using a product rate, a
repayment horizon and an affordability cap (Appendix~\ref{app:variables}).
Balances are amortised at the applicable 2017 statutory maximum \ac{APR}
\cite{sa_ncr_ratecaps_2016} over the horizons in Table~\ref{tab:credit-terms}.
The resulting payment is capped at the Regulation 23A residual-income ceiling
\cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015}.

The statutory rates are proxies. Realised product rates were not available in
the \ac{CCMR} \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017} or the other 2017 sources consulted.
Because realised rates cannot exceed the ceilings, this choice biases
calculated payments upward. Product allocation, horizons and the cap are also
approximations, so the net direction of error in servicing is undetermined. Each household's consolidated balance uses the unweighted mean
rate and horizon of its donor's flagged products. A balance with no flagged
product is assigned unsecured terms.

The cap affects relatively few households but makes a substantial aggregate
adjustment: it binds for 3.8\% of debtors and removes 22.8\% of calculated
service (Appendix~\ref{app:var-servicing}). There is also an accounting overlap.
The non-food expenditure aggregate already includes clothing-account,
hire-purchase and vehicle payments. Among households reporting such payments,
their median share of non-food outlay is 12.6\%. Adding reconstructed service
therefore partly duplicates a flow included in discretionary spending. This
overstates compressible outlay and understates service as a share of that outlay.
The combined effect on default and scenario differences cannot be determined
from these construction choices alone.

The \ac{BNPL} contract, order cap and late fees come from published provider
terms \cite{payflex_terms, payjustnow_terms}. Monetary amounts are deflated
to 2017 Rands. The purchase-size ratio $\kappa=0.139$ comes from the share of
discretionary spending on eligible categories in IES 2022/23
\cite{statssa_ies_2023}. Using a share avoids converting currency amounts,
but still assumes that the spending composition transfers to 2017.
No usable South African provider estimate was located for the rolling limit.
We therefore treat $\lambda$ as an assumption, informed by the stacked exposure
reported by Woolard \cite{woolard2021} and varied in Submodel~12.

\label{sec:lim-transfer}
The behavioural evidence discussed in Section~\ref{sec:lit-behavioural}
comes from United States borrowers. It supplies the minimum-payer share of
0.29 and motivates payment friction and want-driven borrowing, but provides
no local estimates for these rules. No comparable developing-market
evidence was located. We vary the minimum-payer share from 0.20 to 0.40,
while recognising that this cannot resolve the transfer assumption.
A continuous present-bias parameter is omitted because no South African
distribution was available to calibrate it.

\subsection{Estimation and Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:lim-calibration}

We fit two parameters to two parts of the 2017-Q1 \ac{CCMR} arrears
distribution (Table~\ref{tab:ccmr}; Pattern~2 in Table~\ref{tab:patterns}).
With \ac{BNPL} disabled, the income-shock probability is fitted to the 90+ day share
and payment friction to the 1--30 day share. They primarily affect different
parts of the distribution: persistent shocks generate long arrears, while
friction produces brief payment delays. The selected values are 1.6\% and
9\% per tick respectively. Both remain fixed when \ac{BNPL} is introduced.

The remaining bands test other features of the same distribution; outputs with
\ac{BNPL} enabled are assessed separately in Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}.
The fitted income-shock probability is about 1.4 times the upper end of the
observed separation-rate range, 0.54--1.17\% per tick. It may absorb omitted
sources of distress, such as illness, unexpected bills or family changes, and
is therefore interpreted as a reduced-form hazard of income loss and other
distress.

Other inputs retain their assigned values except where varied.
Neither $q_{\text{base}}$ nor $\beta$ has a direct empirical estimate.
Setting $\beta=0$ isolates the peer channel; a realistic adoption rate
remains unidentified. The banking ceiling, TransUnion's holding rate
\cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025} and the \ac{CFPB} stacking shares offer only
indirect comparisons. To address RQ2, we vary platforms from one to six and
access within the banked population, alongside peer influence. For RQ3, each
regulatory switch is compared with the benchmark with and without peer
influence (Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}).

\subsection{Validation}
\label{sec:data-validation}

The checks serve different purposes. Construction checks assess whether the
population preserves the intended data properties. Calibration checks assess
fit to selected targets. External comparisons use quantities not fitted or
directly imposed during construction, but their usefulness depends on whether
they measure comparable populations and outcomes.
Table~\ref{tab:patterns} lists the four patterns used to guide the assessment.

\label{sec:lim-validation}
Only Pattern~2 is both South African and matched to the model's base year.
Even here, the administrative unit is an account and the model unit is a
household. Arrears shares are calculated over credit-active households
(the 44\% holding traditional debt), while population default is tracked
separately. This improves the comparison but does not remove the unit mismatch,
which also limits interpretation of the fitted income-shock probability.

Pattern~1 is not independent of model development. Want-driven borrowing
was introduced partly because shortfall-only borrowing did not reproduce
the complementarity, so Pattern~1 serves as a diagnostic of the resulting
behaviour. Pattern~3 compares against a United Kingdom model. Pattern~4
compares zero savings with expected missed payments in a 2025 survey, which
are different outcomes. Patterns 1, 3 and 4 are therefore plausibility
comparisons.

These external tolerances are broad. The income Gini of 0.651 lies in the
0.630--0.700 interval, whose lower bound uses the World Bank's
consumption-based estimate \cite{worldbank_gini_zaf}. Income and consumption
inequality are not the same measure. Similarly, synthetic debt of
R191.4 billion is 49\% of the corresponding R392.0 billion
\ac{CCMR} book \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017}. Underreporting may contribute to
this gap, but the comparison does not identify its cause. Imputing missing
balances at holder means would instead produce 111\% of the administrative
book, so the debt comparison is sensitive to the missing-data treatment.

Mean household size passes its tolerance but, at 3.03, is below the
Community Survey estimate of 3.3 \cite{statssa_cs_2016}, and the weighted
population contains roughly 10\% more households: 18.67 million against
16.9 million.

Some households begin in a vulnerable position. Income does not cover
committed expenditure for 3.9\%, or committed expenditure plus scheduled
service for 5.2\%; 44.3\% have zero liquid savings. These groups overlap
and are not counts of initial default. Savings or credit may cover some
gaps, and zero savings alone does not trigger distress. The tick sequence
determines that state (Section~\ref{sec:sequence}).

Table~\ref{tab:bnpl-on-checks} reports the unfitted comparisons after
\ac{BNPL} is enabled. At the final tick, 45.0\% of holders have exposure
across multiple platforms. The over-the-horizon measure, 37.0\% of
ever-adopters, is closer in definition to the \ac{CFPB}'s 32\%, although
the markets and observation periods differ.

The mean-purchase comparison is marginal: R645.60 against R992, or
34.9\% below the target under a 35\% tolerance. The mean falls inside by
less than R1, and only eight of twenty replications fall inside, so the
agreement is weak although the stated rule is met. Annual volume also falls
short: R861 per eligible household and R1{,}020 per adopter, both below
the provider band's R1{,}333 lower bound. Pattern~1 is mixed: enabling
\ac{BNPL} raises traditional arrears but lowers traditional interest paid.
Pattern~3 holds for the specified aggregate statistic, and Pattern~4 is of
the same order, 25.9\% against 36\%.

RQ1 is therefore answered only in part. The model approximates the fitted
bands and meets several broad population checks, but misses intermediate
arrears, the servicing share, the volume band and the interest component of
Pattern~1. These results support using the model to investigate mechanisms,
with limited confidence in the empirical accuracy of its outcome levels.
