\subsection{Runs, Replicates and Inference}
\label{sec:sim-params}

Each run follows 5{,}000 households for 52 ticks, or two years. Which households hold the
starting arrears is set by a rule (Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}), so the first 12 ticks are
a burn-in, and arrears, savings, interest and \ac{BNPL} volume are averaged or summed over the
remaining 40. Default, the lender's loans and refusals, purchase sizes and the ``ever''
measures (at the end of any tick) cover the whole run.

\emph{Default during the run} is the share of all 5{,}000
households that enter default after the start: 14.37\% without \ac{BNPL}, or 15.35\% of those
not in default at the start. \emph{Default including the starting stock} adds the 6.36\% placed
in default at the start (Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}), 20.73\% in all. Default is
permanent, so this second measure is also the share ever in default, and tables and figures
report it unless they say otherwise. The arms of an experiment share a seed block
(Table~\ref{tab:experiments}). Within a block the number starting in default is the same in
every arm, except under the 56-day threshold (Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}), so it
cancels from differences between arms. They are the same households, except that a few differ
in arms that change access or a repayment parameter (Table~\ref{tab:experiments}).

% baseline_initial_default, baseline_default (results_numbers.json, seeds 10,000-10,019). Same
% starting households in every arm: checked at initialisation for rq0 and rq3s (QA A2, 2026-11-10).
% 7 of 318 and 24: initialisation only, seed 30,000, access 0.5 and 0.0 and min_payment_frac 0.10

Table~\ref{tab:experiments} lists the experiments and their replicates. The four scenario arms
have 100, because 20 cannot resolve their differences. Every run except the
population-size check uses the same 5{,}000 households (Section~\ref{sec:resample}).
Differences between arms are paired by seed, but after initialisation each run takes every draw
except the activation order from one stream, so the arms soon diverge and pairing reduces the
standard error little. Differences across seed blocks are treated as independent.

Unless a table states otherwise, it reports means over replicates with the replicate standard
deviation in brackets, and differences $\pm$ one standard error, paired by seed. Differences in
rates are in percentage points, written points in the text and pp in tables. Runs use the
reference values, four platforms and access for every banked household. A difference is
detectable when it exceeds two standard errors, and tables print it in bold. With 20 replicates
the standard error of a difference in default is about 0.13 points, so a true change of 0.26
points would be missed about half the time; it is about 0.06 with 100 replicates and 0.04 with
250. About one in twenty contrasts with no true difference passes by chance, so a single
detectable difference carries less weight than one that repeats across seed blocks. The standard
errors measure simulation variance only, not uncertainty in the data, the drawn population or
the assumptions.

\subsection{The Household Population}
\label{sec:population}

\subsubsection*{Primary Dataset: NIDS Wave 5}
\label{sec:backbone}

The starting point is \ac{NIDS} Wave 5~\citep{nids_w5_2018}, and monetary values are in
2017 Rands (R). Of its 13{,}719 household records, 10{,}841 have reported income, a valid
household size and a positive sampling weight; most of the others were not interviewed in
Wave 5. Weighted, the 10{,}841 represent 18.67 million households and about 56.5 million people.

We construct monthly income and its dominant source (wage, grant or other), committed and
discretionary expenditure, liquid savings and traditional debt. Committed expenditure is food
and rent paid. Other non-food cash spending, transport and utilities included, is discretionary,
because \ac{NIDS} offers no finer split. Savings are financial assets winsorised at the 99th
percentile, and traditional debt is outstanding financial debt. Imputed rent, not a cash
payment, is excluded. Asset, debt and rent fields are recorded only where held or paid, so
missing values are set to zero (tested in Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}). Households are
grouped into per-capita income quintiles. Appendix~\ref{app:variables} records the fields,
formulas and missing-data treatment.

\subsubsection*{Imputing Financial Inclusion: FinScope 2019}
\label{sec:match}

We impute banking status and credit-product indicators from the 2019 \ac{FinScope}
\citep{finscope2019}. Only banked households can use \ac{BNPL}, so the imputed banked
share of 82.8\% caps its use. It includes the 13\% of adults whose only account is a
grant card, which \ac{FinScope}'s banking question counts as a bank account. Five further flags identify store cards,
revolving credit, hire purchase, short-term loans and personal loans, and set the terms of
each household's opening debt (Section~\ref{sec:servicing}).

Figure~\ref{fig:cell-donor} shows the matching, within 45 cells of per-capita income quintile
and province. Each \ac{NIDS} household copies all six flags from one
donor in its cell, drawn with probability proportional to survey weight (seed 42), which
preserves the combinations of products observed in \ac{FinScope}. Every cell has at least 30
donors, so no cell needed a wider pool.

\ac{FinScope} interviews one adult per household (Appendix~\ref{app:match-assumptions}), so the
flags understate holding in households with several adults, and the 2019 banking data may
overstate access in 2017. Appendix~\ref{app:matching} discusses these assumptions and reports
donor reuse. National imputed rates are within 1.8 points of their \ac{FinScope} targets, and
within 2.3 in any quintile, which the match is built to give, so this tests its implementation.

\subsubsection*{Population Resampling}
\label{sec:resample}

We sample 5{,}000 households with replacement from the 10{,}841 records, with probability
proportional to survey weight (seed 20260818), so simulation statistics need no weights. The
resample raises the per-capita income Gini from 0.651 to 0.667
(Figure~\ref{fig:pop-fidelity}).

\subsection{Debt-Service and Product Parameters}
\label{sec:servicing}

\ac{NIDS} gives each household's outstanding debt but not the payment due on it. We amortise
each balance at the 2017 statutory maximum rate for its product class over the horizon in
Table~\ref{tab:credit-terms}~\citep{sa_ncr_ratecaps_2016}, capped at the Regulation 23A
residual-income ceiling~\citep{sa_ncr_affordability_2015} (Appendix~\ref{app:variables}).

No 2017 source we consulted, the \ac{CCMR} included~\citep{ncr_ccmr_2017}, reports realised
rates. These cannot exceed the statutory rate ceilings, so the ceilings put payments at or above
their true level. The error from the Regulation 23A payment cap, the product allocation and the
horizons has no known sign. The \ac{NIDS} non-food aggregate already includes clothing-account,
hire-purchase and vehicle payments (Appendix~\ref{app:var-expenditure}), so these are counted
twice, with an unknown effect on default. On net, constructed service is above the range
households report (Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}).

The \ac{BNPL} contract and late fees come from Payflex's published terms
\citep{payflex_terms}, deflated to 2017 Rands. The order cap of R15{,}000
(R9{,}927 in 2017 Rands) is an assumption, because Payflex publishes no maximum order value
\citep{payflex_terms,payflex_limits}. The mean purchase is $\kappa = 0.139$ of the monthly
discretionary budget, the \ac{IES} 2022/23 share of discretionary spending on eligible
categories~\citep{statssa_ies_2023}. Treating a month's spending in those categories as one
purchase, and 2022/23 shares as 2017's, are assumptions.
Payflex sets each customer's limit by its own assessment~\citep{payflex_limits}, and we found no
South African provider that publishes a limit, so $\lambda$ is an assumption, informed
by Woolard's~\citeyearpar{woolard2021} illustrative stacked exposure and swept from 0.10 to 1.00.

\subsubsection*{Transferred Behavioural Evidence}
\label{sec:lim-transfer}
The behavioural evidence of Section~\ref{sec:lit-behavioural} comes from United States

\subsection{Calibration}
\label{sec:lim-calibration}

The labour market is not fitted. The population holds 4{,}670 employed members and 1{,}113
unemployed under the strict definition. Re-employment is the \ac{QLFS} rate of 1.87\% per tick,
and separation, 0.446\%, is set so that separations equal hires in expectation. This is
below the observed band of 0.54 to 1.17\% per tick, because the population's strict
unemployment rate (Appendix~\ref{app:var-employed}) is below the \ac{QLFS}
rate~\citep{statssa_lmd_2022}. At the final tick employment is 0.2\% below its start, and
13.6\% of the members employed at the start are out of work. Within quintiles employment and
income drift, because jobseekers are concentrated in the lower quintiles and a hire brings its
quintile's median pay, above the mean wage per earner in Q1 and Q2 and below it in Q3 to Q5. By
the final tick household income is 14.9\% higher in Q1,
5.3\% lower in Q4, 5.8\% lower in Q5, the largest fall, and 3.9\% lower in total.

The arrears profile is set in three steps, with \ac{BNPL} disabled. First, credit-active
households, about 45\% of the population, are placed in each band of the 2017-Q1 \ac{CCMR} age
profile~\citep{ncr_ccmr_2017} at its share, with those that have least income left after food,
rent and debt service in the oldest bands. All those placed more than 90 days behind, 6.4\% of
households, start in default. No source says which households were in arrears in 2017, so this
placement is an assumption. Second, the write-off rate is derived. Accounts enter the 120+ band
at half the 91--120 share per tick, so a stationary 120+ stock of 12.45\% needs a write-off rate
of $(1.76/2)/12.45 = 0.0707$ per tick, if the 2017 profile was a steady state. Third, two
parameters are fitted jointly to the five arrears bands. Payment friction is the chance of
skipping a traditional instalment at random, whether or not the household could pay it. Arrears
persistence is the chance that a household already behind pays nothing again even when it has
the cash, so a run of such draws can carry a solvent household to default. Without persistence,
households with spare income clear old arrears at once and the profile collapses. The pair is
chosen from a grid by the smallest root sum of squared errors over 20 replicates
(Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}), and the values selected, 0.0575 and 0.72, stay fixed in every
experiment.

Table~\ref{tab:arrears-fit} reports the fit. Every band is within one point of the \ac{CCMR},
and the 90+ share averages 14.08\% after burn-in against 14.21\%, by construction, because the
run starts from these shares and two parameters were fitted to them. On the calibration seeds
(1{,}000--1{,}019) the 90+ share at the final tick is 13.84\%. That tick lies inside the fitted
average, so this is no independent check, but the profile has not drifted.

The profile is stable as a share but not as a book. Without \ac{BNPL}, credit-active households
fall from 2{,}237 at the start to 1{,}175 at the final tick, because written-off and repaid
accounts leave and the only new traditional borrowing is against shortfalls, whereas new
accounts replenish the \ac{CCMR} book~\citep{ncr_ccmr_2017}. The stable 90+ share is a ratio of two shrinking counts.

\subsection{Validation}
\label{sec:data-validation}

We keep four kinds of check apart. The survey population and the starting arrears profile are
inputs, so comparing them with external figures tests the data. The five arrears bands are
calibration targets (Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}), so agreement on them is not evidence.
Stability and consistency checks, among them the construction checks of
Appendix~\ref{app:matching}, test whether the fitted model holds its starting state and is
correctly implemented. Only quantities the model was neither fitted to nor built from can
validate it, and only as far as they are comparable.

\label{sec:lim-validation}
Following pattern-oriented modelling~\citep{grimm2005pom} and the validation literature
\citep{windrum2007validation}, three
patterns guide the behavioural comparison. Pattern~1 is United States evidence that \ac{BNPL} adoption raises
card late fees and interest~\citep{dehaan2024bnpl}, whose counterparts in the model are
traditional arrears and interest. It is not independent of the design, since want-driven
borrowing was added partly because shortfall-only borrowing failed to reproduce it. Pattern~2 is
the 2017 arrears profile, an input and a target, so its stability is a consistency check. Pattern~3 is a middle-income peak in debt-to-income from a United Kingdom
model~\citep{hamill2023creditcard}. Only Pattern~2 is South African and from 2017, and even there the \ac{CCMR}
counts accounts and the model households, a mismatch that computing arrears shares over
credit-active households narrows without removing.

\subsubsection*{Default}

No external figure measures household default directly; bureau figures bound its level. With \ac{BNPL} off, default during the run is 14.37\%, about 7.2\% a year. We report it as
the level because the starting stock is an input. Three comparisons bear on the level, all on
seeds 10{,}000--10{,}019.
The first is the flow. Each tick 0.83\% of the credit-active book passes 90 days, against 0.82\%
if the \ac{CCMR} 91--120 day band holds 1.76\% of accounts for 30 days; the write-off derivation
instead lets half the band age out each tick (0.88\%), and on the 30-day convention its rate
would be 0.0660. The band was a target, so this checks consistency only.
The second is the stock. By the final tick 43.7\% of the households that held credit at any tick
have defaulted, 13.4 points of it from the starting stock, against the 39.3\% of credit-active
consumers with impaired records in the \ac{NCR}'s Credit Bureau Monitor for March 2017
\citep{ncr_cbm_2017}. Impaired records also include judgments and adverse listings, and the
Monitor counts consumers where the model counts households.
The third is arrears at the final tick, when 14.16\% of credit-active households are more than
90 days behind, below the Monitor's 21.7\% of consumers three or more months in arrears, which
also covers mortgage, vehicle and service-provider accounts the model does not hold.

\subsubsection*{What the Validation Supports}

The main limitations concern how the model generates distress: an arrears profile set by its
starting point and two fitted parameters, a book that halves over the run and the high opening
debt service. They bound every statement about the level of distress. Other gaps reflect missing
South African data: default has no external counterpart, purchase size and volume rest on one
provider's disclosures, the adoption parameters have no estimate, and the quintile
results describe a population in which the poorest quintile holds the most defaults and most of
its debt is written off.

The model therefore supports comparisons between arms that share its population,
parameters and seeds better than statements about levels, but each difference is still
conditional on the assumptions the arms share. A misspecified mechanism that interacts with the
change tested biases the difference as well as the level. Section~\ref{sec:results-effect} repeats the
\ac{BNPL} comparison under alternative borrowing, repayment and purchase-size rules. No arm
varies the debt-service construction, the starting profile and who holds it, the payment order,
arrears persistence or the rule that food and rent cannot be cut.

\subsection{Answer to RQ1}
\label{sec:calibration-answer}

The constructed population reproduces household size, income inequality and
the order of magnitude of household debt. With two parameters fitted to its five bands, the
baseline holds the 2017 arrears profile as a share for two years while the credit-active book
halves; this shows stability. Of the comparisons not used in fitting, traditional arrears rise
with \ac{BNPL} as Pattern~1 expects, but the assumed payment order builds the rise in.
Traditional interest does not change detectably, and stacking and volume agree in order of
magnitude only. Debt service exceeds what households report, and bureau figures can only bound
the default rate.
