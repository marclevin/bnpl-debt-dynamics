\subsection{Runs, Replicates and Inference}
\label{sec:sim-params}

Each run follows 5{,}000 households for 52 ticks, or two years. The run starts from the 2017
arrears profile, but which households hold those arrears is set by a rule, not by the model
(Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}), so the first 12 ticks are a burn-in: arrears, savings,
interest and \ac{BNPL} volume are averaged or summed over the remaining 40. Default, the
traditional lender's loans and refusals, purchase sizes and the ``ever'' measures cover the whole
run, and the default rate includes the households placed in default at the start. ``Ever'' means
at any tick of the run; a household that ever held \ac{BNPL}, for example, held a balance at
the end of at least one of the 52 ticks.

Table~\ref{tab:experiments} lists the experiments. Each arm has twenty replicates, except the
four scenario arms, which have one hundred because twenty cannot resolve the differences
between them, the $\beta$ sweep, which has 250, and the variance decomposition, which has two
per design point. Every run uses
the same 5{,}000 households (Section~\ref{sec:resample}). Arms in a block share seeds, so a
replicate starts from the same draws in every arm, and differences between arms are taken seed
by seed. Each run takes every later draw from one random stream, so the arms soon diverge and
pairing reduces the standard error little. Differences across seed blocks are treated as independent.

Unless a table states otherwise, it reports means over replicates with the replicate standard
deviation in brackets, and differences $\pm$ one standard error, paired by seed. Differences in
rates are in percentage points, written points in the text and pp in tables. Runs use the
default parameters, four platforms and access for every banked household. We call a
difference detectable when it exceeds two standard errors, and tables print such differences
in bold. With twenty replicates the standard error of a difference in default is about 0.13
points, so a true change of 0.26 points would be missed about half the time; with one hundred
it is about 0.06 points and with 250 about 0.04. We judge many contrasts this way, so about one in twenty would pass
by chance, and a single detectable difference carries less weight than one that repeats
across seed blocks. The standard errors measure simulation variance only, not uncertainty in
the data, the drawn population or the assumptions. Error bars in figures are 95\% confidence intervals.

\subsection{The Household Population}
\label{sec:population}

\subsubsection*{Primary Dataset: NIDS Wave 5}
\label{sec:backbone}

The starting point is \ac{NIDS} Wave 5~\citep{nids_w5_2018}, and monetary values are in
2017 Rands (R). Keeping the records with reported income, a valid household size and a
positive sampling weight leaves 10{,}841 of 13{,}719 households; every excluded record has a
zero or missing weight. Weighted, they represent 18.67 million households and about 56.5
million people.

We construct monthly income and its dominant source (wage, grant or other), committed and
discretionary expenditure, liquid savings and traditional debt. Food and rent paid form
committed expenditure. Other non-food cash spending, including transport and utilities, is
discretionary, because \ac{NIDS} offers no finer split. Financial assets, winsorised at the
99th percentile, proxy savings, and outstanding financial debt is the traditional balance.
Imputed rent is excluded because it is not a cash payment. Asset, debt and rent fields are
recorded only for holders, so missing values are set to zero; Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}
tests the aggregate consequence. Households are grouped into per-capita income quintiles.
Appendix~\ref{app:variables} records the fields, formulas and missing-data treatment.

\subsubsection*{Imputing Financial Inclusion: FinScope 2019}
\label{sec:match}

We impute banking status and credit-product indicators from the 2019 \ac{FinScope}
\citep{finscope2019}. Banked status determines eligibility for \ac{BNPL}, so the imputed
banked share of 82.8\% caps \ac{BNPL} use. \ac{FinScope}'s banking question counts a grant card as a bank
account, so banked adults include the 13\% whose only account is that card, and the imputed
share inherits this. Five further flags identify store cards,
revolving credit, hire purchase, short-term loans and personal loans, and set the terms of
each household's opening debt (Section~\ref{sec:servicing}).

Figure~\ref{fig:cell-donor} shows the matching. Both surveys are divided into cells by
per-capita income quintile and province. Each \ac{NIDS} household draws one donor from its
cell with probability proportional to the donor's survey weight (seed 42) and copies all six
flags from it, which preserves the combinations of products observed in \ac{FinScope}. The
procedure falls back to a quintile-only pool when a cell has no donors. Every one of the 45
cells has at least 30, so the fallback was never used.

\ac{FinScope} records one adult's holdings, so the flags understate holding in households
with several adults. The 2019 banking data may overstate access in 2017. Appendix~\ref{app:matching} discusses these assumptions and reports donor
reuse. National imputed rates differ from their \ac{FinScope} targets by at most 1.8
percentage points, and by at most 2.3 points within any quintile.

\subsubsection*{Population Resampling}
\label{sec:resample}

We sample 5{,}000 agents with replacement from the 10{,}841 households, with probability
proportional to survey weight (seed 20260818), so simulation statistics need no weights. The
resample raises the per-capita income Gini from 0.651 to 0.667
(Figure~\ref{fig:pop-fidelity}).

\subsection{Debt-Service and Product Parameters}
\label{sec:servicing}

\ac{NIDS} provides each household's outstanding debt but not the payment due on it. We
amortise each balance at the 2017 statutory maximum rate for its product class over the
horizon in Table~\ref{tab:credit-terms}~\citep{sa_ncr_ratecaps_2016}, and cap the payment at
the Regulation 23A residual-income ceiling~\citep{sa_ncr_affordability_2015}
(Appendix~\ref{app:variables}).

No 2017 source we consulted, the \ac{CCMR} included~\citep{ncr_ccmr_2017}, reports realised
rates, which cannot exceed the ceilings, so payments are overstated. The product allocation,
horizons and ceiling err in unknown directions. The \ac{NIDS} non-food aggregate already includes
clothing-account, hire-purchase and vehicle payments, so these are counted twice, as debt service
and as discretionary spending, with an unknown effect on default. On net, constructed service is
above the range households report (Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}).

The \ac{BNPL} contract and late fees come from Payflex's published terms
\citep{payflex_terms}, deflated to 2017 Rands. The order cap of R15{,}000
(R9{,}927 in 2017 Rands) is an assumption, because Payflex publishes no maximum order value
\citep{payflex_terms,payflex_limits}. The mean purchase is $\kappa = 0.139$ of the monthly
discretionary budget, the \ac{IES} 2022/23 share of discretionary spending on eligible
categories~\citep{statssa_ies_2023}. Treating a month's spending in those categories as one
purchase, and 2022/23 shares as 2017's, are assumptions.
No South African provider publishes a platform limit, so $\lambda$ is an assumption, informed
by Woolard's illustrative stacked exposure~\citeyearpar{woolard2021} and swept from 0.10 to 1.00.

\subsubsection*{Transferred Behavioural Evidence}
\label{sec:lim-transfer}
The behavioural evidence of Section~\ref{sec:lit-behavioural} comes from United States
borrowers. It supplies the minimum-payer share of 0.29 and the case for payment friction and
want-driven borrowing; we located no South African or other developing-market estimates.
Varying the minimum-payer share from 0.20 to 0.40 shows how much the value matters but not
whether it transfers. A continuous present-bias parameter is omitted because no South
African distribution was available to calibrate it.

\subsection{Calibration}
\label{sec:lim-calibration}

The labour market is not fitted. In the population, 4{,}670 members are employed and 1{,}113
are strictly unemployed. Re-employment is the \ac{QLFS} rate of 1.87\% per tick, and we set
separation to 0.446\% per tick so that separations equal hires and employment is constant in
expectation. This is below the observed job-separation band of 0.54 to 1.17\% per tick~\citep{statssa_lmd_2022}, because
\ac{NIDS} records a lower unemployment rate than the \ac{QLFS}. After two years employment is
within 0.1\% of its starting level, although 13.5\% of the members employed at the start are
out of work at the final tick. Employment is constant in total but not within quintiles.
Jobseekers are concentrated in the lower quintiles, so by the final tick employment is 30\% above its start in Q1 and 12\% below
it in Q5. Left running, the uniform hazards would equalise unemployment across quintiles. A hire brings the median pay of employed adults in its quintile, which is above the
mean wage per earner in Q1 and Q2 and below it in Q3 to Q5. Household income at the final tick
is therefore 15.2\% and 3.2\% above its start in Q1 and Q2, and 3.6\%, 6.1\% and 5.7\% below
it in Q3, Q4 and Q5. In total it is 3.9\% lower.

The arrears profile is set in three steps, with \ac{BNPL} disabled. First, the run starts from
the 2017-Q1 \ac{CCMR} age profile~\citep{ncr_ccmr_2017}. Credit-active households, about 45\%
of the population at the start, are placed in each band at its \ac{CCMR} share, with
the households that have least income left after food, rent and debt service in the oldest
bands. Those placed at 91 days or more, 6.4\% of all households, start in default. No source
says which households were in arrears in 2017, so this placement is an assumption. Second, the
write-off rate is derived, not fitted. Accounts enter the 120+ band at half the 91--120 share
per tick, so a stationary 120+ stock of 12.45\% needs a write-off rate of
$(1.76/2)/12.45 = 0.0707$ per tick, which assumes the 2017 profile was a steady state. Third, two
parameters are fitted jointly to the five arrears bands: payment friction, the chance of
skipping an affordable traditional instalment, and arrears persistence, the chance that a
household already behind pays nothing again. Without persistence, households with spare income
clear old arrears at once and the profile collapses. The pair is chosen from a grid by the
smallest root sum of squared errors over twenty replicates (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}),
and the selected values, 0.0575 and 0.72, stay fixed in every experiment.

Table~\ref{tab:arrears-fit} reports the fit. Every band is within one point of the \ac{CCMR},
and the 90+ share averages 14.08\% after burn-in against 14.21\%. This agreement is by
construction, because the run starts from these shares and two parameters were fitted to them,
so it is not evidence that the model generates arrears as the market does. The evidence is that
the profile holds: with three more targets than fitted parameters, the untargeted 90+ share is
13.84\% at the final tick, so after two years the profile has not drifted.

The profile is stable as a share but not as a book. Without \ac{BNPL}, credit-active households
fall from 2{,}285 at the start to 1{,}175 at the final tick, because written-off and repaid
accounts leave and the only new traditional borrowing is against shortfalls, whereas new
accounts replenish the \ac{CCMR} book. The stable 90+ share is a ratio of two shrinking counts.

\subsection{Validation}
\label{sec:data-validation}

We check the model in two ways, setting aside the arrears bands of
Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}. Comparisons of the constructed population with external
figures test the survey inputs. Comparisons of the model's behaviour test quantities it was
neither fitted to nor built from, and are only as informative as those quantities are
comparable. Checks that the population reproduces its own sources test the implementation;
they are in Appendix~\ref{app:matching} and are not counted as validation.

\label{sec:lim-validation}
Following pattern-oriented modelling~\citep{grimm2020odd} and the validation literature
\citep{windrum2007validation}, four
empirical patterns guide the behavioural comparison. Pattern~1 is United States evidence that \ac{BNPL} adoption raises
card late fees and interest~\citep{dehaan2024bnpl}, whose counterparts in the model are
traditional arrears and interest; it is not independent of the design,
because want-driven borrowing was added partly when shortfall-only borrowing failed to
reproduce it. Pattern~2 is the 2017 arrears profile, which is now an input and a target, so only its stability tests the model. Pattern~3 is a middle-income peak in debt-to-income from a United Kingdom
model~\citep{hamill2023creditcard}. Pattern~4 sets the share of households without savings
against the share of South African consumers who expect to miss a payment in an online survey
\citep{transunion_cps_sa_2025}, a different outcome, so it can test only the order of
magnitude. Only Pattern~2 is South African and from 2017, and even there the \ac{CCMR}
counts accounts and the model counts households. Computing arrears shares over credit-active
households narrows the mismatch without removing it.

\subsubsection*{Default}

No external figure measures household default directly, but the credit bureau figures bound its
level. With \ac{BNPL} off, 14.37\% of households default during the two-year run, about 7.2\% a
year. A further 6.36\% are placed in default at the start, so 20.73\% are in default at some tick.
We report default during the run as the level, and the starting stock separately, because the
stock is an input. Three comparisons place the level, all on seeds 10{,}000--10{,}019.
The first is the flow. Each tick 0.83\% of the credit-active book reaches 91 days, against the
0.82\% implied by the \ac{CCMR} 91--120 day band, which holds 1.76\% of accounts for 30 days.
That is about 19\% of the book a year. The band was a target, so this is consistency rather than
an independent check.
The second is the stock, which was not targeted. By the final tick 43.7\% of the households that
held credit at any tick have defaulted, against the 39.3\% of credit-active consumers with
impaired records in the \ac{NCR}'s Credit Bureau Monitor for March 2017 \citep{ncr_cbm_2017}.
Impaired records also include judgments and adverse listings, and the Monitor counts consumers,
not households.
The third is arrears at the final tick: 13.84\% of credit-active households are 91 or more days
behind, below the Monitor's 21.7\% of consumers three or more months in arrears, which also
covers retail and telecoms accounts the model does not hold.

\subsubsection*{Behaviour with BNPL}

Table~\ref{tab:bnpl-on-checks} compares the model with \ac{BNPL} against external evidence.
Pattern~1 holds for traditional arrears at both values of $\beta$, and traditional interest does
not change detectably; interest tracks the size of the book more than servicing stress
(Section~\ref{sec:results-injection}). Households pay \ac{BNPL} instalments before traditional
debt, so \ac{BNPL} obligations can crowd out traditional payments. Pattern~3 is reproduced at
the final tick, with debt-to-income highest in Q4, but probably mechanically. At the start Q1's
ratio is 0.81 against Q4's 1.19, and by the final tick it is 0.08, because Q1 has the highest
default rate and defaulted accounts are written off. We do not count Pattern~3 as evidence.
Pattern~4 agrees in order of magnitude; the share without savings falls below its starting
value because most of those households have income above their outlays and save the difference.

\subsubsection*{What the Validation Supports}

The serious failures concern how the model generates distress: an arrears profile set by its
starting point and two fitted parameters, a book that halves over the run, the high opening debt
service and a default rate with no external counterpart. They bound every statement about the
level of distress.

Other gaps reflect missing South African data more than visible misspecification. No source
gives the size or frequency of South African \ac{BNPL} purchases by household, so purchase
size and volume rest on one provider's disclosures and the adoption parameters have no
estimate. Statements about which quintiles are affected describe a population in which the
poorest quintile holds the most defaults and most of its debt is written off.

The model therefore supports comparisons between arms that share its population,
parameters and seeds, more than statements about levels. A misspecified mechanism can still
distort a comparison if it interacts with the change being tested, so
Section~\ref{sec:results-effect} repeats the \ac{BNPL} comparison under alternative borrowing,
repayment and purchase-size rules; no arm varies the debt-service construction or the starting
profile.

In answer to RQ1, the population built from survey microdata reproduces household size,
income inequality and the order of magnitude of household debt, which supports the inputs
rather than the model. The baseline starts from the 2017 arrears profile and, with two
parameters fitted to its five bands, holds it for two years, although the book of credit-active
households halves. Its debt service exceeds what households report, and its default rate cannot
be checked.
