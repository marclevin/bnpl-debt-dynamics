\subsection{Runs, Replicates and Inference}
\label{sec:sim-params}

Each run follows 5{,}000 households for 52 ticks, or two years. Every household starts with
no arrears, so the first 12 ticks are a burn-in: arrears, savings, interest and \ac{BNPL}
volume are averaged or summed over the remaining 40. Default, the traditional lender's loans
and refusals, purchase sizes and the ``ever'' measures cover the whole run. ``Ever'' means
at any tick of the run; a household that ever held \ac{BNPL}, for example, held a balance at
the end of at least one of the 52 ticks.

Table~\ref{tab:experiments} lists the experiments. Each arm has twenty replicates, except the
four scenario arms, which have one hundred because twenty cannot resolve the differences
between them, and the variance decomposition, which has two per design point. Every run uses
the same 5{,}000 households (Section~\ref{sec:resample}). Arms in a group share seeds, so a
replicate starts from the same draws in every arm, and differences between arms are taken seed
by seed. Each run takes every later draw from one random stream, so the arms soon diverge and
pairing reduces the standard error little. Differences across seed groups are treated as independent.

Unless a table states otherwise, it reports means over replicates with the replicate standard
deviation in brackets, and differences with one standard error, paired by seed. Runs use the
calibrated parameters, four platforms and access for every banked household. We call a
difference detectable when it exceeds two standard errors, and tables print such differences
in bold. With twenty replicates the standard error of a difference in default is about 0.07
points, so a true change of 0.14 points would be missed about half the time; with one hundred
it is about 0.03 points. We judge many contrasts this way, so about one in twenty would pass
by chance, and a single detectable difference carries less weight than one that repeats
across seed blocks. The standard errors measure simulation variance only, not uncertainty in
the data, the drawn population or the assumptions. Error bars in figures are 95\% confidence intervals.

\subsection{The Household Population}
\label{sec:population}

\subsubsection*{Primary Dataset: NIDS Wave 5}
\label{sec:backbone}

The starting point is \ac{NIDS} Wave 5 \cite{nids_w5_2018}, and monetary values are in
2017 Rands (R). Keeping the records with reported income, a valid household size and a
positive sampling weight leaves 10{,}841 of 13{,}719 households; every excluded record has a
zero or missing weight. Weighted, they represent 18.67 million households and about 56.5
million people.

We construct monthly income and its dominant source (wage, grant or other), committed and
discretionary expenditure, liquid savings and traditional debt. Food and rent paid form
committed expenditure. Other non-food cash spending, including transport and utilities, is
discretionary, because \ac{NIDS} offers no finer split. Financial assets, winsorised at the
99th percentile, proxy savings, and outstanding financial debt is the traditional balance.
Imputed rent is excluded because it is not a cash payment. Asset, debt and rent fields are
recorded only for holders, so missing values are set to zero; Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}
tests the aggregate consequence. Households are grouped into per-capita income quintiles.
Appendix~\ref{app:variables} records the fields, formulas and missing-data treatment.

\subsubsection*{Imputing Financial Inclusion: FinScope 2019}
\label{sec:match}

We impute banking status and credit-product indicators from the 2019 \ac{FinScope}
\cite{finscope2019}. Banked status determines eligibility for \ac{BNPL}, so the imputed
banked share of 82.8\% caps \ac{BNPL} use. \ac{FinScope}'s banking question counts a grant card as a bank
account, so banked adults include the 13\% whose only account is that card, and the imputed
share inherits this. Five further flags identify store cards,
revolving credit, hire purchase, short-term loans and personal loans, and set the terms of
each household's opening debt (Section~\ref{sec:servicing}).

Figure~\ref{fig:cell-donor} shows the matching. Both surveys are divided into cells by
per-capita income quintile and province. Each \ac{NIDS} household draws one donor from its
cell with probability proportional to the donor's survey weight (seed 42) and copies all six
flags from it, which preserves the combinations of products observed in \ac{FinScope}. The
procedure falls back to a quintile-only pool when a cell has no donors. Every one of the 45
cells has at least 30, so the fallback was never used.

\ac{FinScope} records one adult's holdings, so the flags understate holding in households
with several adults. The 2019 banking data may overstate access in 2017. Appendix~\ref{app:matching} discusses these assumptions and reports donor
reuse. National imputed rates differ from their \ac{FinScope} targets by at most 1.8
percentage points, and by at most 2.3 points within any quintile.

\subsubsection*{Population Resampling}
\label{sec:resample}

We sample 5{,}000 agents with replacement from the 10{,}841 households, with probability
proportional to survey weight (seed 20260818), so simulation statistics need no weights. The
resample raises the per-capita income Gini from 0.651 to 0.667
(Figure~\ref{fig:pop-fidelity}).

\subsection{Debt-Service and Product Parameters}
\label{sec:servicing}

\ac{NIDS} provides each household's outstanding debt but not the payment due on it. We
amortise each balance at the 2017 statutory maximum rate for its product class over the
horizon in Table~\ref{tab:credit-terms} \cite{sa_ncr_ratecaps_2016}, and cap the payment at
the Regulation 23A residual-income ceiling \cite{sa_ncr_affordability_2015}
(Appendix~\ref{app:variables}).

Realised product rates were not available in the \ac{CCMR} \cite{ncr_ccmr_2017} or the
other 2017 sources consulted. Realised rates cannot exceed the ceilings, so using the
ceilings overstates payments. The product allocation, horizons and cap are approximations
whose direction of error is unknown. The \ac{NIDS} non-food aggregate already includes
clothing-account, hire-purchase and vehicle payments, so constructed service partly
double-counts them and discretionary spending is overstated; that error could move default
either way. Section~\ref{sec:data-validation} finds constructed service above the range
households report, consistent with a net overstatement.

The \ac{BNPL} contract and late fees come from Payflex's published terms
\cite{payflex_terms}, deflated to 2017 Rands. The order cap of R15{,}000
(R9{,}927 in 2017 Rands) is an assumption: Payflex states no maximum order value and says
that a customer's spending limit depends on an individual assessment
\cite{payflex_terms, payflex_limits}. The purchase-size ratio $\kappa = 0.139$ is the share
of discretionary spending on eligible categories in \ac{IES} 2022/23
\cite{statssa_ies_2023}, which assumes that the composition of spending transfers to 2017.
No South African provider publishes a rolling limit, so $\lambda$ is an assumption, informed
by Woolard's illustrative stacked exposure \cite{woolard2021} and swept from 0.10 to 1.0.

\subsubsection*{Transferred Behavioural Evidence}
\label{sec:lim-transfer}
The behavioural evidence of Section~\ref{sec:lit-behavioural} comes from United States
borrowers. It supplies the minimum-payer share of 0.29 and the case for payment friction and
want-driven borrowing; we located no South African or other developing-market estimates.
Varying the minimum-payer share from 0.20 to 0.40 shows how much the value matters but not
whether it transfers. A continuous present-bias parameter is omitted because no South
African distribution was available to calibrate it.

\subsection{Calibration}
\label{sec:lim-calibration}

The labour market is set from data and is not fitted. In the population, 4{,}670 members
are employed and 1{,}113 are strictly unemployed. With re-employment at the \ac{QLFS} rate of
1.87\% per tick, employment stays constant in expectation if each employed member loses work
with probability 0.446\% per tick, so that separations equal hires. This is slightly below the
observed job-separation band of 0.54--1.17\% per tick \cite{statssa_lmd_2022}, because
\ac{NIDS} records a lower unemployment rate than the \ac{QLFS}. After two years employment is
within 0.1\% of its starting level, although 13.5\% of the members employed at the start are
out of work at the final tick. Employment is constant in total but not within quintiles.
Jobseekers are concentrated in the lower quintiles, so by the final tick employment is 30\% above its start in Q1 and 12\% below
it in Q5. A hire brings the median pay of employed adults in its quintile, which is above the
mean wage per earner in Q1 and Q2 and below it in Q3 to Q5. Household income at the final tick
is therefore 15.2\% and 3.2\% above its start in Q1 and Q2, and 3.6\%, 6.1\% and 5.7\% below
it in Q3, Q4 and Q5. In total it is 3.9\% lower.

\subsection{Validation}
\label{sec:data-validation}

We check the model in two ways, after setting aside the fitted 1--30 day arrears band, which
is a calibration target and so not evidence. Comparisons of the constructed population with external
figures test the survey inputs. Comparisons of the model's behaviour test quantities it was
neither fitted to nor built from, and are only as informative as those quantities are
comparable. Checks that the population reproduces its own sources test the implementation;
they are in Appendix~\ref{app:matching} and are not counted as validation.

\label{sec:lim-validation}
Following pattern-oriented modelling \cite{grimm2020odd} and the validation literature
\cite{windrum2007validation}, four
empirical patterns guide the behavioural comparison. Pattern~1 is United States evidence that \ac{BNPL} adoption raises
card late fees and interest \cite{dehaan2024bnpl}, whose counterparts in the model are
traditional arrears and interest; it is not independent of the design,
because want-driven borrowing was added partly when shortfall-only borrowing failed to
reproduce it. Pattern~2 is the 2017 arrears profile, of which only the 1--30 day band was fitted. Pattern~3 is a middle-income peak in debt-to-income from a United Kingdom
model \cite{hamill2023creditcard}. Pattern~4 sets the share of households without savings
against the share of South African consumers who expect to miss a payment in an online survey
\cite{transunion_cps_sa_2025}, a different outcome, so it can test only the order of
magnitude. Only Pattern~2 is South African and from 2017, and even there the \ac{CCMR}
counts accounts and the model counts households. Computing arrears shares over credit-active
households, the 45\% that owe traditional debt or arrears at the start, narrows the mismatch
without removing it.

\subsubsection*{Arrears and Default}

The 90+ band was not targeted, so it is evidence (Table~\ref{tab:arrears-fit}). It averages
9.10\% of credit-active households after burn-in, about two-thirds of the \ac{CCMR}'s
14.21\%. The \ac{CCMR} figure is a stock built up over years, most of it more than 120 days in arrears.
Every household in the model starts current and no account is written off, so the band rises
through the run and reaches 11.03\% at the final tick. A two-year run cannot rebuild the whole
stock. The bands from 31 to 120 days are missed: each holds 0.4 to 0.5\% of credit-active
households, against 1.8 to 3.6\% in the \ac{CCMR}. Households in the model tend either to
cure quickly or to stay in arrears, so the model does not show that arrears develop as they
do in the data.
% 11.03%: fit_90plus_final in results/summary/results_numbers.json (seeds 1,000-1,019).

No external figure measures the model's default. The nearest are from the \ac{NCR}'s Credit
Bureau Monitor: in March 2017, 21.7\% of credit-active consumers were three or more months in
arrears, and 39.3\% had impaired records, which also include judgments and adverse listings
\cite{ncr_cbm_2017}. The model's 7.3\% of households over two years is about a third of the
first figure. The bureau figures count individuals at one date and include listings that stay
on record for years, so the level of default is not validated. Most defaulters owe credit, 84.8\%
in the baseline and 97.9\% with \ac{BNPL} at $\beta=0$, but default is mostly a failure to pay
for food and rent: 84.1\% of baseline defaulters were short of them in every tick of the
streak that ended in default, and 15.2\% defaulted on debt service alone. Of baseline
defaulters, 37\% have an earner out of work when they default.

\subsubsection*{Behaviour with BNPL}

Table~\ref{tab:bnpl-on-checks} compares the model with \ac{BNPL} against external evidence.
Pattern~1 holds for traditional arrears only at $\beta=1$; at $\beta=0$ the change is not
detectable. Traditional interest falls at $\beta=0$ and does not change detectably at
$\beta=1$. Neither measure
discriminates well. Interest falls at $\beta=0$ because \ac{BNPL} partly replaces
traditional credit and the book shrinks (Section~\ref{sec:results-injection}), so it tracks
the size of the book more than servicing stress. Households pay \ac{BNPL} instalments
before traditional debt, so \ac{BNPL} obligations push traditional arrears up; the rise is
detectable once peer influence raises \ac{BNPL} volume.
Pattern~3 is not reproduced: debt-to-income is highest in Q1. Pattern~4 agrees in order of
magnitude; the share without savings falls below its starting value because most of those
households have income above their outlays and save the difference.

\subsubsection*{What the Validation Supports}

The failures differ in kind. The serious ones concern how the model generates distress: the
missed intermediate bands, the high opening debt service and a default rate with no external
counterpart. They bound every statement about the level of distress.

Other gaps reflect missing South African data more than visible misspecification. No source
gives the size or frequency of South African \ac{BNPL} purchases by household, so purchase
size and volume rest on one provider's disclosures and the adoption parameters have no
estimate.

Some misses are expected of a stylised model: Pattern~3 comes from another model of another
country, and a model with one lender, no calendar, no write-offs and a two-year horizon cannot
be expected to reproduce an account-level age analysis band by band.

The model therefore supports comparisons between arms that share its population,
parameters and seeds, more than statements about levels. A misspecified mechanism can still
distort a comparison if it interacts with the change being tested, so
Section~\ref{sec:results-effect} repeats the \ac{BNPL} comparison under alternative borrowing,
repayment and purchase-size rules; no arm varies the debt-service construction. Statements
about which quintiles are affected describe a population whose debt-to-income profile across
quintiles does not match the reference pattern.

In answer to RQ1, the population built from survey microdata reproduces household size,
income inequality and the order of magnitude of household debt, which supports the inputs
rather than the model. The baseline reproduces the one arrears band it was fitted to and
about two-thirds of the untargeted 90+ day share, and misses the bands between them. Its debt
service exceeds what households report, and its default rate cannot be checked.
