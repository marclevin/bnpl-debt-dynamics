This section answers the second research question from the final experiment run: six suites, 3{,}840 runs, twenty replicates per arm with unique seeds, at the shock probability and payment friction fitted in Section~\ref{sec:calibration} and with no \ac{BNPL}-side parameter re-tuned.
Every number is generated from the run output by script, and each float's note states its denominator, seeds and uncertainty convention.
Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms} states the baseline before any counterfactual.
With \ac{BNPL} disabled, 13.40\% of households are in default at the end of the horizon, and 13.68\% of credit-active households are ninety or more days in arrears against the \ac{CCMR}'s 14.21\%; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-arrears} gives the profile band by band.
The replicate standard deviation of the default rate is about a third of a percentage point, so one standard error on a difference between two arms is about a tenth of a point, and every difference below carries its own.
Enabling \ac{BNPL} at four platforms, with access at the banked ceiling, raises default by $1.22 \pm 0.11$ points without peer influence and by $2.07 \pm 0.10$ points with it.
Sections~\ref{sec:results-stacking} to~\ref{sec:results-robustness} take the stacking half of RQ2, the access half, the distribution of the effect and its robustness in turn.

\subsection{Invisible Credit and Stacking}
\label{sec:results-stacking}

Stacking is emergent, not programmed.
No rule assigns a household a number of facilities: each platform applies its own rolling limit against the household's income and cannot see the others (Submodel~13), and a household that wants a purchase draws wherever it has headroom.
The one-platform arm fixes the counterfactual.
With a single platform no household can hold facilities at two firms, so the share holding two or more is zero by construction, while default is 14.56\%, already above the no-\ac{BNPL} baseline (Table~\ref{tab:stacking}).
A second platform produces stacking at once: 6.4\% of all households hold balances on two platforms at the final tick without peer influence, 7.6\% with four platforms and 8.0\% with six; with $\beta = 1$ the shares are 43.7\%, 54.4\% and 57.0\% (Figure~\ref{fig:stacking}a).
The rise is concave, and cumulative volume shows why: it rises when a second platform is added and is flat from two platforms to six, as is adoption.
The second platform relieves the households whose first limit binds; further platforms add headroom that demand does not use, because purchases are sized to the household's own budget (Submodel~4).

Among households holding any \ac{BNPL} balance at the final tick, 45.3\% hold two or more facilities with four platforms and no peer influence, and 83.0\% with peer influence.
The \ac{CFPB} reports that 32\% of United States borrowers held loans at more than one firm over a year \cite{cfpb2025bnpl}.
The comparison is one of order of magnitude, and the denominators differ: the \ac{CFPB} counts borrowers over a period, while the holder share is a point in time.
The closer analogue is the share of households that ever held a balance and at some tick held two or more, which is 37.1\% with four platforms and no peer influence (Table~\ref{tab:stacking}).
With peer influence it is 98.5\%: a household that adopts in a group where most have adopted returns to the product until it holds agreements on several platforms at once.
The model therefore produces stacking of the observed order without being fitted to it, and far more of it once adoption is socially transmitted.

Default does not follow the platform count.
Across one to six platforms the population default rate moves within replicate noise: default at six platforms less default at one is $-0.04 \pm 0.09$ points without peer influence and $+0.18 \pm 0.10$ points with it (Table~\ref{tab:stacking}; Figure~\ref{fig:stacking}c).
The injection raises default, but spreading the same obligations across more firms does not raise it further: the platform count changes where a household's facilities sit, not how much it takes on.
The harm the model attributes to \ac{BNPL} is the obligation, not its invisibility across firms.
The second half of RQ2 is therefore answered in the negative, and that answer is the point on which Section~\ref{sec:scenarios} turns.

\subsection{BNPL Access, Peer Influence and Default}
\label{sec:results-rq2}

\textbf{Default rises smoothly with access, and there is no threshold under either coupling.}
Figure~\ref{fig:access-default} and Table~\ref{tab:access} sweep \ac{BNPL} access from zero to the whole banked subpopulation at five values of $\beta$ under the linear rule of Equation~\ref{eq:peer}, and at five threshold dispersions under the Granovetter alternative, each with its control.
In the $\beta = 0$ control, default rises by $1.41 \pm 0.10$ points from zero to full access, and a straight line through the seven arm means has an $R^2$ of 0.987; at $\beta = 3$ the rise is $2.35 \pm 0.10$ points and the $R^2$ is 0.994.
No arm has an $R^2$ below 0.98 or a residual above a tenth of its rise.
Peer influence amplifies the response, by a factor of about 1.7 between the control and the strongest coupling, but does not change its shape.
The registered hypothesis, that default responds non-linearly to access only where social transmission is present, is not supported: the control is as linear as every other arm.

The threshold arm separates adoption tipping from distress tipping.
With heterogeneous thresholds and $\gamma = 0.3$, adoption at full access rises by up to 49.8 points across the access range, against 16.7 in the $\gamma = 0$ control (Figure~\ref{fig:access-default}b): the cascade mechanism works as Granovetter's argument says it should.
Default nonetheless rises by between 1.32 and 1.70 points in the threshold arms against 1.31 in the control, near-linearly in every arm ($R^2 \geq 0.955$).
Moving the threshold mean from 0.15 to 0.45 cuts adoption at full access from 50.0\% to 26.2\% and moves default from 14.94\% to 14.76\% (Table~\ref{tab:access}).
Adoption and default answer to different things: adoption to the social signal, default to the obligations a household carries against its income and buffer.

\label{sec:no-cascade}
The model has no feedback from household default to other households' income or to lenders' capacity to lend (Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}).
It therefore cannot test contagion through those channels.
Peer influence produces abrupt adoption, as the threshold arm shows, but the obligations it creates are serviced household by household, and nothing lets one household's default raise another's.
That is why adoption can tip while default cannot: the social channel enters the model's input and never its output.

Pattern~1 is mixed.
Enabling \ac{BNPL} raises the traditional arrears rate, by $0.64 \pm 0.07$ points without peer influence and $1.14 \pm 0.06$ with it, but lowers the interest paid on traditional debt, by 2.1\% and 1.7\% (Table~\ref{tab:bnpl-on-checks}).
The lender-choice rule routes an eligible household's shortfall to \ac{BNPL} first, so new traditional lending falls from R6.57 million to R3.48 million and the gate refuses 1{,}033 applications against 1{,}228 (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}).
The arrears direction is partly designed in by that rule (Section~\ref{sec:lim-validation}); the interest result is not, and it says that within the horizon \ac{BNPL} substitutes for traditional borrowing at the margin while raising distress on the traditional debt already held.
deHaan et al.\ find both credit-card interest and late fees rising after adoption \cite{dehaan2024bnpl}; the model reproduces the arrears half of that finding and not the interest half.

\subsection{Distribution and Mechanism}
\label{sec:results-distribution}

Table~\ref{tab:distribution} places the injection by income quintile.
Baseline default is highest in the bottom quintile (17.93\%) and the fourth (15.86\%) and lowest in the fifth (10.22\%); the fourth quintile also carries the highest aggregate debt-to-income ratio, 1.49 against 1.32 in the bottom quintile, which is the middle-income peak that Pattern~3 registers.
The injection raises default in every quintile, by between $0.87 \pm 0.22$ points in the second and $1.79 \pm 0.29$ in the third, so the effect is not concentrated at the bottom.
Adoption is nearly uniform, 14.7\% to 19.2\%, because eligibility depends on being banked and the origination propensity is the same for every eligible household (Submodel~11), while the mean purchase scales with the budget, from R102 in the bottom quintile to R1{,}888 in the top.
The near-uniform adoption follows from that rule; the size of the rise in each quintile does not.

The rolling limit binds hardest where purchases are smallest: 25.7\% of bottom-quintile requests are refused by a platform's limit, against 7.3\% in the top quintile.
Yet the limit barely changes default: sweeping $\lambda$ from 0.10 to 1.0 months of income moves the rate from 15.60\% to 15.54\% (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}).
The two facts are consistent.
The limit trims the size of a purchase and does not stop the household borrowing; a trimmed purchase still creates a pay-in-four obligation, and it is the obligation that produces distress.
Where harm concentrates is therefore not where the limit binds but where the buffer is thinnest relative to the obligation, which the by-quintile rows locate in the third quintile rather than the first.

\subsection{Sensitivity and Robustness}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

Table~\ref{tab:robustness} and Figure~\ref{fig:tornado} rank the sensitivity arms by the range of the default rate each produces, and the uncited rule moves the answer most.
Across the three shortfall-borrowing rules, capped and uncapped, default ranges from 13.30\% to 15.54\%.
The rule that adds a tick of committed spending to the shortfall lowers default by $2.15 \pm 0.09$ points relative to the exact-shortfall rule, because a household that borrows more than its gap holds the excess as savings and meets the next tick's committed spending from it: over-borrowing is protective within a horizon in which the extra debt has not yet had to be serviced in full.
Removing the cap on \ac{BNPL}'s share of a shortfall raises default under the two larger rules, by $0.64 \pm 0.13$ and $0.92 \pm 0.10$ points on paired seeds, and not under the exact-shortfall rule ($0.09 \pm 0.11$).
Neither rule has an empirical anchor (Section~\ref{sec:amount}), so the level of default carries this range.
The default horizon matters next: at $k = 4$ ticks, the 60-day convention, default is 16.79\% against 15.45\% at the 90-day $k = 7$.
The purchase-size ratio moves default from 15.00\% to 16.22\% over half to double its derived value, and sizing purchases on income rather than discretionary spending adds $0.54 \pm 0.12$ points.

The remaining arms are in Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}.
A fixed activation order gives 15.47\% against 15.42\% under random order; population size gives 15.05\%, 15.42\% and 15.49\% at 1{,}000, 5{,}000 and 10{,}000 households; the minimum-payer share is flat from 0.20 to 0.40 (15.52\% to 15.62\%).
The Sobol decomposition (Table~\ref{tab:sobol}) attributes essentially all variance in the level of default to the fitted shock probability (first-order index $1.02 \pm 0.15$) and gives every \ac{BNPL}-side parameter a total-order index of 0.02 or less.
It describes the level of default under parameter uncertainty, not the \ac{BNPL} effect, which is a difference between arms at fixed parameters and is not decomposed.
Finally, the single-tick shock rule gives a default rate of 5.66\%: an income loss that lasts one tick cannot produce the arrears the \ac{CCMR} records, which is why the shock persists until re-employment.
