\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section answers RQ2. Every arm uses the parameters set in
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}; no \ac{BNPL} parameter is re-tuned. The conventions for
replicates, standard errors and detectability are those of Section~\ref{sec:sim-params}.
Because $\beta$ has no empirical estimate, we report mainly $\beta=0$, the control without peer
influence, and $\beta=1$, an illustrative value at which most households that use \ac{BNPL}
buy with it repeatedly.

\subsection{Baseline and the BNPL Injection}
\label{sec:results-injection}

Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms} compares the baseline with \ac{BNPL} enabled. On these seeds the
baseline's 90+ share after burn-in is 9.17\%.
% 9.17%: active_90_plus_mean, baseline_no_bnpl, results/raw/rq0.parquet.

Without peer influence, enabling \ac{BNPL} does not change default detectably. Four seed
blocks estimate the effect, at $-0.10$ to $+0.06$ points, and none exceeds two standard errors.
Pooled by precision, the effect is $-0.03 \pm 0.03$ points, which excludes a rise of more than
about 0.04 points. With $\beta=1$ it is $0.17 \pm 0.04$ points, pooled over three blocks. Both
are small beside a baseline default rate of 7.28\%. The 90+ share changes by
$-0.05 \pm 0.09$ points at $\beta=0$ and $+0.10 \pm 0.10$ at $\beta=1$, neither detectable.
% Pooled effects: beta0_effect_pooled and beta1_effect_pooled, results/summary/results_numbers.json
% (blocks: Tables tab:baseline-arms, tab:access, the threshold control and tab:effect).
% 90+ changes: arrears90_change_beta0 and arrears90_change_beta1 (+ _se), results_numbers.json
% (seeds 10,000-10,019, Table tab:baseline-arms).

The effect at $\beta=1$ comes with much more borrowing by about as many borrowers. Peer
influence raises the share of households that ever hold \ac{BNPL} only from 69\% to 75\%, but
volume rises thirteenfold, because those who use \ac{BNPL} buy with it repeatedly: a household that
ever holds a balance makes 37 want-driven purchases over the two years at $\beta=1$, against
2.5 at $\beta=0$. The share of households with no liquid savings rises by $2.66 \pm 0.06$
points at $\beta=1$ and does not change detectably at $\beta=0$ ($+0.10 \pm 0.06$). These are
population aggregates; they do not show that the households that default are the ones whose
savings were depleted.
% Purchases per ever-holder and savings changes: want_per_everholder_beta*,
% zero_savings_change_beta*, results/summary/results_numbers.json.

At $\beta=0$ \ac{BNPL} partly replaces traditional credit. An eligible shortfall goes to
\ac{BNPL} first (Submodel~5), so the mean traditional loan falls by R$152 \pm 21$ and new
traditional lending by R$0.38 \pm 0.09$ million, 15\%, while refusals fall by $17 \pm 8$. At
$\beta=1$ the mean loan falls by a similar amount, R$171 \pm 18$, but lending falls by less,
R$0.24 \pm 0.08$ million, because households apply for traditional credit more often:
$363 \pm 48$ more applications, against $81 \pm 60$ at $\beta=0$. This is consistent with
want-driven purchases depleting savings.
% mean_grant_change_*, lending_change_*, refused_change_*, applications_change_*,
% results/summary/results_numbers.json.

Used only to cover shortfalls, \ac{BNPL} does not change default detectably
($+0.08 \pm 0.07$ points with the want-driven path switched off; Table~\ref{tab:effect}).
The detectable effect at $\beta=1$ comes with frequent want-driven borrowing.

\subsection{Stacking Across Platforms}
\label{sec:results-stacking}

Owing several platforms at once is common among users (Table~\ref{tab:stacking};
Figure~\ref{fig:stacking}). With four platforms and no peer influence, 36\% of the households
holding \ac{BNPL} at the final tick owe two or more, and 31\% of ever-holders do so at some
point, close to the \ac{CFPB}'s 32 to 33\%. At $\beta=1$ almost every ever-holder does.

That near-universal share follows from the model's rules. At $\beta=1$ an ever-holder makes
about 37 purchases in 52 ticks, each agreement stays open for three ticks, and under random
routing a new request goes first to a platform the household does not owe with probability
$3/4$, so almost every such household owes two platforms at some tick.
Under loyal routing, in which a household tries platforms it already owes first, the share
of ever-holders who stack falls from 98\% to 51\% at $\beta=1$, and from 31\% to 15\% at
$\beta=0$, on the same seeds and with the same volume. How often households
stack is therefore set by the routing assumption and by how often they borrow, and the model
has no South African evidence on either.
% Routing shares and volumes: stack_*_random_beta*, stack_*_loyal_beta*, volume_*_beta*,
% results/summary/results_numbers.json (seeds 70,000-70,019).

Default does not change detectably with the routing rule ($+0.00 \pm 0.03$ points at
$\beta=0$ and $+0.09 \pm 0.06$ at $\beta=1$); differences of about 0.2 points at $\beta=1$
cannot be excluded. Default does not rise with the number of platforms. Six platforms
against one change it by $-0.05 \pm 0.06$ points at $\beta=0$ and $-0.15 \pm 0.06$ at
$\beta=1$, and across the five values of $\beta$ by $-0.15$ to $+0.15$, detectable only at
$\beta=1$ (Table~\ref{tab:stacking-full}). Each platform brings its own limit, so platform
count and total credit capacity move together, and the sweep cannot isolate the effect of
platforms not seeing one another. More capacity may give more shortfall relief, which would
explain the fall at $\beta=1$; we did not test this. Against an uncapped arm on its own seeds, a
cap of two platforms owed lowers default by $0.11 \pm 0.05$ points at $\beta=1$, as does a cap
on agreements; against the benchmark the platform cap is not detectable (Table~\ref{tab:cap};
Appendix~\ref{app:levers}).

\subsection{BNPL Access, Peer Influence and Default}
\label{sec:results-rq2}

Table~\ref{tab:access} varies the share of banked households allowed to use \ac{BNPL}.
Without peer influence, moving from zero to full access raises holding by 14 points and does
not change default detectably. With peer influence, holding rises by 53 to 69 points and
default by 0.17 to 0.31 points, detectably at every value of $\beta$.

\label{sec:no-cascade}
At $\beta=1$ and $\beta=3$ the rise is close to proportional to access: straight lines through
the seven arm means have $R^2$ of 0.94 and 0.87 (Figure~\ref{fig:access-default}). The fit is
poor at $\beta=2$ ($R^2=0.46$), where default rises in two separate steps, by
$0.21 \pm 0.07$ points between 15\% and 30\% access and by $0.16 \pm 0.07$ between 85\% and
full access. A tipping point would show as one step followed by a level, and the neighbouring
values of $\beta$ show no step, so nothing in these data suggests one. The design could still
miss one, because the seven access
levels are widely spaced and replicate noise is large beside rises of 0.2 to 0.3 points. No
channel in the model carries one household's default to another's income or to a lender's
supply of credit (Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}), so contagion through those channels lies
outside the test.
% beta = 2 steps between adjacent access levels, paired by seed: results/raw/rq2.parquet
% (access_max_step_beta2 and access_steps_detectable_beta2 in results_numbers.json).

The threshold rule tests whether default depends on how adoption spreads. With $\gamma=0.3$
it raises holding at full access by 30 to 50 points, against 14 at $\gamma=0$, yet default
changes by $-0.05$ to $+0.05$ points, none detectable. Raising the mean threshold from 0.15 to 0.45 lowers full-access
holding from 51\% to 21\% without changing default detectably ($+0.02 \pm 0.07$ points).
Holding is close to linear in access under this rule too ($R^2$ of at least 0.98), so no
adoption cascade occurs on this grid.
% Threshold arms less control, paired by seed: results/raw/rq2t.parquet (computed 2026-10-12);
% mu_adoption_*, mu_default_diff and thr_adoption_r2_min, results_numbers.json.

Holding at the final tick rises with how often households borrow as well as with how many use
\ac{BNPL}. The access sweeps vary how many. The peer rules mainly vary how often: the share that ever holds \ac{BNPL} at full
access is 69\% without peer influence and 71 to 76\% under either peer rule, so the threshold
rule and $\beta=0.5$ mainly make the same households buy more often. Their volume is 1.9 to 6.0
times the control's, and the extra rise in default is at most 0.11 points. At $\beta=1$ volume is 13 times the control's and the extra rise is $0.16 \pm 0.08$
points. From $\beta=1$ to
$\beta=3$ volume after burn-in rises by 9\% and default does not change detectably
($+0.08 \pm 0.08$ points).
% Ever-held, volume ratios and extra rises: results/raw/rq2.parquet and rq2t.parquet, full
% access against zero access on the same seeds, less the control's rise (computed 2026-10-12).
No household can use \ac{BNPL} at zero access, so each linear-rule row of
Table~\ref{tab:access} also estimates the \ac{BNPL} effect. At $\beta=0.5$ that estimate is
$0.17 \pm 0.05$ points, equal to the pooled effect at $\beta=1$, with volume six times the control's and
just above the provider's range (Table~\ref{tab:bnpl-on-checks}).

% beta = 0 (6.0 times) and the provider range R1,333-R4,261 of tab_bnpl_on_checks.

\subsection{Distribution Across Income Quintiles}
\label{sec:results-distribution}

Baseline default is highest in Q1, at 13.83\%, about twice that of any other quintile, and
lowest in Q5. Q1 and Q4 have the highest debt-to-income ratios (Table~\ref{tab:distribution}).
The Q1 excess comes from households whose income is below their food and rent
(Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}); among the others, default is lowest in Q1, at 2.77\%, and
highest in Q4, at 5.93\%.
% baseline_contestable_default_rate_Q1 and _Q4, results/summary/contestable_default.json.
Without peer influence, enabling \ac{BNPL} lowers default in Q1 by $0.20 \pm 0.10$ points and
changes it detectably in no other quintile. At $\beta=1$ the effect is detectable only in Q4,
at $+0.44 \pm 0.18$ points. In Q1 it is $-0.10 \pm 0.15$ points. The Q4 effect exceeds the Q1
effect by $0.54 \pm 0.25$ points, a contrast between the largest and smallest of the five
effects, chosen after seeing them. Q4 loses 6.1\% of its income over the run, more than any
other quintile (Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}). That loss may explain its effect. No arm tests this.

The quintile pattern is therefore weak. Ten independent comparisons at two standard errors
would give at least one detectable result by chance about a third of the time. Purchases are
sized on discretionary budgets, so the mean purchase rises from R108 in Q1 to R1{,}886 in Q5,
and small purchases mean small instalments. That may explain the smaller effect in Q1, but the
experiment does not test it, and Q5, with the largest purchases, shows no detectable rise.
% Chance rate: 1 - 0.9545^10 = 0.37 for ten independent comparisons at two standard errors.

Platform limits bind more often on poorer households: a limit reduces or refuses a quarter
of platform requests in Q1, against about one in ten in Q5. Raising the limit tenfold changes
population default by $+0.10 \pm 0.05$ points (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}).

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

Simulation noise is small beside the assumptions. Default varies by about 0.2 to 0.3 points
between replicates, and results do not change detectably with population size or activation
order (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}). The model is not in a steady state: default
accumulates, so every result belongs to the two-year horizon.

The level of default depends mainly on how persistent income losses are
(Figure~\ref{fig:tornado}; Tables~\ref{tab:robustness} and~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}).
Replacing persistent job loss with a single-tick loss, an arm that also runs without labour
flows (no hires, wage-dominant households only), lowers default at $\beta=1$ from 7.54\% to
4.78\% and the 90+ share from 9.50\% to 7.60\%.
% 7.54% and 9.50%: default_rate_final and active_90_plus_mean, rob_amount_shortfall (seeds
% 50,000-50,019); 4.78% and 7.60%: the same, rob_shock_single_tick; results/raw/robustness.parquet.
The derived separation rate is below the observed band (Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}). A
higher rate raises default (Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}), and an earlier version with a rate
of 1.6\% and no hires gave a \ac{BNPL} effect of 0.60 points (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}),
so along this dimension the effect of 0.17 points is at the low end.
Of the behavioural rules, the default threshold moves the level most: shortening it to 56 days raises default by
$0.41 \pm 0.06$ points. A minimum payment of 10\% of the balance a month raises it by
$0.25 \pm 0.05$, and larger purchases raise it by 0.17 to 0.18 points. The borrowing-amount rules
do not change default detectably while the \ac{BNPL} share of a shortfall is capped.

\subsubsection*{What Moves the BNPL Effect}
\label{sec:results-effect}

Table~\ref{tab:effect} runs every setting with \ac{BNPL} off and on, on one shared seed block.
At $\beta=1$ the effect is positive under every setting and detectable under sixteen of the
twenty-one. The reference effect on this block, $+0.15 \pm 0.08$ points, falls just short of
two standard errors.

Two assumptions move the effect most: purchase size and how much a household borrows against a
shortfall. Halving $\kappa$ leaves the effect at $0.15 \pm 0.06$ points; doubling it raises
the effect to $0.41 \pm 0.06$, and sizing purchases on income raises it to $0.38 \pm 0.08$. A
larger purchase means larger fortnightly instalments, which leave less cash for food, rent and
traditional debt. Borrowing a tick of committed
spending on top of a shortfall raises the effect to $0.49 \pm 0.06$ points, and to
$0.51 \pm 0.06$ without the cap. The remaining settings, including loyal routing, removing
\ac{BNPL} from the shortfall path and the minimum-payment rules, keep the effect between 0.11
and 0.28 points.

Purchase size is poorly measured. The reference $\kappa$ gives a mean purchase of R616 at $\beta=1$, 38\% below the provider-based figure of
R992. Two settings, $\kappa=0.28$ and income-based sizing, give mean purchases within that
figure's tolerance at both values of $\beta$. At $\beta=1$ their effects are 2.5 to 2.7 times
the reference, but their volume, R19{,}000 to R21{,}000 per eligible household a year, is 4.5 to
4.9 times the top of the provider's range. At $\beta=0$ their volume is R1{,}564 and
R1{,}251, near the bottom of that range, and their effects, $+0.02 \pm 0.06$ and
$-0.02 \pm 0.07$ points, are not detectable. The settings closest to the provider data on both
measures therefore agree with the absence of an effect without peer influence
(Section~\ref{sec:results-injection}).

At $\beta=0$ the effect is detectable under two of the twenty-one settings, both of which add
a tick of committed spending to the amount borrowed, and it is never detectably negative. When
households borrow rarely, larger purchases have little to act on. The settings share a seed block and
often a no-\ac{BNPL} arm, so these counts are not independent replicates.

Under the single-tick shock, which also drops labour flows,
the effect disappears at both values of $\beta$. That arm lowers default among households whose
income covers their food and rent from 3.88\% to 1.19\%. It shows that the effect works through
households in a spell of lost income but does not isolate persistence.
% 3.88% and 1.19%: eff_ref_off_contestable and eff_shock_single_tick_off_contestable,
% results/summary/contestable_default.json (seeds 70,000-70,019).
The effect also
disappears when borrowing for food and rent that a loan pays in full is not counted as
distress: $-0.01 \pm 0.04$ and $+0.02 \pm 0.04$ points, with default without \ac{BNPL}
at 4.38\% instead of 7.24\%. Under that rule a loan clears the tick's distress, so households
that lose income keep borrowing from the traditional lender, which assesses surveyed income
and lends about three times as much as in the reference arm. Neither arm is recalibrated. The effect of \ac{BNPL}
therefore depends on households in a spell of lost income and on counting a household that
borrows for food and rent as distressed.

\subsection{Answer to RQ2}
\label{sec:results-answer}

Owing several platforms at once is common among users, at a rate set mostly by the assumed
routing rule and by how often households borrow. Enabling \ac{BNPL} does not change default
detectably without peer influence ($-0.03 \pm 0.03$ points) and raises it by
$0.17 \pm 0.04$ points at $\beta=1$, about 5\% of the default rate among households whose income
covers their food and rent (Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}). Default does not change
detectably with the routing rule and does not rise with the number of platforms. A cap on
platforms owed and a cap on agreements that removes about the same volume change default
equally (Appendix~\ref{app:levers}).
% Cap comparison: results/summary/cap_definitions.md ((a) less (c) at beta=1, cap of 2: +0.00 +/- 0.06;
% volume removed 31.8% and 33.1%).
Default rises with access only where peer influence makes the same households borrow more
often. Under the threshold rule holding rises 30 to 50 points with no detectable change in
default.
% thr_adoption_rise_min/_max (0.295, 0.503) and thr_default_n_detectable (0), results_numbers.json.

The size of the effect depends most on purchase size and on how much households borrow
against a shortfall, neither of which is measured for South Africa. No arm gives platforms a
shared view of exposure; the nearest comparison, mandatory screening, is in
Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}.
