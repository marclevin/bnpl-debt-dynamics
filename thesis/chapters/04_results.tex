\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section answers RQ2. Every arm uses the parameters fitted in
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}; no \ac{BNPL} parameter is re-tuned. The conventions for
replicates, standard errors and detectability are those of Section~\ref{sec:sim-params}.
Because $\beta$ has no empirical estimate, we report mainly $\beta=0$, the control without peer
influence, and $\beta=1$, an illustrative value at which most households that use \ac{BNPL}
buy with it repeatedly.

\subsection{Baseline and the BNPL Injection}
\label{sec:results-injection}

Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms} compares the baseline with \ac{BNPL} enabled. On these seeds the
baseline's 90+ share matches the \ac{CCMR}, as it does on the calibration seeds; the bands the
model misses are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}.

Without peer influence, enabling \ac{BNPL} raises default slightly. Four seed blocks estimate
the effect, and only one of the four estimates exceeds two standard errors on its own. Pooled
by precision, the effect is $0.12 \pm 0.05$ points, small enough that a single
twenty-replicate comparison would usually miss it. With $\beta=1$ it is $0.60 \pm 0.07$
points, pooled over three blocks. Both are small beside a baseline default rate of 13.3\%.

The larger effect comes with much more borrowing by about as many borrowers. Peer influence
raises the share of households that ever hold \ac{BNPL} only from 70\% to 76\%, but volume
rises tenfold, because those who use \ac{BNPL} buy with it again and again: a household that
ever holds a balance makes 34 want-driven purchases over the two years at $\beta=1$, against
2.3 at $\beta=0$. The share of households with no liquid savings rises by $3.05 \pm 0.06$
points at $\beta=1$ and by $0.15 \pm 0.06$ at $\beta=0$. These are population aggregates; they
do not show that the households that default are the ones whose savings were depleted.
Neither setting matches the market: volume is below the provider's range at $\beta=0$ and well
above it at $\beta=1$ (Table~\ref{tab:bnpl-on-checks}).

At $\beta=0$ \ac{BNPL} partly replaces traditional credit. An eligible shortfall goes to
\ac{BNPL} first (Submodel~5), so the mean traditional loan falls by R$134 \pm 15$ and new
traditional lending by about a ninth, while refusals do not rise. At $\beta=1$ the mean loan
falls by a similar amount but lending does not fall, because households apply for traditional
credit more often, $801 \pm 65$ more applications against $138 \pm 57$ at $\beta=0$. This is
consistent with want-driven purchases depleting savings, so that households run short more
often. The smaller traditional book at $\beta=0$ is why traditional interest falls in
Table~\ref{tab:bnpl-on-checks}.

Used only to cover shortfalls, \ac{BNPL} does not change default detectably
($+0.05 \pm 0.09$ points with the want-driven path switched off; Table~\ref{tab:effect}). The
effect comes mainly from want-driven borrowing.

\subsection{Stacking Across Platforms}
\label{sec:results-stacking}

Owing several platforms at once is common whenever households borrow more than occasionally
(Table~\ref{tab:stacking}; Figure~\ref{fig:stacking}). With four platforms and no peer
influence, about half of the households holding \ac{BNPL} at the final tick owe two or more,
and 37\% of ever-holders do so at some point, close to the \ac{CFPB}'s 32 to 33\%. At $\beta=1$
almost every ever-holder does.

That near-universal share follows from the model's rules. At
$\beta=1$ an ever-holder makes about 34 purchases in 52 ticks, and each agreement stays open
for three ticks after checkout, so its agreements nearly always overlap. Under random routing,
a household that owes one of four platforms sends its next request first to a different one
with probability $3/4$. A household that borrows this often is therefore almost
certain to owe two platforms at some tick, and the ``ever'' measure counts a single such tick.
Under loyal routing, in which a household tries platforms it already owes first, the share
of ever-holders who stack falls to 51\% at $\beta=1$ and the share of final-tick holders who stack
falls from 85\% to 22\%, on the same seeds and with the same volume. How often households stack
is therefore set by the routing assumption and by how often they borrow, and the model has no
South African evidence on either.

Default does not change detectably with the routing rule ($-0.07 \pm 0.07$ points at
$\beta=0$ and $+0.02 \pm 0.13$ at $\beta=1$) or with the number of platforms (six platforms
against one: $+0.02 \pm 0.09$ and $+0.01 \pm 0.08$). Differences of about 0.2 to 0.25 points
cannot be excluded. Volume rises when a second platform relieves limits that bind with one,
and changes little after that, because purchases are sized on budgets. Each platform brings
its own limit, so platform count and total credit capacity move together, and no arm gives
platforms a shared view of exposure. The sweep therefore cannot isolate the effect of
platforms not seeing one another. Within the model, the routing rule and the number of
platforms change how debt is spread across lenders without changing default detectably,
although a cap on platforms owed lowers it at $\beta=1$ (Section~\ref{sec:results-answer}).

\subsection{BNPL Access, Peer Influence and Default}
\label{sec:results-rq2}

Table~\ref{tab:access} varies the share of banked households allowed to use \ac{BNPL}.
Without peer influence, moving from zero to full access raises holding by 18 points and does
not change default detectably. With peer influence, holding rises much more and default rises
with access, by between 0.27 and 0.95 points at full access as $\beta$ goes from 0.5 to 3.

\label{sec:no-cascade}
For $\beta \geq 1$ the rise is close to proportional to access: straight lines through the
seven arm means have $R^2$ of 0.91 to 0.94 (Figure~\ref{fig:access-default}). Nothing in these
data suggests a tipping point. The design could still miss one, because the seven access levels
are widely spaced and replicate noise is large beside the smaller rises, such as those at
$\beta=0.5$ and under the threshold rule. No channel in the model carries one household's
default to another's income or to a lender's supply of credit
(Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}), so contagion through those channels lies outside the test.

The threshold rule tests whether default depends on how adoption spreads. With $\gamma=0.3$
it raises holding at full access by 41 to 51 points, against 18 in its control, yet the rise in
default exceeds the control's by only 0.05 to 0.14 points, none detectable. Raising the mean
threshold from 0.15 to 0.45 lowers full-access holding from 51\% to 28\% without changing
default detectably ($-0.17 \pm 0.11$ points). Holding is close to linear in access under this
rule too, so no adoption cascade occurs on this grid. Under the threshold rule, volume at full
access is 2.6 to 4.3 times its control; under the linear rule at $\beta=1$ it is ten times.
Wider use of \ac{BNPL} therefore raises default little unless households also borrow much more.

\subsection{Distribution Across Income Quintiles}
\label{sec:results-distribution}

Baseline default is highest in Q1 and Q4 and lowest in Q5, and the same two quintiles have the
highest debt-to-income ratios (Table~\ref{tab:distribution}). Without peer influence, enabling
\ac{BNPL} changes default detectably in no quintile. At $\beta=1$ the effect is detectable in
Q2, Q3 and Q4 and largest in Q4. In Q1 it is $+0.03 \pm 0.22$ points, which cannot exclude
0.5 points, and in Q5 it falls short of two standard errors. The Q4
effect exceeds the Q1 effect by $1.14 \pm 0.38$ points.

The effect is therefore larger in the middle of the distribution than at the bottom. Because
purchases are sized on discretionary budgets, the mean purchase rises from R103 in Q1 to
R1{,}858 in Q5, and small purchases mean small instalments. That may explain the smaller
effect in Q1, but the experiment does not test it, and Q5, with the largest
purchases, shows no detectable rise. The model does not establish why the effect differs
between quintiles.

Platform limits bind more often on poorer households: in Q1 a limit reduces or refuses a
quarter of platform requests, against about one in seven in Q5. Raising the limit tenfold
changes default by $+0.10 \pm 0.09$ points (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}), so the limit
is not what keeps the effect small in Q1.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

Simulation noise is small beside the assumptions. Default varies by about 0.3 points between
replicates, results do not change detectably with population size or activation order
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}), and with twenty replicates a difference of 0.2 points is
detected about half the time (Section~\ref{sec:sim-params}). Where
an assumption moves a result by more than that, the model is sensitive to the assumption. The
model is not in a steady state: default accumulates and the 90+ share keeps rising because
nothing is written off, so every result belongs to the two-year horizon.

The level of default depends mainly on the income shock (Figure~\ref{fig:tornado};
Tables~\ref{tab:robustness} and~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}). Replacing the persistent shock
with a single-tick loss lowers default from 14.05\% to 4.81\%, and under that rule no shock
probability on the calibration grid reproduces the 90+ target. Of the
behavioural rules, borrowing more than the shortfall moves the level most: adding one tick of
committed spending lowers default by $1.02 \pm 0.13$ points, perhaps because the surplus is
saved and absorbs later shortfalls, which the experiment does not isolate. Shortening the
default threshold to 56 days raises default by $0.84 \pm 0.11$ points.

\subsubsection*{What Moves the BNPL Effect}
\label{sec:results-effect}

Table~\ref{tab:effect} runs every setting with \ac{BNPL} off and on, on one shared seed block.
At $\beta=1$ the effect is positive and detectable under nineteen of the twenty settings, so its
sign does not depend on any one assumption. Its size does, and purchase size moves it most.
Halving $\kappa$ cuts the effect to $0.25 \pm 0.09$ points; doubling it raises the effect to
$0.91 \pm 0.07$, and sizing purchases on income raises it to $1.22 \pm 0.10$. A larger
purchase means larger fortnightly instalments, which leave less cash for food, rent and
traditional debt, so the direction is what one would expect. The rule for how much a household
borrows against a shortfall is the only other assumption with a comparable effect, moving it
from 0.37 to 1.27 points. The remaining settings, including loyal routing, removing \ac{BNPL}
from the shortfall path and the minimum-payment rules, keep the effect between 0.47 and 0.76
points.

Purchase size is therefore a result in its own right, and it is poorly measured. The reference
$\kappa$ gives a mean purchase of R611 at $\beta=1$, 38\% below the provider-based figure of
R992. Two settings, $\kappa=0.28$ and income-based sizing, give mean purchases within that
figure's tolerance. At $\beta=1$ their effects are 1.5 to 2 times the reference, but their
volume, R18{,}000 to R19{,}000 per eligible household a year, is more than four times the top of the
provider's range. At $\beta=0$ their volume lies within that range and their effects,
$-0.02 \pm 0.09$ and $+0.09 \pm 0.07$ points, are not detectable. The settings closest to the
provider data on both measures therefore agree with the small effect without peer influence
(Section~\ref{sec:results-injection}).

Without peer influence purchase size does not change the effect detectably: when households
borrow rarely, larger purchases have little to act on. At $\beta=0$ the effect is detectable
under five of the twenty settings and never detectably negative. Two of the five, $\lambda=0.25$
and $\lambda=1.0$, are one result, because the limit seldom binds at either value. The
settings share a seed block and often a no-\ac{BNPL} arm, so these counts are not independent
replications. Under the single-tick shock the effect disappears at both values of $\beta$, so
in the model the effect of \ac{BNPL} depends on income losses that persist.

\subsection{Answer to RQ2}
\label{sec:results-answer}

With four platforms that cannot see one another, owing several at once is common. Without
peer influence about half of the households holding \ac{BNPL} at the end of the run owe two
or more platforms, and when households borrow repeatedly almost every user stacks at some
point. How often depends mostly on the assumed rule for choosing platforms and on how often
households borrow.

Enabling \ac{BNPL} raises default by $0.12 \pm 0.05$ points without peer influence and by
$0.60 \pm 0.07$ points at $\beta=1$. Default does not change detectably with the routing rule
or the number of platforms. It rises with access only where peer influence makes households
borrow repeatedly, and under the threshold rule a much larger share of households holding
\ac{BNPL} raises default little. Across these experiments default follows how often households
borrow more than the number of households using \ac{BNPL}. Whether the number of platforms a
household owes matters is unsettled: a cap on platforms owed lowers default at $\beta=1$, and
a cap on agreements that removes as much volume does not detectably
(Appendix~\ref{app:levers}). At $\beta=1$ the rise is detectable in Q2 to Q4 and smaller in
Q1 than in Q4.

The size of the effect depends most on purchase size and on how much households borrow
against a shortfall, neither of which is measured for South Africa. At purchase sizes
consistent with the provider figure, the effect is 0.9 to 1.2 points at $\beta=1$, where
volume is more than four times the provider's range, and not detectable at $\beta=0$, where
volume lies within it. The
experiments do not isolate the effect of platforms not seeing one another, because no arm
gives them a shared view of exposure; mandatory screening, whose test sees every platform's
obligations, is examined in Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}.
