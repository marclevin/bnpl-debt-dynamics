\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section answers RQ2: whether cross-platform stacking emerges when platforms cannot
observe one another, and how platform count, access and peer adoption affect default. RQ3
follows in Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}. Every arm has twenty replications with unique seeds,
at the income-shock probability and payment friction fitted in Section~\ref{sec:calibration};
no \ac{BNPL}-side parameter is re-tuned. Differences in the text carry one standard error, and
a difference is described as detectable only when it exceeds two. Figure error bars are 95\%
confidence intervals of the arm mean. Table notes give denominators, seeds and measurement
periods.

Peer influence is reported at $\beta=0$, the control, and at $\beta=1$. Because $\beta$ has no
empirical estimate, $\beta=1$ is an illustrative setting, not a calibrated one.

\subsection{Baseline and the BNPL Injection}
\label{sec:results-injection}

With \ac{BNPL} disabled, 13.40\% of households are in default at the final tick, and 13.68\%
of credit-active households are 90 or more days in arrears, against 14.21\% in the \ac{CCMR}
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-arrears}). The calibration run of
Table~\ref{tab:arrears-fit} gives 13.96\%; the two differ by less than one replicate standard
deviation. Enabling \ac{BNPL} at four
platforms, with access at the banked ceiling, raises default by $1.22 \pm 0.11$ percentage
points at $\beta=0$ and by $2.07 \pm 0.10$ points at $\beta=1$. The same comparison at
$\beta=0$ recurs on three other seed sets: $+1.28 \pm 0.09$ in the effect-robustness suite
(Table~\ref{tab:effect}), $+1.31$ in the threshold control and $+1.41 \pm 0.10$ at the
full-access end of the access sweep. The four estimates are consistent with one another.

The $\beta=1$ arm carries a caveat. Its stacking far exceeds the only external benchmark:
98.6\% of households that ever used \ac{BNPL} held balances on two or more platforms at some
point, against 32\% of United States borrowers over a year \cite{cfpb2025bnpl}. The $\beta=0$
arm, at 37.0\%, is of the same order as the benchmark. The $\beta=1$ results therefore describe
a market with far more concurrent borrowing than the available evidence records, and the
$\beta=0$ arm is the better guide to magnitudes.

\ac{BNPL} partly replaces traditional credit. At $\beta=0$, new traditional lending falls from
R6.57 million to R3.48 million, and refusals at the Regulation 23A gate fall from 1{,}228 to
1{,}033. This substitution follows from the lender-choice rule, which routes an eligible
shortfall to \ac{BNPL} first (Submodel~5). Traditional arrears nonetheless rise, by
$0.64 \pm 0.07$ points at $\beta=0$ and $1.14 \pm 0.06$ at $\beta=1$, while the interest charged
on traditional debt falls by 2.1\% and 1.7\% (Table~\ref{tab:bnpl-on-checks}), which fails
Pattern~1 on that measure. One explanation, offered after the fact, is that interest is charged
on outstanding balances and so falls with the smaller traditional book; this aggregate
measure is not directly comparable with the higher credit-card interest that deHaan et al.\
observe among individual adopters \cite{dehaan2024bnpl}.

\subsection{Invisible Credit and Stacking}
\label{sec:results-stacking}

Cross-platform stacking emerges although no rule assigns households to several providers
(Table~\ref{tab:stacking}; Figure~\ref{fig:stacking}). At $\beta=0$, the share of all households
holding balances on two or more platforms at the final tick rises from 6.4\% with two platforms
to 8.0\% with six; at $\beta=1$ it rises from 43.7\% to 57.0\%. With four platforms, 45.0\% of
final-tick holders have balances on two or more platforms at $\beta=0$, and 83.0\% at $\beta=1$
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}).

Cumulative \ac{BNPL} volume rises when a second platform is added, from R4.82 million to
R5.38 million at $\beta=0$, and is flat from two platforms to six. This is consistent with a
second platform relieving limits that bind with one, and with further platforms adding capacity
that budget-based demand does not use.

Default does not change detectably with platform count. Six platforms less one gives
$-0.04 \pm 0.09$ points at $\beta=0$ and $+0.18 \pm 0.10$ at $\beta=1$. Because volume is flat beyond two platforms, the sweep cannot show what additional
providers would do in a market where they expand borrowing. And because each platform brings
its own limit, provider count and total credit capacity move together, so the sweep does not
isolate invisibility itself. The hypothetical cap on concurrent agreements
(Appendix~\ref{app:levers}) instead separates concurrency from volume. Limiting a household to two open agreements on
any platform lowers default by $1.01 \pm 0.11$ points at $\beta=0$ while leaving volume within
0.4\% of the benchmark (Table~\ref{tab:cap}). Concurrency, not provider count, carries the
default response.

\subsection{BNPL Access, Peer Influence and Default}
\label{sec:results-rq2}

Default rises approximately linearly with access (Figure~\ref{fig:access-default};
Table~\ref{tab:access}). Under the linear peer rule, moving from zero to full access raises
default by $1.41 \pm 0.10$ points at $\beta=0$ and $2.35 \pm 0.10$ at $\beta=3$. Straight-line
fits across the seven access levels have $R^2 \geq 0.982$, and no residual exceeds 10\% of the
total rise. The response grows with $\beta$ but saturates: $\beta=2$ and $\beta=3$ give rises
of 2.32 and 2.35 points.

The heterogeneous-threshold rule raises holding far more than it raises default. At
$\gamma=0.3$, the full-access share of households holding \ac{BNPL} rises by up to 49.8 points,
against 16.7 in the $\gamma=0$ control, while the default rise is 1.32 to 1.70 points against
1.31. Raising the mean threshold from 0.15 to 0.45 halves full-access holding, from 50.0\% to
26.2\%, and moves default from 14.94\% to 14.76\%, which is not a detectable change.

Neither rule produces a tipping point on the tested grid. Under the threshold rule, holding is
itself linear in access ($R^2 \geq 0.997$), so no adoption cascade occurs, and default is linear
in access under both rules. The weakest fit, at $\sigma_\theta=0.05$, has $R^2=0.955$ and a
largest residual of 12.2\% of the rise.

\label{sec:no-cascade}
The grid has seven access levels, so a threshold narrower than
their spacing could go undetected. And no channel in the model carries one household's default
to another household's income or to a lender's supply of credit
(Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}), so contagion through those channels lies outside the test.

The default effect depends only weakly on how many households use \ac{BNPL}. Nearly quadrupling
final-tick holding, from 16.8\% at $\beta=0$ to 65.7\% at $\beta=1$, raises default by less than
one point (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}). With the
want-driven path switched off ($q_{\text{base}} = 0$, $\beta = 0$), so that households use
\ac{BNPL} only to cover shortfalls, enabling \ac{BNPL} still raises default by
$1.16 \pm 0.11$ points, against $1.28 \pm 0.09$ with both paths active on the same seeds
(Table~\ref{tab:effect}). Most of the effect at $\beta=0$ therefore arises from \ac{BNPL} used
to cover shortfalls: instalments due within six weeks that replace traditional credit repaid
over 25 months. This channel rests on the shortfall path of Submodels~4 and~5, which has no
empirical anchor (Section~\ref{sec:amount}).

\subsection{Distribution Across Income Quintiles}
\label{sec:results-distribution}

Baseline default is highest in Q1 (17.93\%) and Q4 (15.86\%) and lowest in Q5 (10.22\%)
(Table~\ref{tab:distribution}). The same two quintiles have the highest aggregate
debt-to-income ratios: 1.49 months of income in Q4 and 1.32 in Q1.

\ac{BNPL} raises default in every quintile, by between $0.87 \pm 0.22$ points in Q2 and
$1.79 \pm 0.29$ in Q3. The increases cannot be ranked with confidence, since only the gap
between Q3 and Q2 exceeds two standard errors. The share holding \ac{BNPL} at the final tick
varies little across quintiles, from 14.7\% to 19.2\%, while the mean want-driven purchase rises
from R102 in Q1 to R1{,}888 in Q5, as the budget-based sizing rule implies.

Per-platform limits refuse 25.7\% of Q1's requests and 7.3\% of Q5's. Yet raising $\lambda$
tenfold, from 0.10 to 1.0 months of income, leaves population default unchanged within noise
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}, at $\beta=1$). Binding limits constrain low-income
households' borrowing without a detectable effect on population default.

\subsection{Sensitivity and Robustness}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

Figure~\ref{fig:tornado} and Tables~\ref{tab:robustness} and~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}
report the level of default in one-at-a-time sensitivity arms at $\beta=1$, four platforms and
full access. Each arm is compared with a reference arm at the default value on the same seed
set. These arms use $\beta=1$; Table~\ref{tab:effect} repeats the principal settings at
$\beta=0$, the better guide to magnitudes (Section~\ref{sec:results-injection}).

The largest influence on the default level is the rule for how much a household borrows when
short of cash (Submodel~4). Relative to borrowing the exact shortfall, adding 25\% lowers
default by $0.90 \pm 0.12$ points, and adding one tick of committed expenditure lowers it by
$2.15 \pm 0.09$. No study located reports borrowing amounts relative to a realised shortfall.
The three rules span the range tested; no evidence locates the true rule within it, and this
is the quantity on which new evidence would most improve the model.

The direction of this sensitivity follows from the model's cash-flow accounting. Borrowing beyond
the immediate shortfall leaves a surplus in savings, which absorbs later shortfalls within the
horizon. Because distress in the model is a failure of liquidity, a larger buffer delays or
prevents it. Removing the cap on \ac{BNPL}'s share of a shortfall raises default by
$0.64 \pm 0.13$ and $0.92 \pm 0.10$ points under the two larger rules, paired by seed, and by
$0.09 \pm 0.11$ under the exact-shortfall rule, which is not detectable. This is consistent with
the same mechanism: an uncapped buffer is financed more by instalments due within six weeks and
less by traditional credit repaid over 25 months.

Three other assumptions move the default level. Shortening the default horizon from 98 to 56
days raises default by $1.34 \pm 0.12$ points. Varying $\kappa$ from 0.07 to 0.28, about half to
twice its derived value, moves default from 15.00\% to 16.22\%. Sizing purchases on income
rather than discretionary spending adds $0.54 \pm 0.12$ points. The remaining arms change
default by at most 0.4 points, and the only one beyond two standard errors is the lower
minimum-payment fraction of 0.025, at $-0.32 \pm 0.10$ (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}).
Activation order and population size, which test the simulation rather than the model's
assumptions, do not detectably change default.

Shock persistence matters more than any of these. Replacing the persistent income shock with a
single-tick loss lowers default from 15.45\% to 5.66\%, and under that rule no shock
probability in the tested range reproduces the \ac{CCMR} 90+ band. Sustained distress in the
model depends on shocks that outlast the household's buffer.

A Sobol decomposition over six parameters (Table~\ref{tab:sobol}) attributes the variance of
the default level almost entirely to the income-shock probability, with a first-order index of
$1.02 \pm 0.15$; the excess over one is estimation noise. This reflects its range, 0.005--0.026,
which runs from below the \ac{QLFS} separation band to more than twice its upper end. Each \ac{BNPL}-side parameter has a total-order index of
0.02 or less.

\subsubsection*{Robustness of the BNPL Effect}
\label{sec:results-effect}

The arms above measure the level of default with \ac{BNPL} enabled. Table~\ref{tab:effect}
tests the \ac{BNPL} effect itself. Every setting is run with \ac{BNPL} off and on, at
$\beta=0$ and $\beta=1$, over one shared seed block, so each effect is a paired difference on
the same seeds.

The effect is positive under every setting at both values of $\beta$. At $\beta=0$ it ranges
from $0.31 \pm 0.07$ to $1.46 \pm 0.06$ points, against $1.28 \pm 0.09$ at default values. At
$\beta=1$ it ranges from $0.76 \pm 0.01$ to $2.91 \pm 0.09$ points, against $2.17 \pm 0.10$.

The borrowing-amount rule moves the effect most. Borrowing one tick of committed expenditure
beyond the shortfall reduces the effect at $\beta=0$ to $0.31 \pm 0.07$ points, but only while
\ac{BNPL}'s share of a shortfall is capped. With the cap removed, the effect is
$1.44 \pm 0.08$ points. The pattern is consistent with the financing mechanism described
above: a buffer financed mainly by traditional credit absorbs later \ac{BNPL} instalments,
whereas a buffer financed by \ac{BNPL} adds instalments of its own.

Purchase size and its sizing base leave the effect at $\beta=0$ between 1.28 and 1.34 points,
as expected when want-driven purchases contribute little to it. At $\beta=1$ they move it from
$1.55 \pm 0.08$ points at $\kappa=0.07$ to $2.91 \pm 0.09$ when purchases are sized on income.
The rolling limit, the minimum-payment settings and the default horizon change the effect by
at most 0.17 points at either value of $\beta$. Under the single-tick shock the effect falls to
$0.68 \pm 0.01$ points, while default without \ac{BNPL} falls from 13.39\% to 4.90\%, so the
effect is larger relative to its baseline.

The effect is positive in every setting tested; its size depends most on how much households
borrow beyond a shortfall and from which lender, quantities for which no estimate exists.

\subsection{Answer to RQ2}
\label{sec:results-answer}

Stacking emerges when platforms cannot observe one another, and it deepens with platform count
and peer influence. Default does not rise with the number of platforms. It rises linearly with
access, more steeply with peer influence, and without a tipping point under either adoption
rule. In this model, default responds to the number of obligations a household services at
once, not to the number of providers, and only weakly to the number of users. Most of the
\ac{BNPL} effect arises from
\ac{BNPL} used to cover shortfalls. The effect is positive in every setting of Table~\ref{tab:effect}. Its
size depends most on a borrowing-amount rule that no evidence fixes.
