\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section answers RQ2: how often households owe several platforms at once when
platforms cannot observe one another, and how enabling \ac{BNPL}, platform count, access
and peer adoption affect default. Every arm has twenty replications at the income-shock
probability and payment friction fitted in Section~\ref{sec:calibration}, and no
\ac{BNPL}-side parameter is re-tuned. Differences in the text carry one standard error,
paired by seed where the two arms share seeds, and we call a difference detectable only
when it exceeds two standard errors. A typical standard error is about 0.1 percentage
points, so a difference must exceed about 0.2 points to count as detectable, and a true
change of that size would be missed about half the time. Figure error bars are 95\%
confidence intervals of the arm mean.

Most comparisons are reported at $\beta=0$, the control without peer influence, and at
$\beta=1$. Because $\beta$ has no empirical estimate, $\beta=1$ is an illustrative
setting.

\subsection{Baseline and the BNPL Injection}
\label{sec:results-injection}

With \ac{BNPL} disabled, 13.31\% of households have defaulted by the final tick, and 14.20\%
of credit-active households are 90 or more days in arrears, against 14.21\% in the \ac{CCMR}
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-arrears}). The calibration run of
Table~\ref{tab:arrears-fit} gives 14.48\% on other seeds; the two differ by less than one
replicate standard deviation.

Without peer influence, enabling \ac{BNPL} has a small effect on default. At four
platforms and access at the banked ceiling the change is $+0.03 \pm 0.09$ points
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}). The same comparison on three other seed blocks gives
$+0.15 \pm 0.07$ (Table~\ref{tab:effect}), $+0.14 \pm 0.11$ and $+0.17 \pm 0.10$
(Table~\ref{tab:access}), and only the estimate in Table~\ref{tab:effect} exceeds two
standard errors. Because the blocks use different seeds, we pool the four estimates,
weighting each by its precision, and obtain $+0.12 \pm 0.05$ points. The pooled estimate
exceeds two standard errors, but at about a tenth of a point the effect is smaller than any
single comparison in this thesis can detect.

With peer influence the effect is larger. At $\beta=1$ default rises by $0.61 \pm 0.11$
points, and the two other seed blocks that include this comparison give $0.60 \pm 0.11$ and
$0.60 \pm 0.12$.

The $\beta=1$ arm stacks far more than the only external evidence records. At $\beta=1$,
98.6\% of households that ever held a \ac{BNPL} balance held balances on two or more
platforms at some point, against 36.8\% at $\beta=0$ and the \ac{CFPB}'s 32\% and 33\%
(Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}). We therefore treat the $\beta=0$ arm as the better
guide to magnitudes, and the $\beta=1$ arm as an illustration of a market with heavy repeat
borrowing.

Peer influence changes how much households borrow far more than how many ever borrow. The
share of households that ever hold a \ac{BNPL} balance is 70.0\% at $\beta=0$ and 75.5\%
at $\beta=1$, while cumulative volume is R6.42 million and R65.88 million. The share of
households with no liquid savings rises by $0.15 \pm 0.06$ points at $\beta=0$ and by
$3.05 \pm 0.06$ at $\beta=1$, so default and depleted savings rise together where
borrowing is heavy. These are population aggregates, and they do not show that the
households that default are the ones whose savings were depleted.

At $\beta=0$, \ac{BNPL} partly replaces traditional credit. New traditional lending falls
from R6.12 million to R5.43 million, consistent with the lender-choice rule sending an
eligible shortfall to \ac{BNPL} first (Submodel~5). At $\beta=1$ new traditional lending
does not fall. The number of applications the traditional lender refuses barely changes:
the change at $\beta=0$ is $+1 \pm 9$ on these seeds and $-16 \pm 7$, about 1\% of all
refusals, on the seeds of Table~\ref{tab:effect}. The smaller traditional book explains the
Pattern~1 result of Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}. Traditional arrears rise, but the
interest charged on traditional debt falls by $1.5 \pm 0.4$\% at $\beta=0$
(Table~\ref{tab:bnpl-on-checks}), because interest is charged on a smaller outstanding
balance. This aggregate measure is not directly comparable with the higher credit-card
interest that deHaan et al.\ observe among individual adopters \cite{dehaan2024bnpl}.

Used only to cover shortfalls, \ac{BNPL} does not change default detectably. With the
want-driven path switched off ($q_{\text{base}} = 0$, $\beta = 0$), enabling \ac{BNPL}
changes default by $+0.05 \pm 0.09$ points (Table~\ref{tab:effect}). A household that
finances part of a shortfall with \ac{BNPL} asks the traditional lender only for the rest,
and in this arm the lender refuses $17 \pm 8$ fewer applications than without \ac{BNPL}
and lends R0.83 million less.

\subsection{Invisible Credit and Stacking}
\label{sec:results-stacking}

When platforms cannot see one another, households come to owe several platforms at once
(Table~\ref{tab:stacking}; Figure~\ref{fig:stacking}). At $\beta=0$, the share of all households holding balances on two
or more platforms at the final tick rises from 8.5\% with two platforms to 9.4\% with six; at
$\beta=1$ it rises from 46.8\% to 59.2\%. With four platforms, 52.0\% of final-tick holders
have balances on two or more platforms at $\beta=0$, and 85.1\% at $\beta=1$
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}). These shares follow from the routing rule of
Section~\ref{sec:stacking}, under which a second request reaches a different platform first
with probability $3/4$ when four platforms are available and $5/6$ when six are. They
therefore measure how often agreements overlap.

Cumulative \ac{BNPL} volume rises when a second platform is added, from R5.14 million to
R6.16 million at $\beta=0$, and changes little from two platforms to six. This is consistent
with a second platform relieving limits that bind with one, and with further platforms adding
capacity that budget-based demand does not use.

Default does not change detectably with platform count at any value of $\beta$. Six platforms
less one gives $+0.02 \pm 0.09$ points at $\beta=0$ and $+0.01 \pm 0.08$ at $\beta=1$, and
between $-0.09 \pm 0.07$ and $+0.14 \pm 0.13$ at the other three values
(Table~\ref{tab:stacking-full}). No contrast between one platform and a larger number exceeds
two standard errors.

Two features of the design limit what the sweep shows. Volume changes little beyond two
platforms, so the sweep cannot show what additional platforms would do in a market where
they expand borrowing. Each platform also brings its own limit, so platform count and total
credit capacity move together, and the sweep cannot separate the effect of platforms'
invisibility to one another from the effect of extra capacity.

\subsection{BNPL Access, Peer Influence and Default}
\label{sec:results-rq2}

The access sweep asks whether default rises smoothly as more of the banked population can
use \ac{BNPL}, or whether it tips once adoption passes some level. Default rises with access
where peer influence is present (Figure~\ref{fig:access-default}; Table~\ref{tab:access}).
At $\beta=0$, moving from zero to full access changes default by $+0.14 \pm 0.11$ points,
which is not detectable. The change is $0.27 \pm 0.11$ points at $\beta=0.5$,
$0.60 \pm 0.11$ at $\beta=1$, $0.78 \pm 0.09$ at $\beta=2$ and $0.95 \pm 0.09$ at
$\beta=3$.

The grid is too coarse to establish the shape of this response. Steps between adjacent
access levels range from $-0.09$ to $+0.33$ points, with standard errors of 0.07 to 0.14,
and six of the thirty steps exceed two standard errors. The largest is $0.33 \pm 0.09$
points, between access of 0.85 and full access at $\beta=3$. Straight-line fits to the arm
means have $R^2$ between 0.68 and 0.94, so the grid cannot distinguish a straight line from
a curve.

The heterogeneous-threshold rule raises holding far more than the control does, but it
does not raise default detectably more. At $\gamma=0.3$ the full-access share of households
holding \ac{BNPL} rises by between 40.7 and 51.0 points, against 17.6 in the $\gamma=0$
control. Holding is close to linear in access ($R^2 \geq 0.998$), so no adoption cascade
occurs on this grid. Default rises by between $0.22 \pm 0.09$ and $0.30 \pm 0.10$ points,
against $0.17 \pm 0.10$ in the control, and none of the differences between the two, which
range from $0.05 \pm 0.08$ to $0.14 \pm 0.13$ points, is detectable. Raising the mean
threshold from 0.15 to 0.45 lowers full-access holding from 50.8\% to 28.2\% and changes
default by $-0.17 \pm 0.11$ points, which is also not detectable.

\label{sec:no-cascade}
Two limits apply to this search for a tipping point. The grid has seven access levels, so a
tipping point narrower than their spacing could go undetected. And no channel in the model
carries one household's default to another household's income or to a lender's supply of
credit (Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}), so contagion through those channels lies outside
the test.

\subsection{Distribution Across Income Quintiles}
\label{sec:results-distribution}

Baseline default is highest in Q1 (17.83\%) and Q4 (15.75\%) and lowest in Q5 (10.10\%)
(Table~\ref{tab:distribution}), the same two quintiles that have the highest aggregate
debt-to-income ratios (Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}).

At $\beta=0$, enabling \ac{BNPL} changes default detectably in no quintile. At $\beta=1$ default
rises by $0.56 \pm 0.25$ points in Q2, $0.83 \pm 0.27$ in Q3 and $1.17 \pm 0.32$ in Q4, while
the changes in Q1 ($+0.03 \pm 0.22$) and Q5 ($+0.49 \pm 0.27$) are not detectable. The rise in
Q4 exceeds the rise in Q1 by $1.14 \pm 0.38$ points. In the $\beta=0$ arm the share holding
\ac{BNPL} at the final tick varies little across quintiles, from 15.6\% to 19.4\%, while the
mean want-driven purchase rises from R103 in Q1 to R1{,}858 in Q5, as the budget-based sizing
rule implies. Larger purchases may explain why default rises more in Q4 than in Q1, but the
experiment does not test this, and Q5, where purchases are largest, shows no detectable
rise.

The per-platform limit binds on 24.8\% of the platform requests that Q1 households make and on
13.6\% of those that Q5 households make, truncating or refusing them. Yet raising
$\lambda$ tenfold, from 0.10 to 1.0 months of income, changes population default by
$+0.10 \pm 0.09$ points at $\beta=1$ (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}). The limits
constrain low-income households' borrowing without a detectable effect on population
default.

\subsection{Sensitivity and Robustness}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

Figure~\ref{fig:tornado} and Tables~\ref{tab:robustness} and~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}
report the level of default in one-at-a-time sensitivity arms at $\beta=1$, four platforms
and full access. Each arm is compared with a reference arm on the same seeds.

The largest influence on the default level is the rule for how much a household borrows when
short of cash (Submodel~4). Relative to borrowing the exact shortfall, adding 25\% lowers
default by $0.28 \pm 0.11$ points, and adding one tick of committed expenditure lowers it by
$1.02 \pm 0.13$. A plausible reason is that borrowing beyond the immediate shortfall
leaves a surplus in savings that absorbs later shortfalls, but the experiment does not
isolate this channel. No evidence places the true rule within the range tested
(Section~\ref{sec:amount}). Removing the cap on \ac{BNPL}'s share of a shortfall does not
change default detectably under any of the three rules.

Three other assumptions move the default level. Shortening the default horizon from 98 to 56
days raises default by $0.84 \pm 0.11$ points. Varying $\kappa$ from 0.07 to 0.28, about half
to twice its derived value, moves default from 13.69\% to 14.34\%. Sizing purchases on income
in place of discretionary spending adds $0.68 \pm 0.11$ points. The remaining arms change
default by at most 0.35 points and none exceeds two standard errors
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}). Activation order and population size test the
simulation and do not detectably change default.

Shock persistence matters more than any of these. Replacing the persistent income shock
with a single-tick loss lowers default from 14.05\% to 4.81\%, and under that rule no shock
probability in the tested range reproduces the \ac{CCMR} 90+ band. Sustained distress in
the model depends on shocks that outlast the household's cash buffer.

A Sobol decomposition over six parameters (Table~\ref{tab:sobol}) attributes the variance
of the default level almost entirely to the income-shock probability, with a first-order
index of 0.99 and a 95\% bootstrap interval of $\pm 0.15$. Part of this dominance reflects
the range sampled, 0.005--0.026, which runs from below the \ac{QLFS} separation band to
more than twice its upper end. Each \ac{BNPL}-side parameter has a total-order index of
about 0.01.

\subsubsection*{Robustness of the BNPL Effect}
\label{sec:results-effect}

The arms above measure the level of default with \ac{BNPL} enabled.
Table~\ref{tab:effect} tests whether the \ac{BNPL} effect itself survives the same
assumptions. Every setting is run with \ac{BNPL} off and on, at $\beta=0$ and $\beta=1$,
over one shared seed block, so each effect is a paired difference on the same seeds.

At $\beta=1$ the effect is positive and detectable under sixteen of the seventeen settings.
Among those sixteen it ranges from $0.25 \pm 0.09$ points
at $\kappa=0.07$ to $1.27 \pm 0.11$ where households borrow one tick of committed expenditure
beyond the shortfall and the shortfall cap is removed. Purchase size and the borrowing-amount
rule move it most. The effect is $0.91 \pm 0.07$ points at $\kappa=0.28$ and
$1.22 \pm 0.10$ when purchases are sized on income. Under the single-tick shock the effect is
$0.02 \pm 0.01$ points, with default without \ac{BNPL} at 4.82\%.

At $\beta=0$ the effect ranges from $-0.11 \pm 0.08$ to $+0.52 \pm 0.09$ points. It exceeds two
standard errors under five of the seventeen settings and is detectably negative under none.
Two of the five, $\lambda=0.25$ and $\lambda=1.0$, give the same default in every replicate;
the limit seldom binds at either value. The largest value again arises where
households borrow beyond the shortfall with the shortfall cap removed.

\subsection{Answer to RQ2}
\label{sec:results-answer}

When platforms cannot observe one another, households come to owe several platforms at
once, and the share that does so rises with platform count and with peer influence. With
four platforms, 52.0\% of final-tick holders owe two or more at $\beta=0$ and 85.1\% at
$\beta=1$. Enabling \ac{BNPL} raises default by $0.12 \pm 0.05$ points without peer
influence and by $0.61 \pm 0.11$ at $\beta=1$. Default does not respond detectably to
platform count. It rises with peer influence and, where peer influence is present, with
access, and these are the arms in which households borrow most.
