\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section answers RQ2: whether cross-platform stacking emerges when platforms cannot
observe one another, and how platform count, access and peer adoption affect default. RQ3
follows in Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}. Every arm has twenty replications at the income-shock
probability and payment friction fitted in Section~\ref{sec:calibration}; no \ac{BNPL}-side
parameter is re-tuned. Differences in the text carry one standard error, paired by seed where
the two arms share seeds, and we describe a difference as detectable only when it exceeds two.
A standard error is about 0.1 percentage points, so a difference must exceed about 0.2 points
to count as detectable, and a true change of that size would be missed about half the time.
Figure error bars are 95\% confidence intervals of the
arm mean. Table notes give denominators, seeds and measurement periods.

We report peer influence at $\beta=0$, the control, and at $\beta=1$. Because $\beta$ has no
empirical estimate, $\beta=1$ is an illustrative setting.

\subsection{Baseline and the BNPL Injection}
\label{sec:results-injection}

With \ac{BNPL} disabled, 13.31\% of households have defaulted by the final tick, and 14.20\%
of credit-active households are 90 or more days in arrears, against 14.21\% in the \ac{CCMR}
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-arrears}). The calibration run of
Table~\ref{tab:arrears-fit} gives 14.48\% on other seeds; the two differ by less than one
replicate standard deviation.

Without peer influence, enabling \ac{BNPL} has a small effect on default. At four platforms and
access at the banked ceiling the change is $+0.03 \pm 0.09$ points
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}). Three other seed blocks run the same comparison and give
$+0.15 \pm 0.07$ (Table~\ref{tab:effect}), $+0.14 \pm 0.11$ and $+0.17 \pm 0.10$
(Table~\ref{tab:access}). Of the four estimates, only the one in Table~\ref{tab:effect} exceeds
two standard errors. The blocks use different seeds, so we pool them, weighting each estimate
by its precision, and obtain $+0.12 \pm 0.05$ points. The pooled estimate exceeds two standard
errors, but at about a tenth of a point the effect is smaller than any single comparison in
this thesis can detect.

With peer influence the effect is larger. At $\beta=1$ default rises by $0.61 \pm 0.11$
points, and the two other seed blocks that include this comparison give $0.60 \pm 0.11$ and
$0.60 \pm 0.12$.

The $\beta=1$ arm stacks far more than the only external evidence records. At $\beta=1$,
98.6\% of households that ever held a \ac{BNPL} balance held balances on two or more platforms
at some point, against 36.8\% at $\beta=0$ and the 32\% and 33\% of United States \ac{BNPL}
borrowers who held simultaneous loans at two or more firms during 2021 and 2022
\cite{cfpb2025bnpl}. We therefore treat the $\beta=0$ arm as the better guide to magnitudes,
and the $\beta=1$ arm as an illustration of a market with heavy repeat borrowing.

Peer influence changes how much households borrow far more than how many ever borrow. The
share of households that ever hold a \ac{BNPL} balance is 70.0\% at $\beta=0$ and 75.5\% at
$\beta=1$, while cumulative volume is R6.42 million and R65.88 million. The share of
households with no liquid savings rises by $0.15 \pm 0.06$ points at $\beta=0$ and by
$3.05 \pm 0.06$ at $\beta=1$. Default and depleted savings therefore rise together where
borrowing is heavy. These are population aggregates, so they do not show that the households
which default are those whose savings were depleted.

At $\beta=0$ \ac{BNPL} partly replaces traditional credit. New traditional lending falls from
R6.12 million to R5.43 million, which is consistent with the lender-choice rule sending an
eligible shortfall to \ac{BNPL} first (Submodel~5). At $\beta=1$ new traditional lending does
not fall. The traditional lender refuses about as many applications as before: the change at
$\beta=0$ is $+1 \pm 9$ applications on these seeds and $-16 \pm 7$ on the seeds of
Table~\ref{tab:effect}. The smaller traditional book also accounts for the Pattern~1 result of
Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}. Traditional arrears rise, but the interest charged on
traditional debt falls by $1.5 \pm 0.4$\% at $\beta=0$ (Table~\ref{tab:bnpl-on-checks}),
because interest is charged on a smaller outstanding balance. This aggregate measure is not
directly comparable with the higher credit-card interest that deHaan et al.\ observe among
individual adopters \cite{dehaan2024bnpl}.

Used only to cover shortfalls, \ac{BNPL} does not change default detectably. With the
want-driven path switched off ($q_{\text{base}} = 0$, $\beta = 0$), enabling \ac{BNPL}
changes default by $+0.05 \pm 0.09$ points (Table~\ref{tab:effect}). A household that finances part of a
shortfall with \ac{BNPL} asks the traditional lender for the rest. In this arm the lender
refuses $17 \pm 8$ fewer applications than without \ac{BNPL} and lends R0.83 million less.

\subsection{Invisible Credit and Stacking}
\label{sec:results-stacking}

Households come to owe several platforms at once (Table~\ref{tab:stacking};
Figure~\ref{fig:stacking}). At $\beta=0$, the share of all households holding balances on two
or more platforms at the final tick rises from 8.5\% with two platforms to 9.4\% with six; at
$\beta=1$ it rises from 46.8\% to 59.2\%. With four platforms, 52.0\% of final-tick holders
have balances on two or more platforms at $\beta=0$, and 85.1\% at $\beta=1$
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}). These shares follow from the routing rule of
Section~\ref{sec:stacking}, under which a second request reaches a different platform first
with probability $3/4$ when four platforms are available and $5/6$ when six are. The shares
therefore measure how often agreements overlap.

Cumulative \ac{BNPL} volume rises when a second platform is added, from R5.14 million to
R6.16 million at $\beta=0$, and changes little from two platforms to six. This is consistent
with a second platform relieving limits that bind with one, and with further platforms adding
capacity that budget-based demand does not use.

Default does not change detectably with platform count at any value of $\beta$. Six platforms
less one gives $+0.02 \pm 0.09$ points at $\beta=0$ and $+0.01 \pm 0.08$ at $\beta=1$, and
between $-0.09 \pm 0.07$ and $+0.14 \pm 0.13$ at the other three values
(Table~\ref{tab:stacking-full}). No contrast between one platform and a larger number exceeds
two standard errors.

Two features of the design limit what the sweep shows. Volume changes little beyond two
platforms, so the sweep cannot show what additional platforms would do in a market where they
expand borrowing. Each platform also brings its own limit, so platform count and total credit
capacity move together and the sweep does not isolate invisibility.

\subsection{BNPL Access, Peer Influence and Default}
\label{sec:results-rq2}

Default rises with access where peer influence is present (Figure~\ref{fig:access-default};
Table~\ref{tab:access}). At $\beta=0$, moving from zero to full access changes default by
$+0.14 \pm 0.11$ points, which is not detectable. The change is $0.27 \pm 0.11$ points at
$\beta=0.5$, $0.60 \pm 0.11$ at $\beta=1$, $0.78 \pm 0.09$ at $\beta=2$ and $0.95 \pm 0.09$
at $\beta=3$.

The experiment cannot establish the shape of this response. Steps between adjacent access
levels range from $-0.09$ to $+0.33$ points with standard errors of 0.07 to 0.14, and six of
the thirty exceed two standard errors. Straight-line fits to the arm means have $R^2$ between
0.68 and 0.94. The largest step is $0.33 \pm 0.09$ points, between access of 0.85 and full
access at $\beta=3$. The grid therefore cannot distinguish a straight line from a curved
response.

The heterogeneous-threshold rule raises holding far more than the control does, but it does
not raise default detectably more. At
$\gamma=0.3$ the full-access share of households holding \ac{BNPL} rises by between 40.7 and
51.0 points, against 17.6 in the $\gamma=0$ control. Holding is close to linear in access
($R^2 \geq 0.998$), so no adoption cascade occurs on this grid. Default rises by between
$0.22 \pm 0.09$ and $0.30 \pm 0.10$ points, against $0.17 \pm 0.10$ in the control. The rise
under the rule exceeds the rise in the control by between $0.05 \pm 0.08$ and
$0.14 \pm 0.13$ points, and none of these differences is detectable. Raising the mean
threshold from 0.15 to 0.45 lowers full-access holding from 50.8\% to 28.2\% and changes
default by $-0.17 \pm 0.11$ points, which is not detectable.

\label{sec:no-cascade}
Two limits apply to the search for a tipping point. The grid has seven access levels, so a
threshold narrower than their spacing could go undetected. No channel in the model carries one
household's default to another household's income or to a lender's supply of credit
(Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}), so contagion through those channels lies outside the test.

\subsection{Distribution Across Income Quintiles}
\label{sec:results-distribution}

Baseline default is highest in Q1 (17.83\%) and Q4 (15.75\%) and lowest in Q5 (10.10\%)
(Table~\ref{tab:distribution}). The same two quintiles have the highest aggregate
debt-to-income ratios: 0.77 months of income in Q1 and 0.69 in Q4.

At $\beta=0$ enabling \ac{BNPL} changes default detectably in no quintile. At $\beta=1$ default
rises by $0.56 \pm 0.25$ points in Q2, $0.83 \pm 0.27$ in Q3 and $1.17 \pm 0.32$ in Q4, while
the changes in Q1 ($+0.03 \pm 0.22$) and Q5 ($+0.49 \pm 0.27$) are not detectable. The rise in
Q4 exceeds the rise in Q1 by $1.14 \pm 0.38$ points. In the $\beta=0$ arm the share holding
\ac{BNPL} at the final tick varies little across quintiles, from 15.6\% to 19.4\%, while the
mean want-driven purchase rises from R103 in Q1 to R1{,}858 in Q5, as the budget-based sizing
rule implies. Larger purchases may explain why default rises more in Q4 than in Q1, but the
experiment does not test this, and the rise in Q5 is not detectable.

The per-platform limit binds on 24.8\% of the platform requests that Q1 households make and on
13.6\% of those that Q5 households make, and it truncates or refuses them. Raising $\lambda$
tenfold, from 0.10 to 1.0 months of income, changes population default by $+0.10 \pm 0.09$
points at $\beta=1$ (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}). Binding limits constrain low-income
households' borrowing without a detectable effect on population default.

\subsection{Sensitivity and Robustness}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

Figure~\ref{fig:tornado} and Tables~\ref{tab:robustness} and~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}
report the level of default in one-at-a-time sensitivity arms at $\beta=1$, four platforms and
full access. Each arm is compared with a reference arm at the reference value on the same seed
set.

The largest influence on the default level is the rule for how much a household borrows when
short of cash (Submodel~4). Relative to borrowing the exact shortfall, adding 25\% lowers
default by $0.28 \pm 0.11$ points, and adding one tick of committed expenditure lowers it by
$1.02 \pm 0.13$. A plausible reason is that borrowing beyond the immediate shortfall leaves a surplus
in savings that absorbs later shortfalls, but the experiment does not isolate this channel.
No evidence places the true rule within the range tested (Section~\ref{sec:amount}).
Removing the shortfall cap, which limits \ac{BNPL}'s share of a shortfall, does not change
default detectably under any of the three rules.

Three other assumptions move the default level. Shortening the default horizon from 98 to 56
days raises default by $0.84 \pm 0.11$ points. Varying $\kappa$ from 0.07 to 0.28, about half
to twice its derived value, moves default from 13.69\% to 14.34\%. Sizing purchases on income
in place of discretionary spending adds $0.68 \pm 0.11$ points. The remaining arms change
default by at most 0.35 points and none exceeds two standard errors
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}). Activation order and population size test the
simulation and do not detectably change default.

Shock persistence matters more than any of these. Replacing the persistent income shock with a
single-tick loss lowers default from 14.05\% to 4.81\%, and under that rule no shock
probability in the tested range reproduces the \ac{CCMR} 90+ band. Sustained distress in the
model depends on shocks that outlast the household's buffer.

A Sobol decomposition over six parameters (Table~\ref{tab:sobol}) attributes the variance of
the default level almost entirely to the income-shock probability, with a first-order index of
0.99 and a 95\% bootstrap interval of $\pm 0.15$. This reflects its range, 0.005--0.026,
which runs from below the \ac{QLFS} separation band to more than twice its upper end. Each
\ac{BNPL}-side parameter has a total-order index of about 0.01.

\subsubsection*{Robustness of the BNPL Effect}
\label{sec:results-effect}

The arms above measure the level of default with \ac{BNPL} enabled. Table~\ref{tab:effect}
tests the \ac{BNPL} effect itself. Every setting is run with \ac{BNPL} off and on, at
$\beta=0$ and $\beta=1$, over one shared seed block, so each effect is a paired difference on
the same seeds.

At $\beta=1$ the effect is positive and detectable under sixteen of the seventeen settings.
Among those sixteen it ranges from $0.25 \pm 0.09$ points
at $\kappa=0.07$ to $1.27 \pm 0.11$ where households borrow one tick of committed expenditure
beyond the shortfall and the shortfall cap is removed. Purchase size and the borrowing-amount
rule move it most. The effect is $0.91 \pm 0.07$ points at $\kappa=0.28$ and
$1.22 \pm 0.10$ when purchases are sized on income. Under the single-tick shock the effect is
$0.02 \pm 0.01$ points, with default without \ac{BNPL} at 4.82\%.

At $\beta=0$ the effect ranges from $-0.11 \pm 0.08$ to $+0.52 \pm 0.09$ points. It exceeds two
standard errors under five of the seventeen settings and is detectably negative under none.
Two of the five, $\lambda=0.25$ and $\lambda=1.0$, give the same default in every replicate;
the limit seldom binds at either value. The largest value again arises where
households borrow beyond the shortfall with the shortfall cap removed.

\subsection{Answer to RQ2}
\label{sec:results-answer}

Households come to owe several platforms at once when platforms cannot observe one another,
and the share that does so rises with platform count and with peer influence. The routing
rule produces these shares, so they say nothing about how households choose providers.
Default does not respond detectably to platform count. It rises with peer influence and,
where peer influence is present, with access, and these are the arms in which households
borrow most.
