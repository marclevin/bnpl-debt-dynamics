\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section answers RQ2. Every arm uses the parameters set in
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}; no \ac{BNPL} parameter is re-tuned.
Because $\beta$ has no empirical estimate, we report $\beta=0$, the control without peer
influence, and $\beta=0.3$, the largest value swept (Section~\ref{sec:results-beta}). Every
result holds for the two-year horizon only.

\subsection{Baseline and the BNPL Injection}
\label{sec:results-injection}

Without \ac{BNPL}, default during the run is 14.37\% (20.73\% including the starting stock).
Enabling \ac{BNPL} raises it by $1.08 \pm 0.11$ points at $\beta=0$ and $1.12 \pm 0.11$ at
$\beta=0.3$ (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}). Weighted by inverse variance over four seed blocks
(Tables~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}, \ref{tab:access} and~\ref{tab:effect} and the threshold rule's
control), whose estimates run from 0.94 to 1.08 points, the effect is $1.03 \pm 0.06$ points at
$\beta=0$; over three blocks at $\beta=0.3$, from 1.07 to 1.12, it is $1.10 \pm 0.07$.

% results/summary/results_numbers.json (blocks: Tables tab:baseline-arms, tab:access, the threshold
% control and tab:effect).

The effect runs through households that use \ac{BNPL} to bridge cash shortfalls and then fall
more than 90 days behind on it. On the seeds of Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms}, $0.94 \pm 0.01$ of the 1.08
points are households in default on \ac{BNPL} alone. The remaining $0.14 \pm 0.11$, defaults that
involve traditional arrears, is not detectable.
With \ac{BNPL} kept out of the shortfall path the effect is $-0.05 \pm 0.16$ points, and with
only the shortfall path it is $+0.93 \pm 0.12$ (Table~\ref{tab:effect}).
% 0.94 +/- 0.01 and 0.14 +/- 0.11: bnpl_only_pp and remainder_pp (+ _se) in
% results/summary/default_trigger.json (notebooks/scripts/check_default_trigger.py, seeds
% 10,000-10,019, beta = 0). BNPL alone: at the tick of default the oldest unpaid BNPL instalment is
% at the threshold and traditional arrears are not. A further 0.31 points are past it on both.

Most of these households belong to the 3.6\% whose surveyed income does not cover their food
and rent (Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}). Of the 120 of them not in default at the start, the
traditional lender's Regulation 23A test never grants a loan to 55 and lends to 65. Without
\ac{BNPL} none of the 55 defaults, because they hold no debt; they go short of food and rent
instead. With \ac{BNPL} the platforms lend to them and 46 of the 55 default, while 57 of the 65
default in both arms. The 55 therefore account for 0.92 of the 1.08 points. This part of the
effect follows from the rules: a household with no debt cannot default, and the platforms'
screen does not compare income with living costs. \ac{BNPL} is tried before the traditional
lender (Submodel~5), so fewer requests reach it and its refusals fall from 2{,}796 to 970 over
the run, of which the group makes 2{,}623 and 817. The effect therefore depends on counting
\ac{BNPL} arrears as default. Under the earlier rule, which did not and also differed in its
starting profile and write-off, it was $-0.10 \pm 0.06$ points (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}).

The 90+ share of credit-active households falls with \ac{BNPL}, from 13.98\% to 12.46\% at
$\beta=0$, because \ac{BNPL} holders join the denominator. We therefore use it for the level
against the \ac{CCMR} and not for effects. On the 250 seeds of Section~\ref{sec:results-beta},
the share of all households more than 90 days in arrears, averaged after burn-in, rises by
$0.23 \pm 0.01$ points at $\beta=0$, and the share ever short of food and rent by
$0.06 \pm 0.01$. On the seeds of Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms} the share with no liquid savings
rises by $0.17 \pm 0.04$ points. New traditional lending does not change detectably at either
value of $\beta$ ($-$R$0.10 \pm 0.25$ million and $+$R$0.15 \pm 0.25$ million).

\subsection{Peer Influence}
\label{sec:results-beta}

We swept $\beta$ from 0 to 0.3 at eight values with 250 replicates each, under a reporting rule
fixed before the runs. The effect counts as non-linear only if a quadratic term, or a hinge at
$\beta=0.1$, is more than two standard errors from zero and changes a conclusion at some value of
$\beta$; otherwise we report the effect at $\beta=0$ and a slope.

Neither term is two standard errors from zero, so we report the effect as linear
(Table~\ref{tab:beta}). At $\beta=0$ it is $+1.05 \pm 0.03$ points, the effect of
Section~\ref{sec:results-injection} on 250 other seeds. It changes by $+0.003 \pm 0.010$ points per 0.1 of $\beta$. Peer influence raises the
probability of a want-driven purchase, so from $\beta=0$ to $\beta=0.3$ \ac{BNPL} volume per
eligible household rises from R862 to R2{,}014 a year, and the share of ever-holders who stack
from 28\% to 70\%. It acts on no other decision, and the effect runs mainly through the shortfall path,
so little slope is expected. The sweep finds no detectable slope, and it would detect a
difference of 0.07 points between two values of $\beta$ about half the time.

\subsection{Stacking Across Platforms}
\label{sec:results-stacking}

With four platforms and no peer influence, 22\% of the households holding \ac{BNPL} at the
final tick owe two or more platforms (Table~\ref{tab:stacking}; Figure~\ref{fig:stacking}).
Over the two years 28\% of ever-holders do so at some tick, against the \ac{CFPB}'s 32 to 33\%
of borrowers in one year~\citep{cfpb2025bnpl}.

That share follows from the model's rules (Section~\ref{sec:stacking}). An agreement paid on
schedule stays open for three ticks after checkout, and one with an unpaid instalment stays open
until it is paid or written off, so under random routing a household that borrows often soon
owes two platforms. Holding and stacking also count the frozen balances of defaulted households
not yet written off. Under loyal routing the share of ever-holders who stack falls from 70\% to
20\% at $\beta=0.3$, and from 28\% to 13\% at $\beta=0$, on the same seeds and with about the
same volume. Stacking is therefore set by the routing assumption and by how often households
borrow, and the model has no South African evidence on either.
% stack_ever_random_beta*, stack_ever_loyal_beta*, volume_*_beta*, results_numbers.json
% (seeds 70,000-70,019).

Default does not change detectably with the routing rule ($-0.07 \pm 0.12$ points at $\beta=0$
and $+0.01 \pm 0.13$ at $\beta=0.3$), and it shows no systematic rise with the number of
platforms (Table~\ref{tab:stacking-full}). Of the 20 contrasts of two to six platforms against
one across the four values of $\beta$, two exceed two standard errors, two platforms at
$\beta=0$ ($+0.22 \pm 0.10$ points) and five at $\beta=0.3$ ($+0.22 \pm 0.11$). That is about
what chance gives, and the contrasts do not rise with the number of platforms. Each platform brings its own
limit, so platform count and total credit capacity move together, and the sweep cannot isolate
the effect of platforms not seeing one another. No cap on platforms owed changes default
detectably (Table~\ref{tab:cap}; Appendix~\ref{app:levers}).

\subsection{BNPL Access, Peer Influence and Default}
\label{sec:results-rq2}

Table~\ref{tab:access} varies the share of banked households allowed to use \ac{BNPL}. Moving
from zero to full access raises holding by 10 to 21 points and default by 0.99 to 1.25 points
under the linear rule, detectably at every value of $\beta$. No household can use \ac{BNPL} at
zero access, so each row is also an estimate of the \ac{BNPL} effect, though not an independent
one, since the rows share that arm.

\label{sec:no-cascade}
Straight lines through the seven arm means of default have $R^2$ of 0.94 to 0.98 at every value
of $\beta$ (Figure~\ref{fig:access-default}). Of the 24 steps between neighbouring access
levels, only one departs by more than two standard errors from the step a straight line through
the two ends implies. At $\beta=0.3$ most of the rise comes
between 30\% and 70\% access, $0.73 \pm 0.12$ points, with $0.09 \pm 0.14$ above 70\%. The data
show no jump, but seven widely spaced levels cannot exclude a gradual S-shape. No channel in the model carries one
household's default to another's income or to a lender's supply of credit
(Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}), so contagion through those channels lies outside the test.
% access_r2_beta*, access_step_dev_beyond_2se_all (6 steps x 4 betas), access_rise_30_70_beta0.3 and
% access_rise_70_100_beta0.3 (+ _se), results_numbers.json.

The threshold rule tests whether default depends on how adoption spreads. Under it, with
$\gamma=0.3$, moving from zero to full access raises holding by 14 to 34 points, against 10 at
$\gamma=0$, and default by 0.89 to 1.09 points, within 0.15 of the 0.94 at $\gamma=0$. Volume is 1.3 to 4.4
times the control's. Raising the mean threshold from 0.15 to 0.45 lowers full-access holding
from 41\% to 13\% without changing default detectably ($-0.18 \pm 0.14$ points).

\subsection{Distribution Across Income Quintiles}
\label{sec:results-distribution}

Baseline default is highest in Q1, at 26.03\%, and falls with income to 16.85\% in Q5
(Table~\ref{tab:distribution}). The \ac{BNPL} effect is concentrated in Q1, at $+4.12 \pm 0.24$
points at $\beta=0$ and $+4.21 \pm 0.16$ at $\beta=0.3$, and is detectable in no other quintile.
Two-thirds of the households whose surveyed income does not cover food and rent are in Q1
(Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}).
% Two-thirds: 121 of the 182 agents with w5_hhincome < expenditure_committed are Q1
% (data/processed/synthetic_population_5000.parquet).

Limits bind more often on poorer households. A limit
reduces or refuses 21\% of platform requests in Q1 and 20\% in Q2, against 13\% in Q5. Raising
the limit tenfold lowers population default by $0.28 \pm 0.12$ points
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}).

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

The replicate standard deviation of default is
about 0.3 to 0.6 points with 5{,}000 households, and results do not change detectably with population
size or activation order (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}).

The level of default rests mainly on the definition of default (Figure~\ref{fig:tornado};
Tables~\ref{tab:robustness} and~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}). The households placed in default
at the start account for 6.36 of the 20.73 points by construction. Defining default at 56 days in
place of 98 raises it by $13.13 \pm 0.13$ points. That arm changes the run as well as the count,
because default freezes a household's accounts, stops its credit and opens its debt to write-off
six weeks sooner. Of the rise, 1.81 points come from households placed
in default at the start and $11.32 \pm 0.13$ from default during the run. The variance
decomposition is of the level of default, not of the \ac{BNPL} effect. Of its six parameters,
only the separation rate accounts for a share of the variance distinguishable from zero. Its
first-order index is 0.84, with a 95\% bootstrap interval of $\pm 0.15$, and default during the
run is 14.9\% in the lowest fifth of its swept range and 15.9\% in the highest. The other
five have total-order indices of 0.12 to 0.14, about the 14\% of the variance across design
points that is replicate noise; with the noise removed, each is within 0.03 of zero (Table~\ref{tab:sobol}). Replacing persistent job loss with a
single-tick loss, an arm that also has no hires and exposes only households living mainly on
wages, lowers default by $0.86 \pm 0.15$ points. With the \ac{BNPL} share of a shortfall capped,
borrowing a quarter more than the shortfall, or a tick of committed spending on top of it,
lowers default by $0.51 \pm 0.14$ and $0.39 \pm 0.15$ points. Purchase size, the minimum payment
and the minimum-payer share do not change default detectably.

\subsubsection*{What Moves the BNPL Effect}
\label{sec:results-effect}

Table~\ref{tab:effect} runs every setting with \ac{BNPL} off and on, on one seed block. The
effect is detectable under every setting but one, at both values of $\beta$. Under the
others it lies between 0.79 and 1.18 points, against a reference effect of $+0.98 \pm 0.13$ at
$\beta=0$ and $+1.07 \pm 0.15$ at $\beta=0.3$. The settings share the block and often a
no-\ac{BNPL} arm, so the detectable settings are not independent replications.

Halving or doubling $\kappa$, which halves or doubles volume, leaves the effect between 0.89 and

The one setting with no detectable effect keeps \ac{BNPL} out of the shortfall path

(Section~\ref{sec:amount}), and no study measures either for South Africa.

\subsection{Answer to RQ2}
\label{sec:results-answer}

Under the assumed random routing, 28\% of ever-holders owe two or more platforms at some
tick. Enabling \ac{BNPL} raises
default by $1.03 \pm 0.06$ points without peer influence, and it also raises the share ever
short of food and rent ($+0.06 \pm 0.01$ points) and the share with no liquid savings
($+0.17 \pm 0.04$). The effect on default runs through \ac{BNPL} used against cash shortfalls and
is concentrated in the 3.6\% of households whose surveyed income does not cover their food and
rent. Among the others it is $+0.11 \pm 0.07$ points at $\beta=0$, not detectable, and
$+0.18 \pm 0.08$ at $\beta=0.3$, which is. Peer influence acts only on want-driven purchases. It
raises volume and stacking, and the effect shows no detectable slope in $\beta$
($+0.003 \pm 0.010$ points per 0.1) and stays within 0.15 points of the control under the
threshold rule. Default rises with access, with no sign of a jump. It does not change detectably with the routing rule
and shows no systematic rise with the number of platforms.

% +0.06 +/- 0.01 and +0.17 +/- 0.04: as in Section 4.1 (effect_consumption_ever_pp, beta_sweep.json;

The size of the effect depends most on whether households can borrow with \ac{BNPL} against a
shortfall (Section~\ref{sec:results-effect}) and on counting \ac{BNPL} arrears as default
(Section~\ref{sec:results-injection}). No arm gives platforms a shared view of exposure, so the
model cannot estimate what their blindness to one another costs; the nearest comparison,
mandatory screening, is in Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}.
