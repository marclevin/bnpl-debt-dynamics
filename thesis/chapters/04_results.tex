\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section answers RQ2. Every arm uses the parameters set in
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}; no \ac{BNPL} parameter is re-tuned. The conventions for
replicates, standard errors and detectability are those of Section~\ref{sec:sim-params}.
Because $\beta$ has no empirical estimate, we report $\beta=0$, the control without peer
influence, and $\beta=0.3$, the largest value swept (Section~\ref{sec:results-beta}).

\subsection{Baseline and the BNPL Injection}
\label{sec:results-injection}

Table~\ref{tab:baseline-arms} compares the baseline with \ac{BNPL} enabled. Enabling \ac{BNPL}
raises default during the run from 14.37\% (20.73\% with the households placed in default at
the start) by $1.08 \pm 0.11$ points at $\beta=0$ and $1.12 \pm 0.11$ at
$\beta=0.3$. Pooled by precision over four seed blocks, whose estimates run from 0.94 to 1.08
points, the effect is $1.03 \pm 0.06$ points at $\beta=0$; over three blocks at $\beta=0.3$ it
is $1.10 \pm 0.07$. The households placed in default at the start are the same in both arms,
so the effect is on defaults during the run.
% Pooled effects: beta0_effect_pooled, beta0_effect_min/_max and peer_effect_pooled (+ _se),
% results/summary/results_numbers.json (blocks: Tables tab:baseline-arms, tab:access, the threshold
% control and tab:effect).

The effect runs through households that use \ac{BNPL} to bridge cash shortfalls, substituting it
for purchases they would otherwise pay in cash, and then fall 91 or more days behind on it. Of
the rise at $\beta=0$, 0.94 points are households whose oldest unpaid \ac{BNPL} instalment
reached 98 days and 0.13 points come through traditional arrears. With \ac{BNPL} kept out of the
shortfall path the effect is $-0.05 \pm 0.16$ points, and with only the shortfall path it is
$+0.93 \pm 0.12$ (Table~\ref{tab:effect}). Most of these households are the 3.6\% whose surveyed
income does not cover their food and rent (Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}). \ac{BNPL} relieves
a shortfall before the traditional lender is asked (Submodel~5), and the platforms' screen is
lighter than the Regulation 23A test, so the lender's refusals fall from 2{,}796 to 970 over the
run.
% 0.94 and 0.13: decomposition of new defaults, 10 seeds at beta = 0 (docs/ARREARS_RULE_2026-11.md).

Two measures move differently. The 90+ share of credit-active households falls with \ac{BNPL},
from 13.98\% to 12.46\% at $\beta=0$, because \ac{BNPL} holders join the denominator, so we use it
for the level against the \ac{CCMR} and not for effects. Over all households the 90+ share rises
by $0.33 \pm 0.05$ points at $\beta=0$. The share of households ever short of food and rent rises
by $0.13 \pm 0.06$ points, and the share with no liquid savings by $0.17 \pm 0.04$.
% 0.33 +/- 0.05 and 0.13 +/- 0.06: docs/ARREARS_RULE_2026-11.md (rq0, seeds 10,000-10,019);
% zero_savings_change_beta0 (+ _se), results_numbers.json.

At $\beta=0.3$ volume is 2.4 times that at $\beta=0$, and a household that ever holds a balance
makes 6.1 want-driven purchases over the two years against 2.5, yet the effect on default is
about the same. New traditional lending does not change detectably at either value of $\beta$
($-$R$0.10 \pm 0.25$ million and $+$R$0.15 \pm 0.25$ million).

\subsection{Peer Influence}
\label{sec:results-beta}

We swept $\beta$ from 0 to 0.3 at eight values with 250 replicates each and fixed the reporting
rule before the runs. The effect would count as non-linear only if a quadratic or a hinge at
$\beta=0.1$ had an extra term more than two standard errors from zero and changed a conclusion at
some value of $\beta$; otherwise we report the effect at $\beta=0$ and a slope.

The effect is linear and flat (Table~\ref{tab:beta}). At $\beta=0$ it is $+1.05 \pm 0.03$ points,
with a replicate standard deviation of 0.53, and it changes by $+0.003 \pm 0.014$ points per 0.1 of
$\beta$. The sweep would detect a difference of about 0.1 points between two values of $\beta$.
Peer influence changes how much households borrow, not how many default: from $\beta=0$ to
$\beta=0.3$ volume per eligible household rises from R862 to R2{,}014 a year and the share of
ever-holders who stack from 28\% to 71\%. Volume enters the provider's range at $\beta=0.2$ and is
still inside it at 0.3, which we report beside the control. The flat effect is consistent with
the shortfall path: $\beta$ acts only on want-driven purchases.

\subsection{Stacking Across Platforms}
\label{sec:results-stacking}

Owing several platforms at once is common among users (Table~\ref{tab:stacking};
Figure~\ref{fig:stacking}). With four platforms and no peer influence, 22\% of the households
holding \ac{BNPL} at the final tick owe two or more, and 28\% of ever-holders do so at some
point, close to the \ac{CFPB}'s 32 to 33\%. At $\beta=0.3$, 70\% of ever-holders do.

That share follows from the model's rules. Each agreement stays open for three ticks, and under
random routing a new request goes first to a platform the household does not owe with
probability $3/4$, so a household that borrows often soon owes two platforms. Under loyal
routing, in which a household tries platforms it already owes first, the share of ever-holders
who stack falls from 70\% to 20\% at $\beta=0.3$, and from 28\% to 13\% at $\beta=0$, on the same
seeds and with about the same volume. How often households stack is therefore set by the routing
assumption and by how often they borrow, and the model has no South African evidence on either.
% stack_ever_random_beta*, stack_ever_loyal_beta*, volume_*_beta*, results_numbers.json
% (seeds 70,000-70,019).

Default does not change detectably with the routing rule ($-0.07 \pm 0.12$ points at $\beta=0$
and $+0.01 \pm 0.13$ at $\beta=0.3$) or with the number of platforms: six platforms against one
change it by $-0.08$ to $+0.17$ points across the four values of $\beta$, none detectable
(Table~\ref{tab:stacking-full}). Each platform brings its own limit, so platform count and total
credit capacity move together, and the sweep cannot isolate the effect of platforms not seeing
one another. No cap on platforms owed changes default detectably (Table~\ref{tab:cap};
Appendix~\ref{app:levers}).

\subsection{BNPL Access, Peer Influence and Default}
\label{sec:results-rq2}

Table~\ref{tab:access} varies the share of banked households allowed to use \ac{BNPL}. Moving
from zero to full access raises holding by 10 to 21 points and default by 0.99 to 1.25 points
under the linear rule, detectably at every value of $\beta$. No household can use \ac{BNPL} at
zero access, so each of these rows is also an estimate of the \ac{BNPL} effect.

\label{sec:no-cascade}
Straight lines through the seven arm means of default have $R^2$ of 0.94 to 0.98 at every value
of $\beta$ (Figure~\ref{fig:access-default}). That measures fit to a line, not proportionality.
The largest rise between neighbouring access levels is $0.40 \pm 0.13$ points, and no step is
followed by a level, so nothing in these data suggests a tipping point. The design could still
miss one, because the seven access levels are widely spaced. No channel in the model carries one
household's default to another's income or to a lender's supply of credit
(Table~\ref{tab:design-concepts}), so contagion through those channels lies outside the test.
% access_r2_beta*, access_max_step_all (beta = 0.3, 30% to 50% access), results_numbers.json.

The threshold rule tests whether default depends on how adoption spreads. With $\gamma=0.3$ it
raises holding at full access by 14 to 34 points, against 10 at $\gamma=0$, and default by 0.89
to 1.09 points, against 0.94 in the control. Raising the mean threshold from 0.15 to 0.45 lowers
full-access holding from 41\% to 13\% without changing default detectably
($-0.18 \pm 0.14$ points). Volume under the threshold rule is 1.3 to 4.4 times the control's, so
peer influence, under either rule, makes the same households buy more often without moving the
effect on default.

\subsection{Distribution Across Income Quintiles}
\label{sec:results-distribution}

Baseline default is highest in Q1, at 26.03\%, and falls with income to 16.85\% in Q5
(Table~\ref{tab:distribution}). The \ac{BNPL} effect is concentrated in Q1, at $+4.12 \pm 0.24$
points at $\beta=0$ and $+4.21 \pm 0.16$ at $\beta=0.3$, and is detectable in no other quintile.
Two-thirds of the households whose surveyed income does not cover food and rent are in Q1, and
these carry most of the effect (Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}). Purchases are sized on
discretionary budgets, so the mean purchase rises from R108 in Q1 to R1{,}911 in Q5, and Q5,
with the largest purchases, shows no detectable rise.
% Two-thirds: 121 of the 182 agents with w5_hhincome < expenditure_committed are Q1
% (data/processed/synthetic_population_5000.parquet).

Platform limits bind more often on poorer households: a limit reduces or refuses a fifth of
platform requests in Q1 and Q2, against 13\% in Q5. Raising the limit tenfold lowers population
default by $0.28 \pm 0.12$ points (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}).

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

Simulation noise is small beside the assumptions. Default varies by about 0.3 to 0.5 points
between replicates, and results do not change detectably with population size or activation
order (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}). Every result belongs to the two-year horizon.

The level of default is set mainly by the definition of default and the starting profile
(Figure~\ref{fig:tornado}; Tables~\ref{tab:robustness} and~\ref{tab:robustness-appendix}). The
households placed in default at the start are 6.36 of the 20.73 points. Defining default at 56
days in place of 98 raises it by $13.13 \pm 0.13$ points; this changes the outcome measured, not
household behaviour. Of the six parameters of the variance decomposition, only the separation
rate has a first-order index clearly above zero: default rises from 21.3\% in the lowest fifth of
its swept range to 22.3\% in the highest. With two replicates per design point the other indices
are dominated by replicate noise, so Table~\ref{tab:sobol} gives a ranking only. Replacing
persistent job loss with a single-tick loss, an arm that also has no hires, lowers default by
$0.86 \pm 0.15$ points. Borrowing a quarter more than the shortfall, or a tick of committed
spending on top of it, lowers default by $0.51 \pm 0.14$ and $0.39 \pm 0.15$ points while the
\ac{BNPL} share of a shortfall is capped. Purchase size, the minimum payment and the
minimum-payer share do not change default detectably.

\subsubsection*{What Moves the BNPL Effect}
\label{sec:results-effect}

Table~\ref{tab:effect} runs every setting with \ac{BNPL} off and on, on one shared seed block.
The effect is detectable under every setting but one, at both values of $\beta$, and lies between
0.79 and 1.18 points. The reference effect on this block is $+0.98 \pm 0.13$ points at $\beta=0$
and $+1.07 \pm 0.15$ at $\beta=0.3$.

Purchase size barely moves the effect. Halving $\kappa$ gives $+0.89 \pm 0.12$ points at
$\beta=0$ and doubling it $+1.01 \pm 0.13$, while volume halves and doubles. The two settings
whose mean purchase is closest to the provider-based figure, $\kappa=0.28$ and income-based
sizing, give $+1.01 \pm 0.13$ and $+1.13 \pm 0.13$. The borrowing-amount rules, the
minimum-payment rules, the platform limit, loyal routing, the single-tick income shock and
default at 56 days all leave the effect between 0.79 and 1.18 points.

The one setting that removes the effect keeps \ac{BNPL} out of the shortfall path:
$-0.05 \pm 0.16$ points at $\beta=0$ and $+0.11 \pm 0.13$ at $\beta=0.3$. Two assumptions shape
that path (Section~\ref{sec:amount}). An agreement frees three quarters of its value in cash, as
though the household financed a purchase it would otherwise have paid for in cash, and the
\ac{BNPL} share of a shortfall is capped at the mean purchase. No study measures either for South
Africa. The settings share a seed block and often a no-\ac{BNPL} arm, so these counts are not
independent replicates.

\subsection{Answer to RQ2}
\label{sec:results-answer}

Owing several platforms at once is common among users, at a rate set mostly by the assumed
routing rule and by how often households borrow. Enabling \ac{BNPL} raises default by
$1.03 \pm 0.06$ points without peer influence, and the effect does not change with $\beta$
between 0 and 0.3 ($+0.003 \pm 0.014$ points per 0.1). It runs through households using
\ac{BNPL} to bridge cash shortfalls and then falling 91 or more days behind on it, most of them
the 3.6\% whose surveyed income does not cover their food and rent. Among the other households
the effect is $+0.11 \pm 0.07$ points, and it disappears when \ac{BNPL} cannot be used against a
shortfall. Default rises with access under both adoption rules and does not change detectably
with the routing rule or the number of platforms. Peer influence under either rule raises volume
and stacking without changing the effect on default.

The size of the effect depends most on whether and how much households can borrow with \ac{BNPL}
against a shortfall, which is not measured for South Africa. No arm gives platforms a shared view
of exposure; the nearest comparison, mandatory screening, is in Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}.
