\section{Cell-Donor Matching: Procedure, Assumptions and Limits}
\label{app:matching}

Section~\ref{sec:match} states the cell-donor match in four sentences and Figure~\ref{fig:cell-donor}
draws it. This appendix gives the procedure formally, states the assumptions under which it is
credible, the circumstances under which it is not, and which of those would move a headline
result. Notation follows Table~\ref{tab:notation}; the procedure itself is Algorithm~\ref{alg:match}.

\subsection*{A. The procedure}

Both surveys are partitioned by the same two variables, per-capita income quintile $q$ and
province $p$, into $|\mathcal{C}| = 45$ cells $c = (q, p)$. For each \ac{NIDS} record $i$ with
cell $c(i)$, one donor $d$ is drawn from the \ac{FinScope} records in the same cell with
probability $w_d / \sum_{d' \in c(i)} w_{d'}$, where $w$ is the \ac{FinScope} household weight,
and the donor's six-flag vector (banked; store card; revolving credit; hire purchase; short-term
loan; personal loan) is copied to $i$ in its entirety. Draws are with replacement, seeded (42), and
the quintile of a \ac{FinScope} donor is assigned from the midpoint of its seven-band income
question divided by household size, against the weighted \ac{NIDS} quintile bounds. The
procedure is specified to fall back to the quintile-only pool if a cell is empty; no draw ever did.

Three facts about the realised match are worth stating because they are not visible in the
body. The smallest cell holds 30 donors and the median 87; only three cells (all in the top
quintile: Limpopo, North West, Northern Cape) hold fewer than 50. Of 4{,}969 available donors,
3{,}597 were used at least once; the median donor was used twice, the 99th percentile 13 times,
and the most-used donor 20 times. Donor reuse is concentrated where \ac{NIDS} is dense and
\ac{FinScope} is thin: in the largest cell, the bottom quintile of KwaZulu-Natal, 1{,}058
recipients drew from 109 donors, and eleven cells have more than three recipients per donor.
The heaviest one per cent of donors nonetheless supply only 5.1\% of recipients.

\subsection*{B. Assumptions under which the match is credible}

\begin{description}
\item[A1. Cell sufficiency.] Conditional on income quintile and province, the six flags are
independent of everything else in \ac{NIDS} that affects a household's behaviour in the model.
This is the load-bearing assumption and it is untestable from the two surveys, since the flags
are observed in one and the balance sheet in the other. Its defence is that quintile and
province are the two strongest correlates of financial inclusion available in \emph{both}
instruments, and that the \ac{FinScope} flags show large between-cell variation along both
(banked status ranges from below 60\% to above 95\% across cells).
\item[A2. Common support.] Every \ac{NIDS} cell has \ac{FinScope} donors. Verified, not
assumed: at least 30 in each of 45 cells, and zero fallback draws.
\item[A3. Population comparability.] The two instruments sample the same population and define
the products the same way. Both are national household surveys; the \ac{FinScope} product
questions name the same instruments the \ac{CCMR} categorises, which is what lets each flag be
priced from the statutory rate table.
\item[A4. Joint-structure preservation.] Because one donor supplies all six flags, the imputed
joint distribution of products within a cell is the donor population's, not a product of six
independent marginals. This is why single-donor transfer was chosen over per-flag imputation
(Figure~\ref{fig:cell-donor}c), and it matters because concurrent-facility stacking is a
statement about the joint distribution of holdings.
\item[A5. Weight-proportional draw.] Drawing in proportion to \ac{FinScope} weight reproduces
that survey's weighted cell marginals in expectation. Table~\ref{tab:imputation_errors} is the
test of A5 and passes at 1.8 percentage points or better.
\end{description}

\subsection*{C. Circumstances under which the match is problematic}

\begin{description}
\item[P1. Within-cell heterogeneity.] Anything that predicts inclusion within a cell, urban or
rural location above all, and also education and the formality of employment, is not matched
on. The imputation therefore reproduces between-cell variation in inclusion and suppresses
within-cell variation: it is attenuated. The \ac{BNPL}-eligible population is more homogeneous
than the real one, and since the omitted predictors also predict liquidity constraint, the
most likely direction is that exposure is less concentrated in the model than in the population.
\item[P2. Small-cell donor reuse.] Recipients are drawn with replacement from pools that in
three cells are near the 30-donor floor, and in the densest cells outnumber donors nearly ten
to one. A heavily reused donor's flag vector is stamped onto many recipients, which reduces the
effective sample of flag vectors below the nominal 10{,}841. The concentration figures in
Part~A bound the effect: the top one per cent of donors supply 5.1\% of recipients, so no
single donor's idiosyncrasy dominates a cell.
\item[P3. Matching-key measurement error.] \ac{FinScope} income is banded, so a donor's
quintile rests on a band midpoint divided by household size. Misassignment across a quintile
boundary places a donor in the wrong cell and attenuates the match further. The effect is
bounded, not eliminated, by the deflation test of Section~\ref{sec:match}: moving every band
boundary by two years of inflation shifts imputed quintile rates by at most 2.4 percentage
points.
\item[P4. Vintage.] The flags are 2019 observations imposed on a 2017 population. Banked status
was rising over that interval, so the 82.8\% \ac{BNPL}-eligibility ceiling is plausibly
slightly too generous for 2017; the direction is known, the magnitude is not, and the
categorical flags cannot be deflated. This is the one limitation here that acts directly on a
model input rather than on its dispersion.
\item[P5. Group B validation is a construction check.] Because the draw is weight-proportional
within the cell, agreement with the \ac{FinScope} cell marginals is expected by construction.
The seven Group B checks in Table~\ref{tab:validation} therefore verify the implementation and
say nothing about the population's external validity; Section~\ref{sec:data-validation} counts
them accordingly.
\item[P6. The joint distribution is never externally validated.] Only marginals are checked. No
South African source publishes the joint distribution of credit-product holding, so A4 buys
structural plausibility, not verified accuracy: the model's joint distribution of products is as
good as \ac{FinScope}'s, and no better.
\end{description}

\subsection*{D. Which of these would move a result}

Not all of P1--P6 matter equally. P4 acts on a level: it moves the eligibility ceiling and
therefore the adoption rate in every \ac{BNPL}-on arm, though by an amount the access sweep of
Section~\ref{sec:results-rq2} brackets, since that sweep varies eligibility over the whole
banked subpopulation. P1 acts on a distribution: it flattens the concentration of exposure
across households within a quintile, which bears on the by-quintile results but not on the
population aggregates. P2 and P3 are second order, being bounded by the concentration and
deflation figures above. P5 and P6 bound what the validation record can claim rather than what
the model does: they are the reason the external checks of Section~\ref{sec:data-validation}
are reported separately from the construction checks, and they are why the match is described
throughout as reproducing marginals rather than as recovering the population.

The alternative not taken, a model-based or hot-deck fusion recovering finer joint structure
from both instruments, is recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:rationale}. It would relax A1 at the
cost of its own validation burden and the risk of fitting the idiosyncrasies of two surveys
rather than the population behind them; the crude match reproduces cell marginals, which is
what the model consumes.
